# Map Generator

This project is a map generator that creates random planet maps using Perlin noise. The generated maps can be used for various applications such as games, simulations, or any other project that requires a random terrain.

## Prerequisites

[mise](https://mise.jdx.dev/) manages the Rust toolchain (pinned in `mise.toml`) and the project tasks.

```bash
mise install
```

Without mise, any Rust toolchain supporting edition 2024 (1.85+) works with plain `cargo` commands.

## Usage

### Build

```bash
mise run build
```

### Run

```bash
mise run gen [-- [OPTIONS]]
```

All parameters are optional. Any omitted value comes from the planet archetype when it sets one, and is otherwise chosen **randomly** at startup; the planet model derives the rest (its size, air, water, volcanism and look). The options describe a planet: the run draws the planetary system around it (see **systems** below), writes it to `systems/<id>/`, and generates the planet and its neighbourhood (its moons; for a giant's moon, the giant and its other moons). The chosen values are printed, then the bodies of the system, the progress of each body generated, and the exact options needed to regenerate the same planet:

**seed** — drives every noise layer. The same seed with the same other options always produces the same world. Each noise layer hashes the seed with its own stream number, so the layers of a world are independent and neighbouring seeds share nothing. A seed noted before that change (November 2026), or before impact craters were added (the same month), gives a different world now. So does a seed noted before the climate fields were interpolated from a coarser grid (format version 15, also November 2026), though only slightly: the same relief and coasts, and biomes that differ on about 0.1 % of the land; a world already generated keeps its files.

**planet** — an archetype names a mass, a composition and an orbit, each a value or a `[min, max]` range (`archetypes/*.toml`, format in `crates/mapgen/src/archetype.rs`): the eight planets of the Solar System (`mercury-like` … `neptune-like`, their masses, core shares and orbits from `planet::reference`) and five kinds of exoplanet (`super-earth`, `mini-neptune`, `ocean-world`, `lava-world`, `hot-jupiter`). It is not a type: the planet model derives what the planet becomes (its class, interior, air, water, regime and life, see the planet model below) from its inputs, and an input the archetype leaves out is drawn from the planet's formation context. Its ranges are drawn from the seed's own stream, so a seed and an archetype give the same inputs again; the star, orbit and eccentricity it leaves open are drawn by the caller's generator, so only the "Reproduce with" line gives the same world. Without `--planet`, the mass is drawn like a small planet's (0.05 to 20 Earth masses, log-uniform) and every input from the context. The archetype places the planet round its own star (`star`, used when `--star` is not given) at its light (`insolation`, Earth's = 1: `a = √(L/S)`) or its period (`period_days`, Kepler's third law: a hot Jupiter), else in the habitable zone. `--mass`, `--core-fraction`, `--water`, `--envelope`, `--co2`, `--n2`, `--radiogenic` and `--life` set the model's inputs over the archetype's: water in Earth oceans (1.4e21 kg each), CO₂ and N₂ as factors on the formation rule of `planet::inputs::volatiles` (1 typical; the rule's own scatter draws `10^N(0, 0.3)`), radiogenic abundance relative to Earth's. More water than the planet weighs with its envelope, set or drawn from its mass, is refused (`water_heavier_than_planet`, naming both values; the server sends them as `water_oceans` and `mass_earth` beside the code). The world takes from the model its circumference (`2πR`), surface pressure (the air's total), erosion (1 with liquid water, half with hydrocarbons, none otherwise, less under 0.01 bar), volcanism (from its heat flux against Earth's, 1 in the heat-pipe regime, halved under a stagnant lid), surface age (by regime: plates 0.2 to 0.6 Ga, episodic 0.3 to 0.8 like Venus, heat pipe under a million years like Io, a stagnant lid 70 to 95 % of the system's age, an ice lid less the more internal heat it lets out, `1 / (1 + (F / 10 mW/m²)⁴)` of that, so that a tidally heated Europa's surface is under 0.1 Ga old and a Callisto's about 4 Ga) and gravity, and when it is generated its sea level (its oceans filling the relief) and temperature; the sea level, land share, volcanism, circumference, erosion, pressure and surface age are no longer options (`sea_level`, `land_fraction`, `volcanic`, `circumference_km`, `erosion`, `pressure_bar` and `surface_age_ga` of a request are refused as unknown fields). A world whose eccentricity is drawn is circularised when its star's tides have done so for the model's mass and radius. The CLI prints the class, the archetype, the model's inputs and its regime, then the world, and its "Reproduce with" line names the archetype, the seed, the inputs the planet formed with and the orbit. The inputs are those `world.json` stores under `formation` (`PlanetModel::formation`): the mass drawn before photoevaporation, the envelope share at formation, the water in Earth oceans of that mass, the CO₂ and N₂ factors (the stored volatiles over their formation rule), the core share, the radiogenic abundance and whether life arose. Pinned, they make the same planet (its inputs and class) under another seed or at another epoch; only the CH₄, NH₃ and sulphur scatter, which no request sets, follows the seed, and the nitrogen ammonia leaves in the air with it.

**axial-tilt** — tilt of the rotation axis. Shapes the temperature profile from equator to poles and places the tropics and polar circles, see [Temperature](#temperature--axial-tilt). Drawn (neither the request nor the archetype sets it) on a planet without a large moon (mass ratio under 0.005), it ranges from 0 to 85° instead of 5 to 40°: without the Moon, Earth's tilt could wander chaotically from nearly 0 to about 85° (Laskar, Joutel & Robutel 1993), and Mars's, without one, ranges from 0 to 60° (Laskar & Robutel 1993). Both the 0.005 threshold and the single tilt drawn for the world's whole life are the app's choices: over hundreds of millions of years a moonless Earth's tilt stays within a range of 20–25° (Lissauer et al. 2012), so the range says where a moonless tilt can be, not how fast it moves. A drawn tilt is 0 on a rocky planet its star has despun (locked, or in the 3:2 resonance, see **Tidal locking** below): the same tides stand it upright, in under 0.1 Gyr round small red dwarfs (Heller et al. 2011), as Mercury's axis is (2 arcminutes).

**erosion**, **pressure** and **surface age** — from the planet model (see **planet** above): how much rain carves the terrain (droplets per cell of the erosion grid, see [Erosion](#hydraulic-erosion)); the surface pressure of the atmosphere in bar (Earth: 1, Mars: 0.006, Venus: 92), stored in `world.json` as `pressure_bar`, which evens temperatures out between the equator and the poles, and below 0.01 bar keeps no water liquid (no rain, rivers or lakes; see [Temperature](#temperature--axial-tilt)), the viewer drawing the sky from it; the age of the oldest surviving surface in Ga, how long impacts have cratered it since it was last renewed, never older than the star (`--age`), stored as `surface_age_ga`, which sets how cratered the world is (see [Impact craters](#impact-craters)). The volcanism sets the share of the surface in volcanic provinces, and so the number of volcanoes, not their size: `0` none, `0.4` 12 %, `1` 30 %. A giant's or an enveloped body's values come from its preset.

**star** and **orbit** — the host star, a main-sequence dwarf from `O3V` to `M9.5V` (the Sun is `G2V`), and the distance of the planet from it. Without `--star`, the archetype's star is taken, else one is drawn with the frequencies of the solar neighbourhood (about three quarters red dwarfs, M; 12 % K, 8 % G, 3 % F, under 1 % A, B and O). The orbit is given either in AU (`--orbit-au`) or as a position in the star's habitable zone (`--hz-position`, Kopparapu et al. 2014: `0` at its inner edge, `1` at its outer edge, below 0 closer in, above 1 farther out), so the same position gives the same light around any star; an archetype places its planet by its light or its period (see **planet** above). The orbit is an ellipse of eccentricity `--eccentricity` with the star at a focus: the planet ranges from `a(1 − e)` (periapsis) to `a(1 + e)` (apoapsis) and receives `1 / sqrt(1 − e²)` times more light over its year than on a circle of the same semi-major axis (×1.02 for Mercury's 0.206, ×1.25 at 0.6), which warms its climate; the seasons stay annual means. Without a value, the eccentricity is drawn from the beta distribution Kipping (2013) fitted to the planets known for a giant (mean 0.22, most below 0.2), and from the one Van Eylen et al. (2019, table 3) fitted to Kepler's small planets for a rocky planet (mean 0.26 for the planets of single-transiting systems, 0.05 for those of multi-transiting ones; fitted to periods of 5 to about 100 days and applied at every distance), except for the planets whose orbits the star's tides have circularised (0): those within about 0.05 AU of a red dwarf or 0.07 AU of the Sun, further out for a heavier rocky planet. A planet the tides despin keeps its eccentricity: on a round orbit it is locked, on a more eccentric one it may be caught in a spin–orbit resonance, whose odds rise with the eccentricity (see **Tidal locking**). The star's temperature, luminosity, mass and radius come from the mean dwarf sequence of Pecaut & Mamajek (2013). Its age (`--age`) is drawn from a stream of its own, uniformly between 0.5 and 10 Gyr (the thin disc has formed stars at a roughly constant rate over about 10 Gyr, Binney, Dehnen & Bertelli 2000; the 0.5 Gyr floor is a calibration), or up to the star's lifetime when that is shorter, and stored as `age_gyr` under `star`; it decides how far the tides have despun and circularised the planet and how far a giant has cooled (worlds made before have the Sun's 4.6 Gyr). `world.json` stores them under `star`, with what they imply: the light the planet gets (`insolation`, relative to Earth's), the habitable zone, the equilibrium temperature (averaged over the year, which an eccentric orbit lowers a little although it brings more light: `1 − e²/16 − 15e⁴/1024`, Méndez & Rivera-Valentín 2017), the year, the apparent size of the star, its lifetime (interpolated by mass in the lifetimes of Fraknoi et al. 2022, Table 22.1, down to 11 trillion years for the lightest star, Adams & Laughlin 1997), whether the planet is tidally locked or held in a spin–orbit resonance (`spin_orbit_3_2` for Mercury's 3:2, `spin_turns` for the turns per orbit of any: 1.5 or 2) and the colour of its light (worlds made before stars have none and orbit the Sun at 1 AU). It sets the planet's temperatures, see [Temperature](#temperature--axial-tilt). The research behind it is in [docs/analysis/10-stars.md](docs/analysis/10-stars.md); see `src/star.rs`.

**moons** — a world has the moons its seed draws (`--moons auto`, the default), none (`--moons none`), or a number of them (`--moons 3`, from 1 to 6; `{"count": 3}` in a request). With a number, the moons the seed draws come first (the largest when there are too many), then more are drawn from a sequence of their own: a giant-impact moon when there is none, debris moonlets close in, then captured moons far out (from 0.03 to 0.45 Hill radii, retrograde beyond a third of it, where only retrograde orbits last, and half as eccentric), each kept when its orbit is stable and outside the chaotic zone of every heavier one (`1.5 a q^(2/7)` from periapsis to apoapsis, Wisdom 1980). A drawn star and orbit that leave no room (a Hill sphere too small, or a planet its star has despun with no moon light enough to last) are drawn again, up to 64 times, so the world stays coherent; set ones that leave no room are refused (`no_room_for_moons`). The moons then shape the rest as usual: a large one steadies the tilt, and the tides set the shores (below). `world.json` records the number asked for (`count_asked`), so the world reproduces. They come from a noise stream of their own, so the planet does not change with them, and follow the research in [docs/analysis/11-moons.md](docs/analysis/11-moons.md):

- **Giant impact** (the Moon, Charon): one large round moon, on about one rocky planet in 12 (Elser et al. 2011) and one small world in two (below 0.25 Earth radii, rising in between), with a mass ratio of 0.005 to 0.03 on large planets and up to 0.15 on small ones, 10 to 65 planet radii out (8 to 25 on small worlds). When the moon's tides have despun the planet within the system's age (the despinning time of Gladman et al. 1996, as for the planets), the planet turns with its moon, its day equal to the moon's month; when their barycentre lies outside the planet, they are a binary, like Pluto and Charon. The 1 in 12 comes from planets of a Sun-like star with Jupiter and Saturn, and is applied round every star; the small worlds' 1 in 2 and the odds below are calibrations.
- **Debris** (Phobos and Deimos): one or two small irregular moons close in (2.2 to 9 planet radii) on one planet in five without a large moon, sized like Phobos around Mars, scaled with the planet.
- **Captured**: one to three small irregular moons (1 to 30 km) on one planet in seven, far out (0.05 to 0.3 Hill radii), eccentric, inclined 10 to 55° or, retrograde, 130 to 170° (orbits between 55 and 130° are generally unstable, Carruba et al. 2002 via Shen & Tremaine 2008), half of them retrograde.

Moons orbit beyond the Roche limit (fluid for round moons, rigid for small ones) and within a third of the Hill radius at periapsis (half for retrograde ones), the cautious end of the published range (Barnes & O'Brien 2002 take 0.36 and 0.5, Shen & Tremaine 2008 find 0.5 and 0.7); one closer than eight mutual Hill radii to a larger moon is dropped. A planet its star has despun (locked, or in the 3:2 resonance) has its synchronous orbit beyond its Hill sphere: tides bring every moon down, and it keeps only moons light enough to outlast the system's age (Barnes & O'Brien 2002, eq. 8: about 2 × 10¹⁸ kg, a 60 km moonlet, round an Earth in the habitable zone of an M5 dwarf). A moon is locked to its planet when the planet's tides have despun it within the system's age (the same despinning time, its Love number from the rigidity of rock), whatever its origin: close moons are, some far captured ones are not. Moons below 600 km across (rock) or 400 km (ice, beyond an equilibrium temperature of 150 K), a mean radius of 300 and 200 km (Lineweaver & Norman 2010), get three unequal axes, the more unequal the smaller and equal at the limit; a locked moon is also stretched by its planet's tides, from the limit up in full, into the hydrostatic ellipsoid of a fluid body (`(b − c) / (a − c) = 1/4`, Noyelles 2017; Mimas comes out 18 km longer than it is wide, 17 measured). Each moon migrates by the planet's tides: it recedes above the synchronous orbit (the planet's day is drawn from 12 to 36 hours), falls in below it or on a retrograde orbit, stays put in a binary, and hardly moves far out. The planet's mass, for its moons as on its card, and its gravity follow the mass–radius relation of rocky planets (see [Gravity modifier](#gravity-modifier--landscape-relief)), as does its density in the moons' Roche limit. On Earth-sized planets, about 63 % have no moon, 21 % one and 16 % two or more. `world.json` stores the system under `moons` (each moon's origin, composition, radius and axes, mass ratio, density, gravity, orbit, period, tidal locking, migration, apparent size from the planet and barycentre; the planet's day, synchronous orbit and Hill radius), and the CLI prints one line on them. See `src/moons.rs`.

The moons shape their planet in two ways. Its drawn axial tilt ranges wider without a large moon (see **axial-tilt** above). And its tides, from the moons and the star (relative to Earth's, the Moon's and the Sun's together: a height in planet radii of `mass ratio × (radius / distance)³`, times the planet's size), set the width of its tidal flats: the look's `shore_width_km` is multiplied by the fourth root of the tides, between 0.5 and 2 (Earth with the Moon keeps its 40 km; without one, the Sun alone gives 0.31 of Earth's tides and 30 km). A planet locked to its star has no tides that come and go (0.5), nor does a binary from its moon, locked face to face with it. `world.json` stores the tides and whether the tilt is steadied under `moons`, and the look it stores has the shore width as generated. In the viewer, a binary's moon hangs over the same point of its planet, at longitude 180°. A world that is itself the moon of a giant (see **host**) turns with the giant, not with its star: its equator lies in the giant's equatorial plane and the giant holds its spin axis, so its seasons follow the giant's tilt, drawn in the usual 5 to 40° (never left free); its star's tides come and go with its month; and of the giant's huge tidal bulge, still on a world locked to it, only the part its slightly eccentric orbit swings (about `3e` of it, as Io's flexes) comes and goes, from 1.5 to 18 times Earth's tides around a Jupiter, so its tidal flats are wide.

**giants** — a world has the giant planets its seed draws in its system (`--giants auto`, the default), or none modelled (`--giants none`), after the research in [docs/analysis/12-gas-giants.md](docs/analysis/12-gas-giants.md). They come from a noise stream of their own, as does the star's metallicity when drawn (`--metallicity`, `[Fe/H]` in dex: a normal law of mean −0.1 and spread 0.2 within `[-1, 0.5]`, like the stars nearby, Casagrande et al. 2011), so the planet's other drawn values do not change with them. A Poisson number of gas giants (0.1 to 13 Jupiter masses, `dN/d ln M ∝ M^−0.31`, Cumming et al. 2008) of mean 0.25 around a Sun-like star of the Sun's metallicity, times `10^(2 [Fe/H])` (Fischer & Valenti 2005) and the star's mass, and of ice giants (10 to 50 Earth masses) of mean 0.3, times `10^(0.5 [Fe/H])`; distances log-normal around twice (gas) and five times (ice) the ice line, `2.7 √L` AU (Fulton et al. 2021); about 1 % of Sun-like stars with a hot Jupiter (1 to 10 days), five times fewer when the world orbits within 0.5 AU; eccentricities from Kipping's law, none for hot Jupiters. A giant too close to the world or another giant (apoapsis to periapsis) is redrawn, then dropped; the gap kept, in mutual Hill radii, is 10 for Earth-mass planets and falls as the pair's mass share to the power −1/12 for heavier ones (Chambers et al. 1996; Pu & Wu 2015): 6.4 for a Jupiter and a Saturn, never under `2√3` (Gladman 1993); at most four. Each gets a letter by distance (`b`, `c`, …), a radius from its mass (up to Neptune's mass, that of a giant of Neptune's composition, the planet model's iron, rock and water layers under the envelope of Lopez & Fortney 2014 thinned by its heavy elements, so that Uranus's mass gives 3.80 Earth radii; then through Neptune, Saturn and Jupiter, `M^−0.04` above, inflated from an equilibrium temperature of about 1 000 K by the heat a few percent of its starlight puts into it, most near 1 700 K and halved by 2 200 K, the share turned into heat falling at high fluxes, Thorngren & Fortney 2018), its year, equilibrium and effective temperatures (with its internal heat, cooled over the age of the system), the cloud class of Sudarsky et al. (2000), a colour and rings (none, faint or bright, with the odds of the giant looks' `[giant] rings`: 25 % bright and 50 % faint on cold gas giants, 70 % faint on ice giants, 5 % faint on hot ones). Around a Sun-like star about one world in four has a gas giant; around red dwarfs, few. They act on the world twice. An eccentric giant forces an eccentricity on it (secular theory, Heppenheimer 1978: `(5/4)(a/a_g) e_g / (1 − e_g²)` inside its orbit): a drawn eccentricity is raised to it (Jupiter forces 0.012 on Earth), before the moons, the tides' effects (tilt, tidal flats) and the spin see the orbit, so a request pinning the final eccentricity resolves to the same world. And the giant just outside the orbit (1.5 to 20 times as far) sets the impact rate, hence the crater density (Horner & Jones 2008, moderated): 0.7 times the Solar System's without one, 1.8 times for a fifth of Jupiter's mass, 1 for Jupiter's, 0.7 for 13; with `--giants none` the rate stays the Solar System's, as for worlds made before giants. `world.json` stores the star's `metallicity` under `star` and the system under `giants` (each giant, the forced eccentricity and the impact factor), and the CLI prints one line on them. See `src/giants.rs`.

**giant worlds** — a planet whose model is a giant (a gas giant, an ice giant, a hot Jupiter, a hot Neptune (an ice giant within 10 days of its star, drawn with the `hot-jupiter` look) or a mini-Neptune: `--planet jupiter-like`, `hot-jupiter`, or a mass of tens of Earth masses) generates a giant world instead of a rocky one (backlog item 14, step B; `src/giant_world.rs`), with the `gas-giant`, `ice-giant` or `hot-jupiter` look (a mini-Neptune as an ice giant of at least 5 Earth masses): no ground, but the top of its clouds. Its look's `[giant]` table gives its kind (`gas` or `ice`) and its odds of bright and faint rings; its mass is the model's (`--mass`). Its radius follows from its model's mass, composition and light: under 30 Earth masses the model's own radius (its solid layers under its envelope, see [docs/science/planet-interiors.md](docs/science/planet-interiors.md)), then the gas giants' law through Saturn and Jupiter, inflated when hot; a giant world without a model takes the radius of the giant neighbours; it has no erosion, volcanoes, craters or giant neighbours. Its moons are its own kind (`--moons`): regular moons grown in its circumplanetary disk, 1 to 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ of its mass together (Canup & Ward 2006), two to five (or as many as asked) on a near-resonant chain from 4 to 8 of its radii outwards, each period about twice the last as Io's, Europa's and Ganymede's, with a forced eccentricity of 0.001 to 0.01; the disk leaves either moons of comparable mass or, more often the lighter the gas giant (Sasaki et al. 2010: Saturn 77 %, Jupiter 23 %), one dominant moon in the chain's outer half with 90 to 97 % of the mass, as Titan (eccentricity up to 0.03; `disk_shape` in `world.json`'s `moons`); an ice giant's moons, from the disk its giant impact raised (Ida et al. 2020), always form a chain of comparable moons with 1 to 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ of its mass, as Uranus's; rocky inside the disk's ice line and icy beyond, a line at `11 (T_eq / 110 K)⁴` giant radii (11 for Jupiter, inside the first moon for Saturn and colder giants); on 35 % of ice giants a large retrograde moon captured instead (Triton), the regular ones left small and close; none orbits inside the giant's Roche limit for an icy moon (`2.44 R (ρ_giant / 1.9)^(1/3)`) or within 0.3 of its radii of its rings' outer edge, the chain moving outwards when it would (`giant_moon_floor_radii`); a hot giant keeps none. A giant of a system draws its regular moons the same way. The chain ends at a third of the Hill radius, and inside a captured Triton's orbit; a giant its star has locked keeps only the moons light enough to outlast the system (Barnes & O'Brien 2002, with the giant's own tides). They are round from the same sizes as a rocky planet's moons (the smallest, beside a captured Triton, are not) and locked to the giant when its tides have despun them. A moon's world is locked to its planet, never to the star, whatever its planet's spin. Asking a giant for more moons than it keeps is an error (`hot_giant_moons`): close to its star, a hot giant keeps none, one its star has locked only moons light enough to outlast its tides, and a small Hill sphere fewer than asked. Its star and orbit are kept rather than drawn again, which would make it another kind of giant. A giant has no giant neighbours and is nobody's moon: `--giants` and `--host` are ignored; the CLI warns, and the server lists them in `ignored` of its answer to `POST /api/systems` (around a planet), which the web app shows. The CLI's "Reproduce with" line and the web app's **Copy command** and **Vary** leave them out. Each is a body of its system, generated as a rocky planet's moons are. Its spin is drawn (gas giants 9 to 12 hours, ice giants 14 to 19; a hot giant, or one its star's tides have locked with a giant's own tidal parameters, see **Tidal locking**, turns once a year) and flattens it (`0.7 q`, `q = Ω²R³/GM`: Jupiter 0.065, Saturn 0.098). Fast rotation bands it: the number of eastward jets in each hemisphere (the wind profile holds twice as many less one, alternating, besides the equatorial jet) follows the Rhines scale, `0.45 √(ΩR/U)`, where `U` is the speed of the eddies that make the jets, `U₀ (F/F₀)^(2/5) (β/β₀)^(−1/5)` from the flux `F = σT_eff⁴` the giant radiates (the starlight it absorbs plus its internal heat) and `β = 2Ω/R`, with Jupiter's 100 m/s, 14.1 W/m² and `β` for gas giants and Neptune-like references for ice giants (an energy-balance argument derived for the app, not a published law). The peak wind of the jets is another number, a calibration on the four giants of the Solar System (`Ro = U/ΩR`: for a gas giant `0.0119 (7.485 W/m² / F_int)^0.5` from its internal heat flux, Jupiter 150 m/s and a Saturn-like giant about 400 m/s against "over 400"; 0.11 for an ice giant, about 285 m/s; Showman et al. 2010), at least an irradiation floor for a giant warm from its star (so at a fixed mass and spin a giant never blows slower closer in, until tidal locking takes its spin, where the wind falls to that floor), and no wind of the profile passes 0.75 of the speed of sound at its effective temperature (the whole profile is scaled down when it would); a hot giant's is 2 000 m/s at 1 500 K. The ice giants' profile is Neptune's, from Voyager 2 (westward 398 m/s at the equator, eastward at high latitudes, Sromovsky et al. 1993). Jupiter gets five jets, a Saturn-like giant four to seven, Neptune one (retrograde at the equator, prograde at high latitudes; an ice giant with two or more jets gets jets at its band edges and a retrograde equatorial one). Pale zones and dark belts alternate from an equatorial zone to the polar regions (58° to 74°), each band with its own width, tint and hue; the latitude a pixel samples is shifted by noise stretched along the bands, more where jets shear; long-lived vortices swirl the bands (a great spot on 45 % of gas giants, in a zone of the south between two jets, as the Great Red Spot lies in the South Tropical Zone, white ovals, dark spots with bright companion clouds on half the ice giants). Poleward of the bands (from 58° to 74°), each pole has its own look, drawn in an azimuthal frame around it: on a gas giant, about 55 % of poles hold a cluster of cyclones, a central one ringed by five to nine others packed edge to edge, each 5 000 to 8 000 km across with spiral arms, a dark eye and collar (Jupiter's eight in the north and five in the south, Adriani et al. 2018), on a dark bluish ground of folded filaments, billowing white clouds and tawny patches (Orton et al. 2017); about 15 % a hexagonal jet round a polar vortex with a bright-rimmed eye (Saturn's north, Godfrey 1988); the rest, like ice giants (a lone polar cyclone on 30 % of their poles) and hot giants, a mottled haze with a bright cap. `world.json` gives each pole's style and ring of cyclones (`poles`, `polar_cyclones`). The colours follow the Sudarsky class of its effective temperature, with its internal heat cooled over the age of its system (`T ∝ M^0.5 t^−0.29`, fitted to the evolution tables of Baraffe et al. 2003 and set to Jupiter's 107 K at 4.6 Gyr: ten Jupiter masses keep about 340 K, Baraffe's 322 K at 5 Gyr): the class comes from the cloud decks that condense on its temperature profile (ammonia, ammonium hydrosulphide, water, `src/giant_clouds.rs`), class I while the ammonia deck reaches the tropopause (up to about 205 K), class II with water clouds on top (up to 350 K), class III with none (to 900 K), class IV to 1 500 K, class V above, or above 900 K when its surface gravity is under 10 m/s² (an inflated hot Jupiter); a deck that only just reaches the tropopause is thin and blends the palette into the next class, and the height between zones and belts follows the scale height and the gap between decks. Palettes: cream and ochre ammonia clouds, cyan to blue methane for cold ice giants (warmer ones keep a blue tinge: methane stays in their gas), white water clouds, azure clear skies, near-black alkali roasters, pale silicate clouds for class V (a Bond albedo near 0.5 against 0.03 for class IV). A hot Jupiter's day side is far hotter than its night side, its hot spot carried east by superrotation. The layers keep the format of a world: the biomes are `GiantZone`, `GiantBelt`, `GiantStorm` and `GiantPolar` (in the giant's own colours in the biome table of its `world.json`; the storm colour is that of its largest storm, the great spot or else the white ovals); the elevation is the height of the cloud tops above a deck at 0.3; the temperature is that of the cloud tops; the moisture layer carries the eastward wind, as a share of the strongest; `colour.png` is the rendered clouds, with a flat `normal.png`. `world.json` stores the giant under `giant`: mass, radius, density, gravity, day, flattening, temperatures, heat ratio, class, jets, strongest winds and the wind profile every 2°, storms (`great_spot`, and `great_spot_at`, its longitude and latitude, which the viewer uses to find the spot that drifts slowly), palette and rings (edges and gaps in planet radii, opacity, colour). Detail tiles are refused: there is no ground to detail.

**giant moons** — a rocky world can be the moon of a giant planet that orbits the star where the world would (backlog item 14, step C; `src/exomoon.rs`): rarely when drawn (`--host auto`: one world in 50, as an Earth-sized moon needs a capture or a super-Jupiter, moons formed around a giant holding about 10⁻⁴ of its mass, Canup & Ward 2006), always with `--host giant`, never with `--host none`. The giant is drawn on its own noise stream: 0.3 to 10 Jupiter masses (log-uniform), the world 6 to 40 of its radii out (within a third of its Hill radius), on an orbit of eccentricity 0.001 to 0.012 kept by resonances, locked to it (its day is its orbit). The giant's tides heat the world, `Ė = (21/2)(k₂/Q) G M² R⁵ n e² / a⁶` (Peale et al. 1979, Io's `k₂/Q` = 0.015: Io gets about 2 W/m²), an internal heat added to the climate as radiated power and raising a drawn volcanism (none below 0.05 W/m², all from 50); a world of liquid water is moved out until starlight and tides stay under 80 % of the runaway limit (300 W/m² absorbed, the habitable edge of Heller & Barnes 2013), a dry or volcanic one may be kneaded like Io, up to a world of lava. The world's planet model takes the giant's tides as its tidal heat too (the giant's mass, the world's distance and eccentricity, with the model's own `k₂/Q`), drawn again once the giant's orbit is settled, and inside the giant's magnetosphere its air meets the escape of an unshielded body. Such a world has no moons of its own: asking for a number of moons keeps a drawn world from becoming one, and asking for both (`--moons 3 --host giant`) is an error (`moons_of_a_moon`). The giant is a planet of the world's system, generated as a giant world (512 pixels wide), and its other regular moons as rocky or icy moons of their own (sized like the world's moons and seeded from the giant's seed, a round one through the planet model as a system's round moons are), the world among them, all bodies of the system, and `world.json` stores it under `host`: the giant's description, the orbit (km, giant radii, eccentricity, period), its apparent size, the tidal heating (W/m², and the temperature it would keep alone), whether the world lies inside the habitable edge, and the giant's Hill radius. The CLI prints a line on it.

Each moon is generated after its planet as a world of its own, a body of its system (`bodies/<planet>-<n>/`, nearest first) with the layout of a world. A round moon (600 km across for rock, 400 km for ice) goes through the planet model as a planet does, with its mass, its ice share and how it formed (a giant's disk, a capture, an impact) as inputs, its planet's light and its planet's tides as heat: its type is the model's class (`ice-world`, `rocky`, `hydrocarbon-world`…) and its world a planet's of that model, at its tilt, with its own day (`day_hours`: a moon locked to its planet turns once per orbit, as a world asked around a giant does) and with no moons of its own (see `docs/system-spec.md`, "Moons"). A smaller moon gets the `rocky-moon` or `icy-moon` look (`looks/moons/`, not archetypes: never asked for), no air, no erosion, never wet, 4 Ga of craters, maria only on rocky moons of 500 km in radius or more, the planet's star and orbit, a tilt to its orbit of 6.68° for a giant-impact moon (the Moon's, NASA: its planet swings north and south in its sky, the libration in latitude) and 0° for the others, the same in the system's spins, and a map as many pixels per km as the planet's (from 512 pixels wide up to the planet's width). The noise relief of a body (its span from the old floor to the top of its mountains at its gravity) is flattened towards its waterline (so that its coasts stay put; its sea level lies above the altitude lines) to stay within 8 % of its radius (Vesta's central mound is about 8 % of its radius), so relief grows as a share of the radius as bodies shrink, as on real small bodies; and on an airless body without plates whose lid is stagnant or overturns now and then (every moon, airless dead worlds, airless episodic worlds; not heat-pipe worlds, whose lava and sinking crust raise Io's 17 km blocks), within the relief impacts give it, a 9 km span calibrated so that a Moon-sized rocky moon spans about the Moon's 13 km with its craters (14 km at 256 × 128), ice holding 0.4 of rock's (complex craters on Ganymede are 0.4 as deep as lunar ones, Schenk 2002), which also flattens icy bodies' complex craters. Planets keep their whole relief, the detail tiles' octaves with it, their relief noise is no finer than at 5 000 km around, and their hillshade exaggerates less, down to the true relief (under 300 km in radius): craters shape them, as on Phobos. An irregular moon, or a round one stretched by its planet's tides, gets `raw_data/shape.png`, its radius as a share of its mean one (a triaxial ellipsoid, the long axis at longitude 180° where it faces its planet, the short one along its spin, with low-frequency bumps), and `shape` in its `world.json`; the elevation details it. The server writes the moons into the world's temporary directory, so they come and go with it, and reports a "Moons" stage.

**circumference** — equatorial circumference of the planet in kilometres, `2πR` of the model's radius. Drives two derived quantities (noise scale and gravity) described in the [Calculations](#calculations) section below.

**systems** — the generator is becoming a system generator (see `docs/system-spec.md`): a star and every body around it, drawn in milliseconds (`src/system.rs`), each body's maps generated later. `--system` draws a whole system from archetypes and counts, `--draw-only` the system around the planet the other options describe (a giant's moon puts its giant on that orbit and the world among its regular moons); both write `systems/<id>/system.json` (the id is the star's designation and the seed, `mgs-38819-7`; `--alone` draws a lone planet, its star, the planet and its moons only, with `-lone-` before the seed: `mgs-38819-lone-7`) and print its bodies. A planet asked for by archetype (`earth-like=2`) sits at its archetype's insolation (`a = √(L/S)` for the range in Earth's, so a Venus-like planet gets 1.7 to 2.1) or period (Kepler's third law, a hot Jupiter's days), with the archetype's mass drawn in its range and its inputs (core share, water, life…, `archetypes/`) drawn on a stream of the body's own, so that no other body's draws shift them (the masses, orbits and eccentricities still come off the system's stream in request order, so removing one planet can move the others); the star the archetype names is ignored, the system's star rules, and a planet may be pushed out to four times the outer orbit of its range to keep the spacing (more of them than fit is refused with the archetype named). The planets are placed from the star out, at least 10 mutual Hill radii apart for Earth-mass planets, fewer for heavier ones (6.4 for a Jupiter and a Saturn, as for giants above), a hot Jupiter (drawn round about 1 % of Sun-like stars, as for a world's giants, when the system is left to the draw) keeping others beyond four times its period; a rocky planet takes the size of the mass it was spaced with, by the mass–radius relation of rocky planets (see **Gravity modifier**; the mass of its archetype, 0.9 to 1.1 Earth masses for an Earth-like one…), and draws its moons as a world does, a giant its regular moons as a giant world draws them: two to five on a resonant chain within a third of its Hill radius, a captured Triton on some ice giants, only light ones if its star has locked it. Rocky planets keep out of the first cold giant's belt zone, 0.40 to 0.63 of its distance, where that giant stops a planet forming. The system then draws its small bodies (`src/belts.rs`, after [docs/analysis/13-asteroids.md](docs/analysis/13-asteroids.md)), each from a stream of its own so the planets and moons do not move:

- **An inner belt** inside the first cold giant, from 0.40 of its distance to its 2:1 resonance, with Kirkwood gaps at its 3:1, 5:2 and 7:3 and the Hildas in its 3:2. None without a giant, whose planetesimals grew into planets. Its mass is log-uniform from 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻² Earth masses; the Sun's is 4 × 10⁻⁴.
- **An outer belt** between the outermost giant's 3:2 and 2:1, or at 11 to 18 ice lines without a giant, for half of F, G and K stars, a quarter of M dwarfs and three in ten hotter stars. With a giant, a **scattered disk** goes with it.
- **Trojans** at each planet's L4 and L5 points. A planet keeps them where Routh's criterion holds (mass share under 0.0385) and no heavier giant orbits within a period ratio of 3 (a rocky neighbour does not count: Mars keeps its trojans next to the Earth).

A belt is drawn only where the planets leave it a span of 1.15 in ratio at least. The belts set every body's crater rate: the giant beyond it (Horner & Jones 2008) times four fifths of the asteroids (the inner belt against the Sun's) and a fifth of the comets (the outer belt and scattered disk against the Sun's 0.05 Earth masses), a twentieth at least; a moon strikes at its planet's rate. Systems drawn before keep their bodies and their crater rates.

A request that cannot be met fails with its reason: more than 15 planets, more planets of a type than fit in its band (`zone_full`, with how many fit), an unknown type. Each body stores its orbit, size, mass, spin, the starlight it gets, the colours of the sphere the viewer draws until its maps exist, and the parameters its generation needs. The star stores its activity (rotation, cycle, spots, granulation and limb darkening; see [docs/science/star-activity.md](docs/science/star-activity.md)), derived when reading a system drawn before it existed; the viewer's star view draws it as a living surface, its spots born, turning and decaying on the system clock (see [web/README.md](web/README.md)). `--body <id>` then generates a body's maps and its neighbourhood's (a planet and its moons; a moon, its planet and the planet's other moons), each into `systems/<id>/bodies/<body>/` with the layout of a world and its `world.json` naming its system and body, written into `bodies/.tmp-<body>/` and renamed into place once complete, then marked `generated` in `system.json`; with `--in-system <id>` it generates in a system already drawn (`map-generator --in-system mgs-48937-21 --body c-1`).

The viewer can also show a system's star alone, close up (the *star view*, `#system=<id>&body=star`): its granules boiling, the supergranule network seen from afar, its spots born, turning and decaying, their filaments, umbral dots, light bridges and pores changing shape over their life, its coronal loops, from 12 star radii down to 1.02, on a clock from 21 seconds to 64 days a second that runs over the star's activity cycle, with jumps to its next maximum and minimum. It shares its day with the system view, and only the view shown runs (see [web/README.md](web/README.md)).

The planet model (`crates/mapgen/src/planet/`, issue #423) derives what a planet is from its mass and composition rather than from a type name: the interior and radius, the XUV a star gives it and the envelope that photoevaporation leaves, its internal heat, and, for a body with no envelope left, the water it keeps, its tectonic regime, core and dynamo, atmosphere, surface temperature and liquid, life, and a class (`rocky`, `ocean-world`, `runaway-greenhouse`, `hydrocarbon-world`, `lava-world`, `volcanic-world` (a solid surface in the heat-pipe regime, Io's, read as a moon in the viewer: "volcanic moon") and the others) (an `ice-world` is a planet of at least 5 % water and ices that keeps at least 0.01 Earth oceans at its surface: one that lost its ice to its star is `rocky` or `super-earth`). `planet::draw_inputs_with` draws a planet's inputs and sets those an `InputOverrides` names (core share, water in Earth oceans, envelope, CO₂ and N₂ as factors on the formation rule, radiogenic abundance, life yes/no) after the draws, so without overrides it draws exactly as `draw_inputs` and an override shifts no other draw (`derive_model_with` is the same for a body of a system). A body with a water layer and a frozen surface also gets its `ice_shell` (`planet::ice_shell`: the thickness of the shell, `shell_km`, that carries its internal heat out by conduction through ice whose conductivity is k = 651/T, and the liquid ocean left beneath it, `ocean_km`, down to the ice III–V–liquid triple point, 350.1 MPa): Europa's gets some 15 km over an ocean. A `hydrocarbon-world`'s surface liquid lies poleward of 55° of latitude only, as Titan's lakes and seas do (the low latitudes are dry dunes): its `world.json` carries that latitude as `liquid_poleward_deg` (absent on every other world, whose sea follows its relief alone), planet or moon alike; a calibration with Titan as its target. `planet::planet_model` chains it all and `planet::body_model` runs it for a planet of a stored system; a drawn planet stores its model (`Body.model`) and is typed by its class (`body_type` is the class key), and the world of a planet with a surface is generated from the model through `planet::interim`: the base look closest to it (`base_of`), its sky, gravity and regime, then, when the world is generated, its sea level and its temperature shift (a planet's calibrated sea level and temperature are in its `world.json`; `body.params` hold the drawn values). A planet that is no giant whose envelope hides its surface (about 0.3 % of them) renders with its class's look (`frozen` for an ice world, `barren` otherwise) at the model's radius and gravity, with no ocean and its temperature aimed at a bare surface's; part 3b derives the surface under the envelope. The formats moved on (world format 17); systems drawn before keep their bodies and are listed as outdated. The world a request asks for (`--planet <archetype>`, or none) goes through the model like its neighbours: a system drawn around it stores its model on its body (the moon of a giant included, its model at the giant's orbit round the star) and types it by its class. A whole system (`--system`) names archetypes, each planet at its archetype's insolation or period with its mass and inputs. `cargo run --release -p mapgen --example planet_census -- 500` draws systems and prints the base looks the worlds render with, the class, liquid, life and regime mixes, the runaways by source (insolation limit or boiled ocean), the planets whose envelope hides their surface, the radius histogram and the classes of the close-in planets. See [docs/science/planet-interiors.md](docs/science/planet-interiors.md) and [docs/science/planet-atmospheres.md](docs/science/planet-atmospheres.md).

### Environment

| Variable               | Effect                                                                                                                          | Default |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| `MAPGEN_NO_NOISE_MAPS` | `true` (or `1`, `yes`, `on`) skips the noise maps like `--no-noise-maps`; `false` (or `0`, `no`, `off`) keeps them, the default | `false` |
| `MAPGEN_NO_SVG`        | `true` (or `1`, `yes`, `on`) skips `world.svg` like `--no-svg`; `false` (or `0`, `no`, `off`) writes it, the default            | `false` |

The flag wins when both are given: `--no-noise-maps` always skips the maps (same for `--no-svg`). For example, to skip them for every run of a shell session:

```bash
export MAPGEN_NO_NOISE_MAPS=true
mise run gen -- --planet earth-like
```

### Examples

```bash
# Fully random world
mise run gen

# Mars-like planet, everything else random
mise run gen -- --planet mars-like

# Earth-like world with three oceans of water and no life
mise run gen -- --planet earth-like --water 3 --life no

# A dry rock of half Earth's mass with no CO₂, round the Sun at 1 AU
mise run gen -- --mass 0.5 --water 0 --co2 0 --star G2V --orbit-au 1

# Quick low-resolution preview, images only
mise run gen -- --width 480 --height 270 --no-noise-maps

# A whole system: two Earth-like planets and a Jupiter-like giant round a K star
mise run gen -- --system earth-like=2,jupiter-like=1 --star K1V

# A whole system with a Titan: its Saturn comes with it
mise run gen -- --system earth-like=1,titan-like=1 --star G2V

# Then generate one of its bodies (here the giant's first moon)
mise run gen -- --in-system mgs-12392-11 --body e-1
```

### Hex grid export

For games and other tile-based uses, an existing world can be resampled onto a hexagonal grid. It is a separate, on-demand step that reads `raw_data/`, so generate the world with raw data first:

```bash
mise run gen -- --seed 42 --planet earth-like
mise run hex -- systems/mgs-36196-42/bodies/d/sizes/2048x1024 --columns 96
```

| Option               | Description                                                                | Default  |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------- |
| `<world>`            | a body resolution (`systems/<id>/bodies/<body>/sizes/<WxH>`)               | required |
| `--columns <n>`      | hex columns around the planet, `4`–`1024`; rows follow from the map height | `96`     |
| `--river-flow <km²>` | land hexes whose largest river drains at least this area get a river flag  | `500000` |

It writes `<world>/hex/`:

- `hex.png`: the map painted with one colour per hex, outlined, with a blue dot on river hexes.
- `hex.json`: the grid layout and one entry per hex (row-major, `index = row × columns + col`): majority `biome` id (rivers never win a hex, whatever biome the look remaps them to, such as `AcidRiver` on caustic worlds or `LavaField` on volcanic ones, where the river polylines tell them apart; they set `river` instead), mean `elevation`, `moisture` and `temperature`, `max_flow_km2` and `river`. The biome table is copied from `world.json`.

The same resampling runs in the web app (hex overlay of the viewer), through the `mapgen::hex` library module: the server resamples the world it keeps for the detail tiles, one grid at a time, and writes no `hex/` files. While a generation runs it refuses hex grids (503), as loading a world would add to the generation's peak.

The grid is pointy-top in "odd-r" offset coordinates: odd rows are shifted half a hex east, and columns wrap east-west like the planet. Hexes are regular on the flat map, not on the sphere, so polar hexes cover less real ground than equatorial ones; a true spherical hex grid (geodesic) would be a different, much larger project.

The repository is a Cargo workspace:

| Path                     | Content                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `crates/mapgen/`         | The `mapgen` library: generation passes, looks, exports, and `WorldRequest` (parameter limits and defaults)                                                      |
| `crates/cli/`            | The `map-generator` and `hexgrid` command-line tools, thin wrappers around the library                                                                             |
| `crates/server/`         | The `mapgen-server` web server: world library, generation jobs with live progress, static files; see its module documentation for routes and environment variables |

| `archetypes/`            | Built-in archetypes: named sets of planet model inputs (embedded in the binaries)                                                                                  |
| `looks/`                 | Internal looks of worlds until #424 (embedded in the binaries); `moons/` holds the moons'                                                                           |

The library also reports generation progress: `generate_with_progress` calls back as each stage starts (noise, erosion, temperature, coast, moisture, rivers, biomes). Two serial passes run beside parallel work to keep every core busy: the distance to the coast (a Dijkstra) beside the moisture pass, so the coast stage is reported just before the moisture stage and the two take their time together, and the hydrology's priority-flood beside the clouds, which the rivers stage now computes. Its `ts` feature derives TypeScript bindings on the types a web front-end needs.

## Web app

A browser app to explore the generated worlds as a 3D globe or a flat map, switch between biome, elevation, temperature, moisture, river, volcanic, distance-to-sea and floodplain layers, raise the relief, light the planet with a seasonal sun, overlay the hex grid, inspect any point, its river and its hex, measure distances, compare two worlds side by side, download a world's files, and queue new worlds from a form, in English or French. See [web/README.md](web/README.md) for the full feature list and the keyboard shortcuts. It is a personal tool served locally behind the machine-wide Traefik, like the other `*.localhost` apps.

```bash
mise run up      # build and start it on http://planets.localhost
mise run logs    # follow its logs
mise run down    # stop it
```

The container reads and writes `systems/` of this checkout (systems generated with `mise run gen` show up too; the `worlds/` directory of before systems is no longer read or mounted; delete it by hand if you still have one) and reads the archetype files of `archetypes/` (mounted read-only at `/data/archetypes`): any `.toml` archetype added there is offered in the form and by `GET /api/archetypes` without a rebuild, once the server restarts (`docker restart map-generator`): it reads the archetypes at startup. The 13 built-in archetypes are embedded in the binary; a missing directory means built-ins only. Generated files keep the host owner.

**The index.** The server also keeps a SQLite database, the *index*, in `db/` of this checkout (`MAPGEN_DB`, `/data/db/mapgen.sqlite` in the container, mounted from `./db` by `compose.yaml`; `mise run up` creates the directory as the host user, and `db/` is git-ignored). It holds the systems and bodies the library lists (so that `GET /api/systems` no longer opens every `system.json`), each body's seed and parameters, the generator version it was made with and when it was last opened, the names given to bodies, the **pins** of the main menu (`systems.pinned_ms`), the **displayed resolution** of each body (`bodies.displayed_width` and `displayed_height`, NULL for the original), and the generation jobs (with the resolution a job adds, `jobs.size`), which survive a restart (a job queued or running when the server stopped is listed as failed, `interrupted by a server restart`). It is rebuilt from `systems/` when the file is missing or empty, and reconciled with the tree at every start (systems added, rewritten or removed on disk), so the files need no migration (the database has its own layout version, see below); a file that is not a database is kept aside as `mapgen.sqlite.corrupt-<ms>` and rebuilt. Names are still written to each system's `names.json` too, so the tree alone remains enough to rebuild the index; the pins, the displayed resolutions, the dates of last opening and the job history live in the database only (a rebuilt index shows every body at its original resolution; the resolutions on disk are found in the tree). **Pins are not mirrored to any file**: if the database is lost, the names come back from the tree and the pins do not (a system drawn again with `replace` keeps its pin; a deleted one loses it). Pins are also lost when a start finds a system's directory absent (`systems/` not mounted: the reconcile drops the system, and its pin with it), and a database set aside as corrupt takes them along (`mapgen.sqlite.corrupt-<ms>` still holds them). **Layout versions**: the database layout is version 4 (`PRAGMA user_version`; version 4 added `systems.outdated`). The `outdated` flag does not need a layout change: the index records each system's `system_format` (the `format_version` of its `system.json`) and, at every start, flags the systems whose format differs from the running generator's, so a new `system_format_version` keeps pins, displayed resolutions and job history (a file from before that column has its systems read again once). There is no migration: a file of any other layout, older or newer, is renamed `mapgen.sqlite.layout-<version>-<ms>` (with its `-wal` and `-shm`) with a logged warning, and a new index is built from the tree; the names come back from `names.json`, the pins, displayed resolutions, dates of last opening and job history stay in the file set aside. Two servers opening the same old file at once set it aside once. The journal is WAL (readers do not block the writer, commits are cheaper): at every start the server sets it, checks that the pragma answers `wal` and that a write round-trips, and falls back to SQLite's rollback journal with a logged warning if the file system (the Docker VM's bind mount) does not support it. In WAL mode `db/` holds `mapgen.sqlite`, `mapgen.sqlite-wal` and `mapgen.sqlite-shm`: take a backup copy with the server stopped, or with `sqlite3 db/mapgen.sqlite ".backup copy.sqlite"`, and copy it along with `systems/` when you move the data. The API is unchanged.

| Piece     | Technology                               | Location         |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Server    | Rust, axum; calls the `mapgen` library   | `crates/server/` |
| Front-end | Svelte 5, strict TypeScript, three.js    | `web/`           |
| Container | Multi-stage `Dockerfile`, `compose.yaml` | repository root  |

Generations run one at a time (each one already uses every core): later ones wait in a queue. Their progress streams to the page (`GET /api/jobs/{id}/events`); `GET /api/jobs` lists the recent jobs (kept in the index, so also after a restart), so a reloaded page picks up the ones still running, and `DELETE /api/jobs/{id}` cancels one: a queued job never starts, a running one stops when its next stage begins (or, during the export, once the files are written) and leaves nothing on disk: each body is written into a hidden `systems/<id>/bodies/.tmp-<body>/` directory renamed into place once complete, removed if the job is cancelled or fails, and swept at startup if the server was killed mid-export; the bodies a cancelled job finished stay generated. A system cannot be deleted while one of its bodies is being generated (409). Zooming in loads [detail tiles](#detail-tiles--deep-zoom) rendered on demand by the server (`GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/tiles/{z}/{x}/{y}?rev=&render=`, and a body's crater epochs at `…/bodies/{body}/epoch/…`; `size=<W>x<H>` on these, the drift previews and the hex grids reads that resolution of the body, the displayed one by default, 404 when it is not generated), cached by the browser for good since the URL names the body's revision and the renderer version (`RENDER_VERSION` in `src/detail.rs`, mirrored by `TILE_RENDER_VERSION` in the viewer and checked by a test): bump both whenever tile colours or shading change, or browsers keep tiles of the previous renderer. A body generated before a renderer change keeps its old `world.png` under new tiles until it is regenerated. The server keeps the most recently used worlds in memory within `MAPGEN_TILE_CACHE_MB` (default 1024 MiB; a world takes about 23 bytes per pixel, 0.8 GB at 8192 × 4096, and the last one used is always kept). It loads one world at a time and evicts the least recently used ones before loading, so the budget holds during the load too. A generation waits for a load in progress to finish before it starts. While a generation runs, it frees them and answers tile and hex grid requests with 503 and `Retry-After` (the viewer retries both), so they do not add to the generation peak; tiles render at most four at a time, and a request the viewer abandons before its turn is dropped. The library shows each system's first generated planet's `thumb.png` (`GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/thumb?rev=`, cached for good like the tiles); for a body without one, the server makes it on the first request from `world.png`, decoded one row at a time (about 4 MB and 0.4 s at 8192 × 4096, two at a time, during generations too), and writes it into the body's directory. The server refuses to draw a system that exists already (409, `system_exists`) unless the request asks to replace it (`POST /api/systems?replace=true`): the new system is written into a temporary directory and swapped in (the old one renamed to `systems/.old-<id>/`, the new one renamed into place, then the old one removed; the startup sweep puts the old one back if the server was killed between the two renames), and a system being generated cannot be replaced (409). Maps are limited to `MAPGEN_MAX_PIXELS` pixels and 8192 pixels per side. The server defaults to 8192 × 4096, the largest of the 2:1 sizes the form offers (1024 × 512, 2048 × 1024, 4096 × 2048 and 8192 × 4096); `compose.yaml` sets the same limit for the 6 GB Colima VM of the workstation setup. A generation peaks at about 0.16 GiB at 1920 × 1080, 0.55 GiB at 4096 × 2048 and 2.0 GiB at 8192 × 4096 (about 70 bytes per pixel, measured in Docker; about 2 s, 8 s and 40 s on 6 cores): keep the limit under the memory the container can use, or the server is killed mid-job. With Colima's default 2 GB VM, lower it (`MAPGEN_MAX_PIXELS: "8388608"`, 4096 × 2048) or give the VM more memory (`colima stop && colima start --cpu 6 --memory 6`). The web app skips the diagnostic maps, which would add about 10 bytes per pixel, and `world.svg`: neither is written unless asked. `MAPGEN_NOISE_MAPS=true` makes the server write the `noise_maps/` of every body it generates (a debug option, off by default, with the memory above); the world card's "Export SVG" action draws the SVG of a body on request (`GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/world.svg?size=<W>x<H>`, a download, from the stored body or else regenerated from its seed and request, never stored). Bodies generated before keep the files they have. Between generations the server holds little more than the worlds loaded for tiles: the Docker image limits glibc to two malloc arenas (`MALLOC_ARENA_MAX=2`), and the server returns freed memory to the system after each generation and cache eviction (`malloc_trim`); after a 4096 × 2048 generation it holds about 50 MB instead of 170–400 MB. A failed API call answers `{"error": "..."}` with an English message; the errors a user can fix also carry `detail`, the reason as data (`{"code": "out_of_range", "field": "mass_earth", "min": 0.01, "max": 4131}`; the codes are those of `ErrorDetail` in `crates/server/src/api.rs`), which the web app words in its own language. System files (`/systems/...`) and the page are sent with `Cache-Control: no-cache`, so browsers revalidate them (a system id is reused when a system is deleted and drawn again), while the content-hashed bundles under `/assets/` are cached for a year; JSON, JavaScript, CSS and SVG answers above 1 KiB are gzipped when the browser accepts it, PNG images are sent as they are.

Bodies are named after the IAU's conventions for exoplanets (`mapgen::naming`). The star carries a catalogue designation of the fictional *Map Generator Survey*, `MGS` and five digits drawn from the seed and the spectral type. The planets take the star's designation and a lowercase letter by their distance to the star, from `b` (`MGS 46433 c` between the giants `b` and `d`). Real letters follow the order of discovery; here every planet is known, so they follow the orbits. Moons take their planet's name and a Roman numeral, from the closest (`MGS 46433 c II`); a world asked for as the moon of a giant is numbered among the giant's moons (`MGS 59826 b I`). `PUT /api/systems/{id}/names` gives the star and any body names of your own (`{"names": {"c": "Gaia", "c-1": "Séléné", "star": "Helia"}}`, 1 to 60 characters each, 422 otherwise). The request replaces every name given before: one left out goes back to its designation, and none at all removes the file. The names are kept in the index and in the system's `names.json`, by body id, and come with the system (`GET /api/systems/{id}`) and in the library (`GET /api/systems`). A system replaced (`?replace=true`) keeps the names that still match one of its bodies, in `names.json` and in the index, as the command line does.

**Eviction of unopened bodies.** The disk a generated body takes (up to GBs at 8192 × 4096) is taken back when nobody opens it: a body not opened for 14 days loses its generated files, all of `bodies/<body>/sizes/*`, the original resolution included, and is generated again with the current generator when its view is opened. The server runs a sweep at startup and every 6 hours and logs each one (bodies evicted, MiB freed). What stays: `system.json` (the body is then `generated: false`, listed as "not generated" in the library and the system view), the index rows (seed and parameters, displayed resolution, last opened date, names, pins) and `thumb.png`, so cards look the same. Opening the view of an evicted body (never a listing, a card or a thumbnail) queues its generation at once, at the resolution it was displayed at when the index remembers one, else at its original; `GET /api/systems/{id}` lists such bodies in `evicted`. A body counts from its last opened date (set when its own view requests its files, tiles, layers or resolutions, at most once a minute), or from when it was generated if that is later, and at most from when the index first saw it, so a rebuilt index (new layout, lost `db/`, restored tree) never evicts every old body at its next start. Only a body's own view opens it: the textures of other bodies in the sky, the star view's markers and the host planet of a moon are fetched with `?peek=1` and are not counted. The age is checked again under the lock just before each eviction, so a body opened since the sweep listed it stays. A body is never evicted while a job of its system is queued or running (the sweep leaves it for the next one), and deleting or replacing a system holds the same lock. A value of `MAPGEN_EVICT_AFTER_DAYS` that is not a number of days (`-1`, `off`) turns eviction off, with a warning. The index records the generator that made each body as `<crate version>+<git commit>` (the build id: `GIT_SHA` from the environment, else `git rev-parse HEAD` at build time; the Docker build gets it as the build argument `GIT_SHA`, which `mise run up` sets from the checkout; a build with neither records the bare crate version, and then only crate versions are compared): when a regenerated body was made by another build, `POST …/generate` answers `regenerated` (each evicted body of the family the job makes again, with the recorded and the current version and format revision) and the viewer tells the user once per body that the world may look different. Making evicted bodies again does not move their system in the library (`system.json` keeps its date); a body never generated before dates it as ever. The body opened comes back at the resolution it was displayed at; the moons, giant and siblings made by the same job come back at their original resolution only. Only the index knows the generator of an evicted body: if it is rebuilt from the tree, an evicted body is simply "not generated".

| Variable (server)         | Effect                                                                                                    | Default |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| `MAPGEN_EVICT_AFTER_DAYS` | Days a body can stay unopened before its generated files are deleted; `0` (or a value that is not a number of days) turns eviction off | `14`    |
| `MAPGEN_SYSTEMS_DIR`      | Drawn systems and their bodies                                                                            | `systems` |
| `MAPGEN_DB`               | The index (SQLite)                                                                                        | `db/mapgen.sqlite` |
| `MAPGEN_ARCHETYPES_DIR`   | Extra planet archetypes (`*.toml`); a missing directory means the built-in ones only                      | `archetypes` |
| `MAPGEN_MAX_PIXELS`       | Largest map accepted (width × height)                                                                     | `33554432` |
| `MAPGEN_TILE_CACHE_MB`    | Memory for the worlds loaded for tiles (MiB)                                                              | `1024`  |
| `MAPGEN_NOISE_MAPS`       | `true` writes the diagnostic `noise_maps/` of every body generated                                        | off     |

**Archetypes in the API.** `GET /api/archetypes` lists what a request can name as `planet`: the 20 built-in archetypes (`built_in: true`; the seven moon archetypes carry their `moon` table and are asked in a whole system only) and every `.toml` file under `MAPGEN_ARCHETYPES_DIR`, each with its `spec` (the value to send), `name`, `description`, `inputs` and `orbit` as the file sets them, its `locales`, and `giant`: whether its planet renders as a giant, as the model derives it for the archetype asked alone at seed 0 (the form then offers a giant's mass and hides the moons, drift and giant neighbours). `moon` tells a moon archetype and `moon_host` the giant archetype it orbits. A file whose `name` is already taken (a built-in's, or an earlier file's) is skipped with a warning: a name names one archetype. `GET /api/form` gives the form's limits: `archetypes` (the planets and giants a whole system can be asked for) and `moon_archetypes` (the moons it can be asked for), `inputs` (the limits of the model inputs a request can override: mass, core share, water in Earth oceans, envelope share, CO₂, N₂, radiogenic abundance, axial tilt), the eccentricity, moon count and orbit limits, and the map sizes. A planet that no archetype of the catalog names is refused with `400` and `detail.code = "unknown_archetype"`; a whole system takes built-in archetypes only. `GET /api/presets` is gone. **The generation form** offers the archetypes (or a planet drawn from its star; the moon archetypes are not offered as a single world: the planets step of a system has a "Moons" group under the planets, with the same counts, and a moon whose giant is not asked adds that giant to the count against the 15 planets; a moon's card shows its air, the ocean under its ice and, for a small moon, its bulk) and, for a planet, its star, orbit, axial tilt, moons and giants; the model inputs (mass, water, core share…) are not in the form yet, only in a request or the CLI. A varied, rerolled or drifted world sends its archetype (a world asked as none opens the form on "drawn from the star") and pins the inputs it formed with (`formation` of its `world.json`), so it keeps its planet under a new seed or epoch while the form keeps its planet; the form remembers no model input (not a giant's mass); a form remembered from before the archetypes falls back to `earth-like` and drops the knobs it held. The index moved to schema 5: an index of an older schema is set aside as `mapgen.sqlite.layout-<n>-…` and rebuilt from the tree (pins, job history and last opened dates start over).

**Systems** (see [docs/system-spec.md](docs/system-spec.md)): the server keeps drawn systems under `MAPGEN_SYSTEMS_DIR` (default `systems/`), one directory each with its `system.json`, the names given to its bodies (`names.json`, by body id, the star included) and a directory per generated body (`bodies/<body>/`, laid out as a world). `POST /api/systems` draws one from a `SystemRequest` (`{"system": {...}}`, a whole system; `{"around": {...}}`, a system around the planet a world request asks for, which is queued at once; `"alone": true` in it makes a lone planet, whose id has `-lone-` before the seed), `GET /api/systems` lists them (each with its star's light colour, its habitable zone and every body's semi-major axis, eccentricity and longitude of periapsis, for the library's orrery, and whether it is a habitable world, `habitable`: a planet whose model has liquid water on its surface) and `GET /api/systems/{id}` gives one. `PUT /api/systems/{id}/names` replaces the names given and `PUT /api/systems/{id}/name` (`{"name": "Sol"}`, null or blank to clear) sets only the system's own name, and `PUT /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/name` (same body) one body's name (the web app names bodies this way; `PUT /names` stays for bulk use), the one given to its star (key `star`), merging it into the names already there so that a body named meanwhile is kept (clearing it brings the designation back), and `PUT` / `DELETE /api/systems/{id}/pin` pin or unpin a system in the main menu (both idempotent, both answer the system's summary, whose `pinned` flag `GET /api/systems` carries too). `POST /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/generate` queues a body and its neighbourhood (a planet and its moons; a moon, its planet and its siblings) as one job, a system generating one job at a time. Its progress events name the body being made and its place in the job (`body`, `body_step`, `bodies`), and a cancelled job keeps the bodies done before it. Each generated body has its own revision and its tiles, epochs, thumbnail and hex grid under `/api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/…`.

**Varying a giant.** A giant asked as no archetype, such as a giant of a drawn system, opens the form on "drawn from the star" with a giant's fields: its mass offered, its moons, drift and giant neighbours hidden, as long as the request keeps its inputs. The form reads that it is a giant from its `world.json` (`giant`), since no archetype says so.

**Export of a system.** The app keeps no backup of its own; it exports instead. `GET /api/systems/{id}/export` answers `<system-id>.zip` (404 for an unknown system, 410 for one an older generator made), and the **Export** button (⤓) on a system's card or row in the main menu downloads it. The archive holds `<system-id>/system.json` and, for each generated body, its PNG images: `bodies/<body>/thumb.png` and `bodies/<body>/sizes/<W>x<H>/world.png` and `legend.png` for every resolution on disk. Tiles, raw data, SVG and noise maps are left out; they are rebuilt from the seed. `system.json` (schema 1) holds:

```json
{
  "schema": 1,
  "exported_at": "2026-11-09T14:03:21Z",
  "generator": { "version": "0.1.0", "system_format_version": 5 },
  "id": "mgs-48213-42",
  "seed": 42,
  "request": { "system": { "star": "K1V", "planets": { "mars-like": 3 }, "seed": null } },
  "create": { "system": { "star": "K1V", "planets": { "mars-like": 3 }, "seed": 42 } },
  "star": { "designation": "MGS 48213", "type": "K1V", "name": "Helia" },
  "bodies": [
    {
      "id": "b", "designation": "MGS 48213 b", "name": "Gaia", "kind": "planet",
      "type": "rocky", "parent": null, "seed": 1441805244,
      "request": { "...": "the body's WorldParams, as system.json stores them" },
      "generated": true,
      "made_with": { "version": "0.1.0", "format_version": 20 },
      "files": ["bodies/b/thumb.png", "bodies/b/sizes/2048x1024/world.png", "bodies/b/sizes/2048x1024/legend.png"]
    }
  ]
}
```

`request` is the system's request as stored (a seed left to chance is `null` or absent) and `create` the body of the `POST /api/systems` that draws the system again: the same id, system seed, bodies and body seeds. For a whole system (`{"system": …}`) it is `request` with the seed filled in. For a system around a planet (`{"around": …}`) it also pins the focus body's resolved parameters: what the request left open (the star, the orbit, the eccentricity, tilt, metallicity, moons, giants, host and size, and the inputs the planet formed with, its `formation`) is drawn by a random generator, not by the seed, so `create` sets each of them to the focus body's value, read from its parameters and its model (`PlanetModel::formation`) in `system.json`, as the CLI's "Reproduce with" line does (`WorldRequest::pinned`). A value the request asked for stays as asked: a position in the habitable zone, not the orbit it gave. A body never generated, or whose files are gone, is listed with `generated: false`, `made_with: null` and no `files`. `made_with.version` is `null` for a body found in the tree and not generated by this server. The pin and the displayed sizes are not exported. There is no import; the export is the copy to keep (`docs/analysis/15-storage.md`, decision 6).

**Resolutions** of a body: a generated body can have other map sizes than the one it was generated at (its *original*), each in `bodies/<body>/sizes/<W>x<H>/`. `GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/sizes` lists them (`BodySizes`: `original`, `displayed`, `present` with each one's revision, `pending` with each one's job, the `presets` that can be generated, `max_size` and `max_pixels`; 404 while the body is not generated). `POST …/sizes/{W}x{H}` queues one of the offered 2:1 sizes (1024x512 to 8192x4096; 400 `size_not_offered` otherwise, 400 `too_many_pixels` beyond `MAPGEN_MAX_PIXELS`) as a job like any other (the server remembers the size as the displayed one when the job is done, whether or not a tab follows it; `GenerateStarted`; `job_id` null when that size is on disk, the same job when it is queued already; `GET /api/jobs` gives its `size`). `DELETE …/sizes/{W}x{H}` deletes an added size (204), its cached world and drift previews; never the original (409 `original_size`), nor a size being generated (409 `being_generated`), 404 `size_not_generated` when absent. `PUT …/displayed-size` (`{"width": 2048, "height": 1024}`, 204; 404 `size_not_generated` when absent) remembers the size the body is shown at, in the index, for every browser: tiles, epochs, drift previews and hex grids without `size=` read it (the original when it is not on disk any more), and deleting it brings the original back. A system cannot be deleted or replaced while one of its sizes is being generated (409). In the web app, the **Resolution** switch of the planet view's icon bar shows these sizes as chips under the planet: a click shows a size on disk, generates one that is not, and the `×` of an added size deletes it (see [web/README.md](web/README.md)).

For development with hot reload, see [web/README.md](web/README.md) (`mise run dev`).

### Archetypes and looks

An archetype is a named set of inputs of the planet model, not a type of planet: it names a mass, a composition and an orbit, and what the planet becomes (its class, its air, its oceans, its life) is derived from them (see **planet** above). The twenty built-in archetypes (thirteen planets, seven moons) live in `archetypes/` and are embedded in the binary; any TOML file of the same format can be given to `--planet`. The web app lists the built-in ones.

An archetype file has an `[inputs]` table and an optional `[orbit]` table. Every input and orbit field except `mass_earth` is optional, and one left out is drawn from the formation context like that of a planet nobody asked for. A number is a fixed value and a `[min, max]` pair a range the draw picks from.

| Table      | Field                | Unit and meaning                                                                         |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `[inputs]` | `mass_earth`         | Earth masses (required)                                                                  |
|            | `core_mass_fraction` | iron share of the refractory mass                                                        |
|            | `water_oceans`       | water and ices, in Earth oceans (1.4e21 kg each)                                         |
|            | `envelope_fraction`  | hydrogen and helium share of the mass                                                    |
|            | `co2`, `n2`, `ch4`   | factors on the formation rule of the gas, 1 typical (`ch4`: the methane its ices carry)  |
|            | `radiogenic`         | radiogenic abundance, Earth's = 1                                                        |
|            | `life`               | `true`: life arises wherever the world allows it; `false`: never                         |
| `[orbit]`  | `star`               | spectral type (`G2V`), used when the archetype is asked alone                            |
|            | `insolation`         | light received, Earth's = 1 (1361 W/m²); or `period_days`, not both (a hot Jupiter)      |
|            | `eccentricity`       | of the orbit                                                                             |
|            | `axial_tilt`         | degrees                                                                                  |

`name` (lowercase letters and dashes), `description` and `[locales.<language>]` (`name` and `description` in another language, two or three lowercase letters; the web app shows them when its interface is in that language) complete the file. Unknown fields are errors. The Solar System archetypes take the masses, core shares and orbits of `crates/mapgen/src/planet/reference.rs`, with ranges of about ±10 % on mass and insolation. `mars-like` departs from them on two inputs so that it renders as Mars does: 0.002 to 0.008 oceans of water (Mars's polar deposits and ground ice, about 0.004, stay under the 0.01 that shows as ice, so its look is `barren`) and CO₂ and N₂ factors of 0.3 (Mars lost most of its early air, which the formation rule does not model), which give about its 6 mbar at the Sun's age on the median of the seeds; single worlds spread from about 0.002 to 0.02 bar with the mass (escape takes more from a lighter one) and the age of the star (a young one has lost less). The exoplanet kinds name a range typical of the kind; the four small ones (`super-earth`, `mini-neptune`, `ocean-world`, `lava-world`) keep a near-circular orbit (`eccentricity = [0, 0.05]`), and asked alone they orbit a star of their own rather than a drawn one: `lava-world` and `mini-neptune` a K dwarf (`K0V`, `K2V`), as 55 Cnc e, CoRoT-7 b and K2-141 b do, so that a red dwarf does not pull them inside the closest orbit allowed; `ocean-world` a `G2V`, whose habitable zone keeps Earth's light (0.9 to 1.1) inside its inner edge (1.107, Kopparapu et al. 2014), where a cooler star's would run the ocean away. 

**Moon archetypes.** `titan-like`, `europa-like`, `ganymede-like`, `callisto-like`, `io-like`, `triton-like` and `enceladus-like` are giants' moons: in place of `[orbit]` they have a `[moon]` table, and `[inputs] mass_earth` is the moon's (1e-6 to 0.1 Earth masses). They are asked for in a whole system only (`--system titan-like=1`), never as a world on their own. A whole system that asks for a moon and names no star takes the star its host's archetype names (`G2V` for the four giants; the first moon's host, in name order), so that the moon forms as its archetype describes it; a star named with `--star` is honoured, and the moon then comes out as the model says (an asked moon around a red dwarf may lose its air).

| Table    | Field                   | Unit and meaning                                                                  |
| -------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `[moon]` | `host`                  | the built-in giant archetype it orbits (`saturn-like`)                            |
|          | `origin`                | `"disk"`: grown in its giant's disk; `"captured"`: a passing body (an ice giant's) |
|          | `semi_major_axis_radii` | its orbit, in its giant's radii                                                   |
|          | `eccentricity`          | of its orbit round its giant                                                      |
|          | `ice_fraction`          | share of its mass in water and ices, the rest rock                                |

A moon asked for rides the first giant of its host's archetype in the same request that can carry it: one whose moon draw could have made it (its disk holds the moon's mass, 1 to 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ of a gas giant's mass, 1 to 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ of an ice giant's; an icy moon orbits beyond the disk's ice line; the orbit lies outside the Roche limit and within a third of the Hill sphere, half for a captured moon; a giant its star despins keeps it), with the other moons it carries kept apart. `saturn-like=1,titan-like=1` gives one Saturn with its Titan; `neptune-like=1,titan-like=1` gives a Neptune and a Saturn, since a Titan is over a Neptune's disk. Otherwise the request adds a giant of the host's archetype for it, counted among the 15 planets, placed no nearer its star than where the moon can orbit; a star that leaves no such place refuses the request (`type_impossible`, a Titan round an M9.5 dwarf, whose Saturn's Hill sphere is too small). The moon is drawn once in its archetype's ranges and stored on its giant (`asked_moons`); the giant's other moons are drawn around it, sharing what is left of the disk. It then goes through the model as every round moon does, with its archetype's inputs: an archetype sets causes, not the outcome. Over seeds 1 to 10, with no star named (its Saturn's G2V), every Titan is a hydrocarbon world with methane lakes and haze: `titan-like` sets the methane its ices carry (`ch4 = [1.0, 2.0]`, an interior reservoir that outlasts photolysis, Tobie et al. 2006, Lorenz et al. 2008), the nitrogen dissolved in its methane keeps it liquid below pure methane's 90.67 K and any air above methane's triple-point pressure (0.117 bar) holds the liquid (see [docs/science/atmospheres.md](docs/science/atmospheres.md)); round an old K dwarf named it can still lose its methane, round an M dwarf its whole air; Europa with an ocean under its ice, Io a dry heat-pipe world, Ganymede and Callisto airless, Triton under a thin N₂ air; Enceladus has no ocean yet (issue #463).

A copy-pasteable example, `archetypes/mars-like.toml`:

```toml
name = "mars-like"
description = "Mars-like: a small cold desert under a thin air"

[locales.fr]
name = "Martienne"
description = "Un petit désert froid sous un air ténu"

[inputs]
mass_earth = [0.096, 0.118]
core_mass_fraction = 0.24
water_oceans = [0.002, 0.008]
envelope_fraction = 0.0
co2 = 0.3
n2 = 0.3

[orbit]
star = "G2V"
insolation = [0.39, 0.47]
eccentricity = 0.093
axial_tilt = 25.19
```

The request can set any input after the archetype's draw (`--mass`, `--water`, `--core-fraction`, `--envelope`, `--co2`, `--n2`, `--radiogenic`, `--life`, see [Options](#options)).

**Looks.** Until issue #424 a world is still drawn by a *look*, the internal rule set of `looks/*.toml` (biome rules and remap, climate shift, sky, geology, and for a giant its cloud bands): `planet::interim` picks one for the model of each planet (`base_of`), the moon draw gives the `rocky-moon` and `icy-moon` looks of `looks/moons/` to the moons, and nothing in a request or an archetype names one. They are embedded in the binary, not meant to be edited, and #424 deletes them when the model alone draws a planet. The eight looks of planets:

| Look          | Renders                                           | Exclusive biomes                                                                                |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `terran`      | liquid water: the full biome spectrum             | none                                                                                            |
| `volcanic`    | lava worlds and heat-pipe regimes                 | `MagmaSea`, `ScorchedWaste`                                                                     |
| `frozen`      | ice worlds and liquid hydrocarbons                | `FrozenOcean`, `FrozenLake`, `GlacialPlain` (`HydrocarbonSea`, `HydrocarbonLake` for methane)   |
| `caustic`     | runaway greenhouses                               | `AcidSea`, `DeepAcidSea`, `CausticLake`, `AcidRiver`, `AcidShore`, `ToxicSwamp`, `AcidFlatland` |
| `barren`      | dry rock                                          | `RockyWaste`, `DustPlain`                                                                       |
| `gas-giant`   | gas giants: Jupiter or Saturn, bands and rings    | `GiantZone`, `GiantBelt`, `GiantStorm`, `GiantPolar`                                            |
| `ice-giant`   | ice giants and mini-Neptunes, blue methane        | the same                                                                                        |
| `hot-jupiter` | hot Jupiters: days from their star, dark, locked  | the same                                                                                        |

A look file has no name and no defaults: its tables are `[climate]` (`temperature` °C, `moisture`, `volcanic`, `internal_heat` K, added to every pixel before biome selection), `[rules]` (any field of `BiomeRules` in `src/biome.rs`), `[atmosphere]` (`rayleigh`, `haze`, `haze_colour`, `greenhouse`, `clouds`, `cloud_cover`, see `src/atmosphere.rs`), `[geology]` (`regime`, `wet_until`, `maria`, see `src/geology.rs`), `[giant]` (`kind` and `rings`, see **giant worlds** above; the mass and orbit are the model's and the archetype's) and `[remap]` (`target = [biomes replaced by it]`, run after biome selection; a biome under two targets is an error). The model sets over them what it derives: the temperature shift, the internal heat, the regime, the sky, on a world whose liquid is methane and ethane its dark seas and lakes (`HydrocarbonSea`, `HydrocarbonLake`, in place of the `frozen` look's frozen water) and, without life, the lifeless remap.

`raw_data/world.json` stores `class` (the class key, a round moon's included, or `rocky-moon` / `icy-moon` for a small moon), `archetype` (the archetype asked for, display only; left out for a planet nobody named), `model` (the planet model, a round moon's included; left out for a small moon), `moon` (true for a moon of a system's planet, left out otherwise), `formation` (the inputs a request pins to remake the planet; left out for a moon, whose model comes from how it formed around its planet) and the full `look`, so a world can be regenerated even if the look files change.

### Output

Each run writes a system into `systems/<id>/` (`<id>`: the star's designation and the seed, lowercase, `mgs-36196-42`): its `system.json` (the star and every body, see `src/system.rs` and `docs/system-spec.md`) and one directory per body generated, under `bodies/<body>/` (`c`, `c-1`), with the layout of a world below. A system drawn again into the same directory replaces it, keeping the names given in the web app (`names.json`). Each planet of `system.json` stores its physical model (`model`) and is typed by its class (`rocky`, `super-earth`, `mini-neptune`, `gas-giant`…), not by a preset, and its world is generated from that model (its size, air, sky, regime and volcanism when the system is drawn; the sea level its oceans fill and its mean temperature when the world is generated, stored in its `world.json`; see `docs/system-spec.md`); the planet a system was drawn around keeps the archetype it was asked by (`params.archetype`, display only), a round moon stores its own model and is typed by its class, and a smaller moon keeps `rocky-moon` or `icy-moon`. A system of an older layout (`system.json` format version 1, its bodies' files not in `sizes/`; version 2, its planets typed by preset and without a model) is refused: delete it (`mise run clean-systems`) and draw it again.

#### Soft biome borders

`world.png` and the viewer's detail tiles use a **surface palette** (`surface_color` in `src/biome.rs`): colours as seen from orbit, with dark olive vegetation, pale tan deserts and dark open water, while exotic biomes keep a muted hue of their own. The **classification palette** (`biome_color`), saturated and far apart, stays on `world.svg` (seas included, with no per-pixel variation so its run-length encoding stays compact), the hex grid and the `color` of the biome table. `legend.png` shows both swatches for each biome: surface first, classification second.

Land colours vary within a biome: two smooth noise fields on the sphere (wavelengths from 400 km down to 50 km, a noise stream of their own) shift the brightness by up to 18 % and the warmth, red against blue, by up to 8 %, so large forests and deserts are no longer flat plates. The fields depend on the position alone, so the base map and the detail tiles agree; lava varies too, so no step appears through the soft border around it. This is rendering only.

`world.png` draws biome borders as ecotones. Each land pixel takes the mean colour of the biomes its inputs select when nudged by 128 small offsets (a 4D Gaussian, σ 0.9 °C on temperature, 0.05 on moisture, 0.003 on the highland line and 0.004 on the volcanic zone): far from any threshold the colour is flat (8 probes tell, so most pixels skip the sampling), near one the colours mix, averaged in linear light (an sRGB average darkens every border). The width of a transition is set in input units, not pixels, so it suits any zoom level; mountain, snow and volcanic borders soften too, volcanoes less than climate borders. The same offsets serve every pixel, so transitions show neither grain nor terraces. Coastlines, shores, rivers and lakes keep their exact inputs and stay crisp. This is rendering only: `raw_data/biomes.png` and `world.svg` keep crisp, flat classes.

Clear liquids take their colour from their depth (Beer-Lambert absorption in linear light): the bed shows through shallow water and fades with depth, faster in the channels the liquid absorbs most, towards the colour of deep water. Water absorbs red first, so shelves turn turquoise and the open sea dark blue past the shelf break; acid absorbs blue first, so its shallows turn yellow-green. The colour depends on depth alone, so there is no step where Ocean becomes Deep Ocean. A lake's depth is measured from its own level (its highest pixel), not from the sea, and counts four times, as lakes are far shallower than seas. Frozen seas and lakes whiten into fast ice along the shore (up to 25 % over the last `0.05` elevation units), magma seas darken into a cooled crust, and rivers stay flat. Every sea also carries a pattern of its own kind, mixed in linear light and the same on the base map and the detail tiles: water and acid show swirls drawn by currents (a domain-warped noise from 900 km down to 56 km; teal plankton up to 30 % on water, sulphur yellow up to 14 % on acid), ice shows bluish leads of open water between floes (crests of a ridged noise, 700 km), magma shows rafts of dark cooled crust and yellow-hot gaps (1200 km down to 75 km, up to 60 % crust and 30 % hot colour); all seas vary in brightness by up to 8 %. Rivers stay flat. Contour lines are drawn under water only, as bathymetry: on land the hillshade shows the relief, and contours drew rings that read as altitude bands. The hillshade follows a soft curve (`1 + 0.4 × tanh((lit − 1) / 0.4)`, relative to flat ground) rather than a hard clamp, so steep slopes stay distinct; detail tiles use the same curve.

#### Raw data

`raw_data/` holds one lossless PNG per layer, pixel-aligned with `world.png` (row 0 = north pole, column 0 = longitude 0), and a `world.json` describing how to decode them.

| File                  | Encoding         | Content                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `elevation.png`       | 16-bit grey      | Terrain height in `[-1, 1]`, before the sea-level shift (biomes use `elevation − sea_level`)                                                                                                                                                    |
| `elevation_base.png`  | 16-bit grey      | The elevation without the impact craters younger than the wet era (those drawn after erosion), same range: the surface the time slider will draw them on (format version 7 and later)                                                           |
| `moisture.png`        | 16-bit grey      | Moisture in `[-1, 1]` (base-2 log of the annual rain, 0 = 1 000 mm), planet-type offset included                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| `temperature.png`     | 16-bit grey      | Annual mean temperature in °C (−200 to 600), planet-type offset included                                                                                                                                                                        |
| `flow.png`            | 16-bit grey, log | River flow: `log10(1 + km²)` in `[0, 8]`, the wet-equivalent area draining through the pixel                                                                                                                                                    |
| `volcanic_zone.png`   | 16-bit grey      | Volcanic zone in `[0, 1]` as biome selection sees it (planet shift included)                                                                                                                                                                    |
| `coast_distance.png`  | 16-bit grey      | Distance to the nearest open water in `[0, 2000]` km (0 on water, capped)                                                                                                                                                                       |
| `highland_offset.png` | 16-bit grey      | Local shift of the mountain line in `[-1, 1]`: a pixel is highland when `elevation − sea_level + offset` exceeds the look's `highland`; on worlds without plates it includes the thick crust's Airy step, negated                                                                                                        |
| `floodplain.png`      | 16-bit grey      | Floodplain weight in `[0, 1]`: 1 on the flat floor of a river valley, 0 away from rivers                                                                                                                                                        |
| `biomes.png`          | 8-bit grey       | Biome id of each pixel                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| `clouds.png`          | 8-bit grey       | Cloud cover in `[0, 1]` (`pixel / 255`), from the climate and the cloud kind; absent when the planet has no clouds                                                                                                                              |
| `albedo.png`          | 8-bit grey       | Brightness shift of the ground in `[-1, 1]` (`pixel / 255 × 2 − 1`, 0 at 128): the bright ejecta and rays of fresh impact craters (zero on worlds with air enough for liquid water) and the dark lava of the maria (format version 8 and later) |
| `colour.png`          | 8-bit RGB        | The render of `world.png` without its hillshade, reference lines and depth contours: the viewer draws the lines itself, can hide them, and lights the relief with `normal.png` |
| `normal.png`          | 8-bit RGB        | Relief normals in the map's tangent frame (east, north, up), `channel = (n + 1) / 2 × 255`, from the hillshade's slopes and exaggeration: flat `(128, 128, 255)` on water and on giants, softened on lava (format version 16) |
| `colour_small.png`, `normal_small.png` | 8-bit RGB | The same at 512 pixels of width at most (the normals averaged as vectors), for the small planets of the star view |

A 16-bit pixel `p` decodes to `min + p / 65535 × (max − min)`, with `min` and `max` given per layer in `world.json`. For `flow` (encoding `gray16-log10`) this gives `log10(1 + km²)`: the area is `10^value − 1`. The precision is about `3 × 10⁻⁵` of the range (0.012 °C for temperature).

  "look": { "...": "..." },

The example is abridged. Biome ids are stable: new biomes only get appended. `format_version` changes when the layout of `raw_data/` changes: version 2 added `volcanic_zone`, `coast_distance` and `highland_offset`, so that every input of biome selection is exported, and `rivers.json`; version 3 added `floodplain`; version 4 stores temperatures in °C instead of the former `[0, 1]` scale; version 5 adds `clouds`; version 6 adds `surface`; version 7 adds `elevation_base`, and its worlds have impact craters; version 8 adds `albedo`; version 9 adds `moons` (and the `moons/` directory) and, on irregular moons, `shape`; version 10 adds the star's `metallicity` and `giants`; version 11 reads the biomes with the seasonal cycle and stores the moisture as the log of the annual rain; version 12 adds the swing of a long solar day to the seasons; version 14 drops the code that re-derived older worlds; version 17 generates a planet's world from its derived state (the planet model); version 18 stores `class`, `archetype`, `model` and `look` instead of `planet_type` and `preset`. Version 19 changes the relief: the crater transition follows the strength of the crust, the impact flux and crater scale follow the orbit, and the crust floats by its thickness. Version 20 types a round moon by its model's class, stores its model, and adds `moon` (with the fifth `system_format_version`). The generator reads only the current format (`MIN_FORMAT_VERSION` in `crates/mapgen/src/export/raw_data.rs`): a world of an older version is refused: the tiles, epochs and hex grids answer 410 Gone with a message asking to delete it (`mise run clean-systems`) and draw it again, and the viewer shows that message instead of requesting them; a `system.json` that no longer parses (systems before format 11) answers 410 too. The server keeps running and still lists them. A system whose `system.json` is not of the current `system_format_version` is listed with `outdated: true` in `GET /api/systems` (its id, seed, star and date when they parse, no planets): the main menu shows it with the label "Outdated: made by an older generator" and a delete button only, no link to open it, no rename, pin or export. Nothing deletes it but the user. A world is not migrated, because the algorithms that made it are not kept.

`rivers.json` holds the drawn river polylines (version 2 and later; its file name is in `world.json` under `rivers`):

```json
{
  "version": 3,
  "width_law": { "w0_ref_px": 0.9, "q0_km2": 120000, "exponent": 0.5, "max_ref_px": 4, "reference_width_px": 1920, "ink": 0.5, "raster_exponent": 0.6666667, "real_km_per_sqrt_km2": 0.002 },
  "rivers": [
    { "id": 0, "parent": null, "mouth": "sea", "source": null, "estuary": false, "points": [[129.656, -62.275, 158147], [129.802, -62.306, 158175]] }
  ]
}
```

Each river lists `[longitude°, latitude°, flow km²]` from head to mouth; longitudes are continuous along a river, so they may leave `[0, 360)` where it crosses the seam. `mouth` is `sea`, `lake` or `river` (then `parent` is the river it joins). `source` is the river a delta distributary branches from (its first point lies on it), and `estuary` is `true` when the river widens into an estuary towards its mouth (version 3). The nominal width is `w0_ref_px × (flow / q0_km2)^exponent`, capped at `max_ref_px`, in reference pixels (1/`reference_width_px` of the circumference); on a raster `W` pixels around the planet a river is drawn `ink × nominal × (W / reference_width_px)^raster_exponent` pixels wide, and never thinner than `real_km_per_sqrt_km2 × √flow` km. Version 1 files (before `ink`) drew the nominal width. About 0.5 MB per world (250 KB gzipped).

The `mapgen` library reloads a world with `export::raw_data::load_world`; for version 1 worlds it recomputes the missing layers from the world parameters and elevation (and has no river polylines).

Reading a layer in Python:

```python
import json
from PIL import Image

meta = json.load(open("raw_data/world.json"))
layer = meta["layers"]["elevation"]
pixels = Image.open("raw_data/elevation.png")
lo, hi = layer["min"], layer["max"]
elevation = [lo + p / 65535 * (hi - lo) for p in pixels.getdata()]
```

## Calculations

The science behind some of these calculations is explained one question at a time in [docs/science/](docs/science/README.md), for readers who want to understand a topic: the theories with their equations and sources, then how the app computes it.

### Temperature — axial tilt

The sea-level temperature of each latitude comes from the annual-mean sunlight it receives: the daily-mean sunlight at the top of the atmosphere (Nadeau & McGehee 2021, eq. 45–47, after Sellers 1965), averaged over a circular orbit (an eccentric one multiplies every latitude by the same factor, see below):

```text
Q(φ, δ) = (4/π) (h0 sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ sin h0)    relative to the global mean S0/4
cos h0  = −tan φ tan δ                                   (h0 = 0 in polar night, π in polar day)
sin δ   = sin(tilt) sin λ                                (λ: place on the orbit from the spring equinox)
S(φ)    = mean of Q(φ, δ(λ)) over λ
```

With no tilt the poles get no light; at Earth's tilt the equator gets 416 W/m² and the poles 172; from a tilt of 53.9° the poles get more than the equator. `S` is then rescaled so that Earth's tilt (23.44°) gives `t = 0` at the poles and `t = 1` at the equator (kept within `[−0.5, 1.5]` for other tilts), and turned into an annual mean temperature in °C by a curve fitted to Earth's zonal mean:

```text
T = −18 + 56.28 t − 11.28 t²     (−18 °C at the poles, 12 °C at 42°, 27 °C at the equator)
```

The tilt moves the light between latitudes, not its global mean, so the rows are then shifted to keep the global mean temperature of Earth's tilt (14.5 °C): in an energy balance model whose outgoing radiation is linear in temperature, the mean temperature depends only on the mean absorbed light (Budyko 1969; North et al. 1981).

Temperature is not linear in sunlight: the tropics are flatter, heat being carried polewards, so a straight line would leave the mid-latitudes too cold. Land then cools with its height above the waterline: at the standard atmosphere's lapse rate, 6.5 °C per km (162.5 °C per elevation unit of 25 km), under 1 bar of air or more, and less under thinner air, whose ground follows its own radiation more than the air: `6.5 × (p / 1 bar)^0.378` °C per km, the power law through Earth's rate and Mars's (0.998 °C per km near the ground at 0.007 bar, NASA Glenn's Mars model; the power law between them is a calibration). It fades smoothly to none on an airless body. The sea surface does not cool. Worlds stored before `format_version` 11 keep the former 13.5 °C per unit in their detail tiles and epochs.

A large-scale anomaly is added on top: `18 °C × fbm` on land and `9 °C × fbm` on the sea (features of about 3 000 km, standard deviation about 3.6 °C on land). It stands for ocean currents, monsoons and continentality without modelling them, so climate zones shift by up to ~15° of latitude between continents instead of following parallels. It is zero-mean: the share of cold and hot climates does not change. One current is modelled apart: the **cold eastern-boundary currents** (California, Canary, Humboldt, Benguela, Western Australia). The sea with land to its east in its row, within 2 500 km, is cooled by up to 1.7 °C (full strength to 1 000 km, none at 2 500 km), in the latitudes of the trades (a Gaussian of 10° centred on 25°, in Earth degrees stretched with the wind belts). The magnitude is read in NOAA's optimum-interpolation SST (Reynolds et al. 2002, doi:10.1175/1520-0442(2002)015<1609:AIISAS>2.0.CO;2, 1991-2020 normals): west of the coasts, from 20° to 30° in both hemispheres, the sea is 1.2 to 2.0 °C colder than the mean of its latitude, within 2 000 km, and warmer beyond (1° cells, so the narrow cold tongue of the coast itself is smoothed out; the value and the shape are a calibration to that table). The moisture pass reads the cooled sea, whose air holds less vapour, so the coast behind it is drier (the Atacama, the Namib); the cooling is part of the stored temperature of the sea, not of the land. Not on a world locked to its star or without liquid water.

| Axial tilt | Effect                                                   |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0°         | Strongest equator-to-pole contrast, large ice caps       |
| 23.44°     | Earth baseline                                           |
| ~54°       | Nearly uniform sunlight across latitudes                 |
| 54°+       | Poles receive more sunlight over a year than the equator |

The atmosphere then acts on these rows. **Heat transport:** above 1 bar, dense air carries heat to the poles, so each row is pulled towards the area-weighted mean by `1 / (1 + 0.1 × (pressure − 1))` (0.45 at 13 bar, 0.1 on Venus at 92 bar); the global mean does not move, and thinner atmospheres keep Earth's contrast. **Star:** the rows are Earth's around the Sun; the host star and the orbit then move them, through the flux the planet gets relative to Earth's (`S`):

- a planet with plate tectonics and liquid water (≥ 0.01 bar) has a carbonate-silicate cycle that regulates its greenhouse: across the habitable zone it stays temperate, from +30 °C at the inner edge through Earth's climate at Earth's place (position 0.07) to −15 °C at the outer edge; beyond the zone it follows the flux like the others. A wet world with a stagnant lid has a weaker one, half the way from its unregulated temperature to the regulated one (volcanic outgassing can sustain a carbon cycle there for 1 to 5 Gyr, Foley & Smye 2018; the half is a calibration);
- any other planet follows the flux: temperatures in kelvin are multiplied by `S^(1/4)` (Mars at 1.52 AU: ×0.81; Venus at 0.72 AU: ×1.18), which also narrows the equator-to-pole difference in degrees around a dim star;
- `[climate] internal_heat` (K) adds the planet's own heat as radiated power, `T = (T⁴ + T_int⁴)^(1/4)`: volcanic worlds (285 K) stay scorching far from their star.

The Sun at 1 AU leaves the rows unchanged, so `earth-like` worlds are exactly as before.

**Tidal locking:** a planet whose tidal locking time (Gladman et al. 1996, about `166 Gyr × a⁶ / M²` for an Earth-like planet) is shorter than its star's age (the star's age, `--age`) is despun; the time goes as the planet's `m / R³` (Earth's for the formula above). On a round orbit it ends locked and always shows the same face to its star: the planets in the habitable zone of M dwarfs, and of the coolest K dwarfs. On an eccentric one it may be caught on its way down in a spin–orbit resonance, as Mercury's 3:2 (three turns every two orbits, a solar day of two years, the climate of a turning planet) or the 2:1: the odds of each rise linearly with the eccentricity, from 0 at 0.06 to 1 at 0.20 for the 3:2 ("At eccentricities lower than 0.06, the most probable state becomes the synchronous one", Ribas et al. 2016 for Proxima b; 3:2 capture is guaranteed for Mercury from e = 0.20 to 0.41, Makarov 2012), from 0.161 to 0.34 for the 2:1 (inevitable from 0.34 to 0.49, 0.23 at Mercury's 0.2056, Makarov 2012); the world's seed draws the outcome, the 2:1 first, then the 3:2, a planet no resonance catches ending locked. The 5:2 and higher are not modelled (Makarov gives no threshold for them). An archetype's `[orbit]` table sets the eccentricity (`mercury-like`: 0.206) but no spin state: the seed draws the outcome. The tides circularise orbits too, far more slowly (`τ_e = (4/63) Q' (m / M) (a / R)⁵ / n`, Goldreich & Soter 1966, about 10⁸ Gyr × a^6.5 / M^1.5 for an Earth-like planet, going as `m / R⁵`: a super-Earth of 5.5 Earth masses circularises three times faster): a drawn eccentricity is 0 only for the planets within about 0.05 AU of a red dwarf or 0.07 AU of the Sun, which end locked; the `mercury-like` archetype (e = 0.206) keeps its eccentricity, and a drawn Mercury is caught in 2:1 one time in four. A giant locks with its own tides (Jupiter's `k₂ = 0.565`, Durante et al. 2020, and `Q ≈ 5 × 10⁴` from Lainey et al. 2009), about 56 times slower than a rocky planet at the same place: hot Jupiters are locked, a giant at 0.3 AU from the Sun is not. A despun giant on an eccentric orbit is not locked: a fluid planet settles at the pseudo-synchronous rate (Hut 1981; `1 + 6e²` turns per orbit to second order, Leconte et al. 2010), locked within 5 % of one turn per orbit (e under about 0.09). Locked planets' temperature follows the angle from the point under the star, at the centre of the map (longitude 180°, on the equator), instead of the latitude: the light is `4 cos θ` times the mean on the day side and none on the night side, turned into °C like the rows (41 °C under the star, −49 °C on the night side around the Sun's light), then evened out by the atmosphere and moved by the star as above. Winds, rain and clouds keep their latitude bands. **Greenhouse:** the look's `[atmosphere] greenhouse` is added to every pixel; a world drawn from its planet model has none (0 °C), since its generation aims the mean temperature at the model's, greenhouse included. **Liquid water:** below 0.01 bar (water's triple point is 0.006 bar) moisture is −1 everywhere, so there is no rain, no river and no lake, and the model gives no erosion (it erodes with liquid water, scaled by `min(1, P / 0.01 bar)`); only a world whose envelope hides its surface, drawn with the `barren` look, keeps the look's `erosion = 1` for valleys carved when it was wetter.

The `[climate]` table of the world's look then shifts the whole temperature field (for example `+4.5 °C` on caustic worlds, `−10 °C` on `mars-like`, about −50 °C on average with its distance, whose ice thresholds are lowered to a summer of `−62 °C` to keep its caps from about 65° of latitude; frozen worlds get their cold from their distance alone). Biomes use annual mean thresholds where the annual mean decides: sea ice below −13.5 °C, boreal (tundra, taiga) below 0 °C, temperate below 12 °C and tropical above. Where the seasons decide, they use the summer and winter temperatures, with Köppen's limits (Kottek et al. 2006): ice cap, frozen rivers and highland snow need a summer below 0 °C (frost climate EF; `polar`, `highland_snow_temperature`), boreal land whose summer stays below 10 °C is tundra, too cold for trees (ET; `tundra_summer`), and land is tropical only if its winter stays at 18 °C or above (tropical climates A; `tropical_winter`): warmer land with cooler winters is temperate. Dry land comes first, by Köppen's arid criterion B (see [Moisture](#moisture--winds-and-rain-shadows)): desert and shrubland are set by the year's rain against a threshold of the temperature, not by a moisture cut-off. Without tilt there are no seasons, so every land pixel whose annual mean is below 0 °C is ice cap: a world drawn with almost no tilt (as moonless worlds can be) has large ice sheets.

**Seasons.** The climate layers are annual means, and a season-blind climate would paint the poles of a world tilted by 90° as warm and steady. Each row therefore also gets its seasonal half-range, half the difference between its summer and its winter: the yearly swing of its daily-mean sunlight (sunniest day minus darkest day, over two, from the formula above), turned into degrees by the curve's mean slope (45 °C per unit), damped by thermal inertia (an interior follows 0.45 of it, the sea, whose mixed layer stores a season's heat, 0.10; land goes from the sea's value at the coast to the interior's as `1 − exp(−d / 400 km)` with the distance `d` to the coast; calibrated on Earth's seasonal ranges, no source), by dense air like the rows, and moved by the star like the rows. At Earth's tilt land swings by about ±11 °C at 30° and ±20 °C at 60°, the sea by ±2 and ±4 °C; at 90° a land pole swings by ±57 °C. Summer is the annual mean plus the half-range, winter the mean minus it. A planet locked to its star has no seasons. A planet whose solar day lasts more than a month (the 3:2 resonance: two years; a slow rotator) adds the swing of its day: the first harmonic of the daytime light (`2 cos φ` times the global mean light at the equinox), turned into degrees the same way, damped by the same thermal inertia at the period of the solar day (`1/√(1 + (k·year/day)²)`, `k` fixed by the yearly values above, after North et al. 1981) and by a monthly mean's window (`sin x / x`, `x = π·month/day`, none for a day of a month or less), so Earth's days add nothing. Yang et al. 2014 (doi:10.1088/2041-8205/787/1/L2) is the picture: with a long day the dayside is much warmer than the nightside. The air damps that swing twice: it keeps the heat of the day for its radiative time `t_rad = c_p p / (g σ T_eq³)` (126 days for Earth; Koll & Abbot 2016, doi:10.3847/0004-637X/825/2/99), a factor `1/√(1 + (2π t_rad / day)²)`, and it carries it to the night side when the time a gravity wave takes to cross the planet is more than about 10⁻² of `t_rad` (Koll & Abbot's threshold for large day-night gradients, the logistic shape being ours); a world with no air keeps all of the swing, one under thick air none. A Mercury-like world (no air, a 176-day solar day) swings by about ±170 °C on land at the equator (Earth's slope of 45 °C per unit of light, so too little for a real Mercury); an earth-like world in the 3:2 resonance (M2V at 0.2 AU, 1 bar) by about ±10 °C (±93 °C without the air), and keeps some jungle and savanna. The half-range is not stored: it comes from the world's parameters, so `raw_data` keeps its layers (`format_version` 14). Eccentric orbits keep their annual mean (`1/√(1 − e²)`, below), and their seasons follow the distance to the star: the light of each day is scaled by `(1 + e cos ν)² / (1 − e²)^(3/2)` (Berger 1978, doi:10.1175/1520-0469(1978)035<2362:LTVODI>2.0.CO;2), `ν` the angle from the perihelion, whose longitude (`--perihelion`, drawn from the seed; Earth 283°, Mars 251°) says in which season the planet is closest (Mars gets 46 % more light at perihelion than at aphelion). With no tilt the distance alone swings the equator of a Mars-like orbit by about ±7 °C on land. In a system the bodies' longitude is not drawn but read from their orbits: the sky puts a planet on its node line at the spring equinox of its northern hemisphere, so the longitude of perihelion is the orbit's argument of periapsis (`periapsis_deg`), and a moon takes its planet's; the uneven seasons then agree with the sky of the viewer (systems stored before keep their parameters). Worlds made before the longitude was stored (no `perihelion_longitude_deg`) keep seasons that ignore the distance.

### Moisture — winds and rain shadows

Moisture comes from winds carrying ocean evaporation over land:

- **Wind belts.** Easterly trade winds blow towards the equator up to the edge of the Hadley cells (30° on Earth, NOAA), westerlies towards the poles up to the polar front (60°), polar easterlies away from the poles. The zonal wind is `−cos(3β)` in the trades, `sin(6(β − 30°))` in the westerlies and `−sin(3(β − 60°))` in the polar cells, `β` being the latitude stretched so that the planet's own edges fall on Earth's; the meridional wind, half as strong at most, is calm at the equator, the edges and the poles. The Hadley edge scales as `1 / (Ω a)` with the rotation rate and the radius (Held & Hou 1980): `30° × (day / 24 h) × (Earth's circumference / circumference)`, kept within 15–70°, the polar front halfway from it to the pole. A planet locked to its star turns once a year: its cells reach 70°. The atmosphere's mass, which also narrows them (Kaspi & Showman 2015), is left out.
- **Transport.** The lengths below (evaporation 500 km, rain 2 000 km, supply 3 000 km) are Earth's and stretch with the planet's circumference, between half and twice Earth's, so a larger world's interiors are not drier for being farther from the sea. Each row is simulated with the wind blowing east and blowing west, blended by how much the wind blows each way, so moisture stays continuous across the belts; each column is simulated with the meridional wind, from where air sinks (the Hadley edges, the poles) to where it rises (the equator, the polar fronts). The two are weighed by the strength of each component (`|v| / (|u| + 0.25 + |v|)` for the meridional one), so a range lying east-west casts its shadow too.
- **Rain.** Over the sea, air picks up humidity towards what it can hold. What air can hold follows the saturation vapour pressure (Clausius-Clapeyron, `6.112 exp(17.67 T / (T + 243.5))` hPa, Bolton 1980), so it about halves for every 10 °C of cooling. Over land the air drizzles out steadily (60 % of that rain evaporates again, as land evapotranspiration returns 0.59 of land rainfall, Oki & Kanae 2006), and whatever it can no longer hold where it has cooled (climbing a slope, or moving to colder latitudes) condenses and rains out at once. That rain runs off: a 3 km range 50 km wide gets more than five times the rain of flat ground on its windward side, and its lee less than half of it (Roe 2005 gives a third to a half), twenty times drier than the windward slope: about 12 m a year against 0.3 m, where the Southern Alps get 11–12 m and under 1 m. Marine air sits at 80 % of what it can hold, the average relative humidity of the lower planetary boundary layer (Held & Soden 2000, p. 459; `EVAPORATION_KM` against `RAIN_KM`).
- **Foehn.** Air that rained out on a climb comes down warmer than it went up (it climbed at the moist rate and sinks at the dry one, 9.8 °C per km): it is warmed by the latent heat of that rain, `2491 K × kg of water per kg of air`, shown on the ground once it stops climbing and fading over 200 km. The lee of a 3 km range is a few degrees warmer.
- **North-south supply.** Rows with little ocean of their own still receive background humidity, set by the ocean fraction within ±20° of latitude and of the whole planet.
- **Circulation.** The rain is raised or lowered by up to 0.4 octave (a factor of 1.3) by `cos(6β)`, wetter at the equator and the polar front and drier at the subtropical highs and the poles, and by 0.6 octave more in an ITCZ belt about 10° wide at the equator. Under the sinking air of the Hadley edge, land receives only half of the background humidity. All of this is calibration (no source) against GPCP's zonal means.

The rain rate is smoothed over 25 km (at least a pixel) and turned into annual rain in mm: `2 120 mm × rate`, divided by the capacity of the air to the power 0.8 (the capacity floored at 0.1, about −8 °C), times the circulation factor. The constant is calibrated so that the mean over Earth's land of the stored moisture's rain (noise included, so within 125 to 8 000 mm) is 821 mm over `earth-like` seeds 1–4 and 11–16, GPCP version 2.3's 2.25 mm a day (Adler et al. 2018, doi:10.3390/atmos9040138); the other constants fit GPCP's zonal means, 5° N to 65° N and S, to about 14 % rms once normalised (calibration, see the doc comments in `climate.rs`) (ITCZ 1 500 mm against 1 900, subtropics 620 against 665, mid-latitudes 630–910 against 880–960). The unclamped mean is about a fifth higher, from windward maxima GPCP's 2.5° cells never see. The moisture is the base-2 logarithm of the rain over 1 000 mm, divided by 3 (so −1 is 125 mm, 0 is 1 000 mm and 1 is 8 000 mm), plus `1.0 × noise`, clamped. The `[climate]` table of the world's look then shifts the whole field, in octaves of a third (for example `−0.55` on volcanic worlds is a factor 0.3).

**Arid land.** Köppen's criterion B (Kottek et al. 2006): land is arid when the annual rain is under `10 P_th`, desert (BW) under `5 P_th`, shrubland (steppe, BS) between, with `P_th = 2 |T|` mm plus 0, 14 or 28 for rain mostly in winter, spread, or mostly in summer (two thirds of the year's rain). The app has no monthly rain, so the share of the summer half-year is the one the model's own rain gives it (the holding capacity to the power 0.2 at the half-years' mean temperatures, as the cold damping leaves it): it almost always comes out spread, `P_th = 2 |T| + 14`. Rain peaking in winter (the Mediterranean) and monsoons are not modelled. Polar climates (summer below 10 °C on boreal land) come first, as in Köppen. On four `earth-like` seeds arid land is about 30 % of the land (Peel et al. 2007: 30.2 %), 9 % desert and 21 % shrubland (Earth: about half and half); over seeds 11–16 it is 26 %. Tropical climates A come out at 14 % against Earth's 19 %, since the dry tropics are now arid. Default `terran` draws are often mostly arid (21–48 % over six seeds, against 30 % for Earth): hot subtropical land with 640–820 mm of rain falls under Köppen's `P_th` of `2 T + 14` (`10 P_th` is 640–820 mm at 25–34 °C), which on Earth is also arid, and `terran` draws vary with where their land lies: land under the Hadley edge or near the poles (cold air rains little) is dry, and a world with 60 % land has large interiors. The other moisture thresholds are now annual rain: forest from about 600 mm, taiga from about 500 mm, jungle from about 1 200 mm, wetland on shores above 1 000 mm. The former cut-offs `shrubland_moisture` and `desert_moisture` no longer exist: a preset file that sets either one fails to load with an "unknown field" error. Worlds made before this model (format version 10 and older) are no longer read.

### Clouds

The cloud cover of each pixel, in `[0, 1]`, depends on the atmosphere's cloud kind (`[atmosphere] clouds`):

- **water**: `0.12 × equatorial convergence` (a band about 10° wide) `+ 0.15 × circulation band + 0.25 × rain`, plus polar stratus (`+0.3` towards the poles, where the rain term fades out: cold air rains little but stays overcast), plus `1.5 ×` the cloud systems (4-octave noise) and `0.6 ×` finer cells (3 octaves, five times smaller; stronger in the tropics, where clouds are convective clusters), through `smoothstep(0, 0.9)` so cloud fields have thick cores and thin edges. Clouds gather where air rises (the equatorial convergence and the storm tracks around 60°) and where it rains (windward slopes), and thin out over the subtropical highs. Earth-like worlds come out with a mean cover of 0.44–0.52: about 0.7 at the equator, 0.3 around 30°, 0.5–0.7 along the storm tracks around 60°, 0.25–0.5 at the poles (four `earth-like` seeds). In the viewer, where thin clouds are translucent, they hide about 30 % of the ground.
- **sulfuric**: a deck in zonal bands, `smoothstep(−0.3, 0.6, 0.2 + 0.7 × cover + noise)`: total on `venus-like`, patchy on `volcanic` worlds.
- **dust**: low, patchy and thin (`0.8 × smoothstep(0.15, 0.6, …)`), about 10 % of `mars-like` worlds.
- **none**: no cloud layer.

`[atmosphere] cloud_cover` shifts the cover (−0.3 on frozen worlds). The viewer draws the layer over the globe and the flat map (**Clouds**, `C`), and can let it drift slowly around the planet (**Drift**, `W`). The noise has features of about 3 000 km. For water clouds it is warped by a larger noise, which curls cloud systems into swirls and fronts, and squeezed north-south only at mid-latitudes (up to ×2 at 45°), where the westerlies draw fronts into bands; tropical and polar clouds are not stretched. Sulfuric decks (×6) and dust (×1.5) are squeezed everywhere but near the poles. Airless worlds have no clouds.

### Regional variability

Before biome selection, both climate fields get a regional variability term standing for local differences of soil, aspect and circulation: `11.25 °C × fbm` on temperature (about 2 °C of standard deviation) and an independent `0.50 × fbm` on moisture (about 0.09, 0.27 octave of rain), with features from about 1 000 km down to 60 km. Biome borders then become irregular and interleaved instead of following isotherms and isohyets. The term is part of the exported `temperature` and `moisture` layers, so they still explain every biome.

### Rivers and lakes

1. **Depression filling.** Starting from the sea, the terrain is flooded upwards (priority-flood, Barnes et al. 2014) so every land pixel gets a path down to the sea; pits are filled to their spill level. The sea is every body of water below the waterline of at least 100 000 km² (between Lake Superior and the Caspian, a calibration); a smaller one, such as a crater whose floor dips under the waterline inland, is a pit like any other and fills with its basin. Taken for the sea, such a crater drained the basin around it: since the map draws craters down to 4 pixels across, a basin held a lake of 3.8 % of the planet at 1024 pixels of width and none at 2048. Water crosses the filled flats along a tiny slope whose cost per step varies with a noise, so paths across flats wander instead of running straight to the spill point; real slopes keep their height, so divides and lakes do not move. The flood stays sequential (it pops the pixels one by one in height order); it reads the neighbours by index only, without their distances, and takes about 0.2 s at 1920 × 1080 and 2.4 s at 8192 × 4096. Flooding the land masses in parallel (they are independent, the sea never changes) was tried and gave nothing: one continent usually holds most of the land.
2. **Routing.** Each land pixel drains to a lower neighbour on the filled surface, scored by its slope (the steepest scores 1) plus the descent of a large-scale routing noise (wavelength 1 200 km, same weight). Always downhill, so the filled surface stays valid, but on even slopes paths converge into the noise's valleys and join: river networks branch like trees instead of running parallel down mountain flanks. The wavelength is in km, so networks do not depend on the map size.
3. **Accumulation.** Every pixel adds its runoff — its area in km² × the runoff share of its annual rain `P` and mean temperature `T`: `(P − E) / P` with Turc's evapotranspiration `E = P / √(0.9 + P² / L²)`, `L = 300 + 25 T + 0.05 T³` (Turc 1954), divided by 0.6 (calibrated so `earth-like` worlds keep about 200 river lines); hot dry land (300 mm at 20 °C) returns nothing, so arid worlds are not netted with rivers — and passes the total downstream. The flow of a pixel is the wet-equivalent area draining through it.
4. **Lakes** are filled pits deeper than `0.004` that receive at least 40 000 km² of flow; the depth is measured from the spill level, without the slope added across flats, which grows with the number of pixels crossed and turned wide flats of high-resolution maps into lakes; **rivers** are pixels carrying at least 120 000 km². Under the polar temperature threshold, rivers stay ice and lakes freeze into ice cap.
5. **Vector rivers.** River pixels are chained along their receivers into polylines (the main stem follows the largest tributary), smoothed (a moving average over 5 points removes the pixel staircase, which small maps showed as zigzags, then Chaikin corner cutting), resampled and displaced sideways by noise into meanders whose wavelength (`7 ×` width, 6–40 px) and amplitude (`2.5 ×` width, 1–3 px) grow with the river, plus a slower wander. Guards keep them sound: no offset climbing the valley side, no bend tighter than half its radius, a gap to other rivers, loops cut, tributaries snapped onto their displaced parent, and every river ended at the first other river it crosses, which it then joins (downstream of its own tributaries' mouths). Lengths are in reference pixels, 1/1920 of the circumference, so the shapes do not depend on the map size.
6. **Drawing.** Rivers are drawn anti-aliased over the map. Their nominal width is `0.9 × √(flow / 120 000 km²)` reference pixels (capped at 4, tapered at the head); on a map `W` pixels wide they are drawn `0.5 × nominal × (W / 1920)^(2/3)` pixels wide, never thinner than their real width (`0.002 × √flow` km). The largest rivers are 2 px wide at 1920 × 1080, 3.3 px at 4096 and 5.3 px at 8192, about 8–14× their real width (instead of 21–27× before), so they read at map scale, and each doubling of resolution narrows it by 1.26× in km. Rivers thinner than a pixel are drawn with exact (box-filtered) coverage, so small ones fade instead of keeping a solid one-pixel line. `world.svg` draws them as stroked paths. The biome layer marks a pixel `River` where the drawn river covers at least half of it, so `biomes.png` matches the picture; frozen rivers (below the polar temperature) are not drawn, nor rivers across a lava field or a volcano: lava overrides water, so lakes there become lava too, and rivers start at the edge of the flow.

7. **Valleys.** The terrain around each river is lowered into a valley (`mapgen::carve`). Its axis is the river averaged over one meander wavelength, so walls follow the river's course, not each bend. Its bed is the lowest of the filled surface under the river and of the ground across the floodplain, never rising downstream, incised by `0.0006 × (flow / 120 000 km²)^0.25`, smoothed along the river, and kept `0.002` above the sea (or above the lake the river ends in). The cross-section is a flat floodplain as wide as the meander belt and at least `14 km × √(flow / 120 000 km²)` (about 80 km for the largest rivers), then walls rising `0.00005` per km, blended back into the terrain between 60 % and 100 % of the valley half-width (4 × the floodplain half-width + 60 km), and faded in from the river's head. Each pixel takes the profile of its nearest stretch of valley, blended over 5 km. The new elevation is the minimum of the terrain and the valleys, so carving never raises ground, never creates water and keeps the divides. Floodplains get `+0.15` moisture: rivers draw green strips across dry land. Their weight is exported as `floodplain.png`, and detail tiles keep floodplains flat (80 % less height detail). Carving takes about 2 s at 8192 × 4096.

8. **Deltas and estuaries.** Rivers reaching the sea with at least 800 000 km² of flow get an outlet. Where the land is low (under `0.012` above the waterline) at the apex, `120 km × √(flow / 1 M km²)` upstream of the mouth (60–300 km), 80 % of them get a **delta**: one distributary (two above 3 M km²) leaves the river at the apex 40° off the seaward direction, bends back towards the sea with a slow wander, and takes 20 % of the flow; a branch that does not reach the sea or would cross another river is dropped. The delta plain, within `0.35 ×` the delta length of its channels, is flattened towards `0.002` above the waterline and gets `+0.3` moisture (wetlands near the coast); it never moves the coastline. Elsewhere 60 % of them get an **estuary**: the drawn river widens to 3× towards its mouth over `3 × √(flow / 1 M km²)` reference pixels (about 60 km), on the map, in `world.svg` and in the detail tiles. The choice hashes the seed and the river, so it is deterministic.

Thresholds are in km², so the river network does not depend on the map resolution. Planets with nothing liquid to flow (whose look remaps `River` to dry ground, like `barren`; lava rivers of `volcanic` worlds still flow) have no rivers at all: no drawn lines, valleys or deltas, and an empty `rivers.json`; only the drainage flow is exported. On non-terran planets, rivers and lakes are remapped like seas: lava and magma on volcanic worlds, ice on frozen worlds, acid on caustic worlds, dry rock on barren worlds.

### Detail tiles — deep zoom

The generated layers stop at the base resolution (about 20 km per pixel at 1920 pixels on an Earth-sized planet), and the generation noise has no feature smaller than that: a larger map adds no detail. The `mapgen::detail` module synthesises detail on demand, consistently with the base world, as 256 × 256 tiles of an equirectangular quadtree (level `z` has `2^(z+1)` × `2^z` tiles; level 8 is about 300 m per pixel):

- **Elevation**: the base elevation, interpolated by a cubic B-spline (continuous slope and curvature, so the lit relief shows no crease along base pixels), plus fractal octaves of features smaller than two base pixels and larger than six tile pixels (finer ones only read as grain), so each tile is the low-pass of its children. The first octave is `0.0015` elevation units on plains and up to four times more on high ground, scaled by the surface of the base biome (blended across borders): 10 % on snow and ice, except on a world with a model whose crust is an ice shell (a round icy moon or an ice world: Europa, Titan), where frozen ground is the shell, its bedrock, and keeps all of it (sea ice stays at 10 %, and a small icy moon keeps the smooth ice of its look), 5 % on lava (a fluid surface), 40 % on sand and ash, all of it on rock and vegetation. Each octave is 0.4 of the previous one on high ground and 0.3 on plains, so slopes get gentler at every zoom level instead of turning into grain. Lava's hillshade is also kept at 40 %, on the base map and in the tiles. Height detail fades out within `0.006` of the waterline, so it never turns the shore into islets and ponds. Near the coast (within `0.01`–`0.04` of the waterline) the base elevation is instead read at a position displaced sideways by every detail octave (0.35 of its wavelength, 0.8× less at each finer octave): coastlines get capes and bays at every zoom level and stay one connected line.
- **Climate**: temperature, moisture and the other biome inputs are read at a position warped by about one base pixel, so biome borders become irregular; temperature follows the detailed elevation through the lapse rate.
- **Channels**: each river is cut into the detailed elevation along the same curve as its drawing, `0.0005 × (flow / 120 000 km²)^0.3` elevation units deep (exaggerated like the rest of the relief), with banks rising from a quarter of the drawn width to the full width from its middle, so the hillshade shows them; never below the sea.
- **Picture**: the generation's own biome rules and look remap, soft borders, and the vector rivers redrawn at the tile's scale (Catmull-Rom smoothed) with the base map's width law at the level's resolution (`W` = columns × 256): no jump in width when tiles replace the base map, 1.26× thinner in km at each level, down to their real width (`0.002 × √flow` km). A tile has no hillshade: it is a 256 × 512 PNG holding its colour in its upper half and its relief normals in its lower half, from its own elevation with a one-pixel border, so the viewer lights its relief like the base map's and neighbouring tiles meet without a seam (`RENDER_VERSION` 8).

- **Craters**: the [impact craters](#impact-craters) too small for the base map (from 4 tile pixels up to its smallest, 4 base pixels) are drawn on each tile from the same crater field, so zooming in reveals smaller and smaller ones. They are added to the relief rather than replacing it (see the craters section), scaled down with their size from the base map's relief at its smallest crater to a tenth of it, since the hillshade's 60-fold exaggeration would make small crater walls vertical. Biomes follow the ground without them: a small crater changes the relief, not the surface it is dug into.

A tile takes about 4 ms on 10 cores; on a heavily cratered world (a 4 Ga `mercury-like`) about 16–22 ms, against 5 ms without craters (the steep size law below 1.4 km and the slower diffusion of small craters draw more of them: 1.6 to 1.8 times the time on the main branch's craters, measured on one renderer and world at levels 8 to 12). A world prepared for tiles keeps its layers as stored in `raw_data/` (16 bits per value, decoded as they are sampled), a byte of lake and river flags and the water depth per pixel, and a byte of albedo: about 23 bytes per base pixel (0.2 GB at 4096 × 2048, 0.8 GB at 8192 × 4096), loaded in about 0.1 s at 4096 × 2048 since the layer files are decoded in parallel. Coast-related biomes (beaches, wetlands) still use the base coast distance, so their 40 km band is smoother than the detailed coastline at the deepest levels.

### Elevation — continents, coasts and mountains

Elevation before erosion is `continent + plates × relief_scale + mountain × mountain_weight × mountain_blend`, `plates` being the relief of plate boundaries at Earth's gravity (zero without plate tectonics), and the volcanoes add `cone × relief_scale` after it; `relief_scale` and `mountain_blend` follow gravity (see [Gravity modifier](#gravity-modifier--landscape-relief)).

- **Continents.** A 5-octave FBM on the unit sphere. Its 2 coarse octaves shape continents and basins; its 3 fine ones add hills. Away from the coast (more than about 0.03–0.2 above or below the waterline) only 30 % of the fine octaves remain, so plains, deserts and the abyssal plains of the sea floor are smooth (visible where seas have dried up); coasts keep them all, and so does high ground, with the mountain weight.
- **Floating crust.** The crust floats on the mantle (Airy isostasy): a crust `t` thick stands `(t − t_o)(1 − ρc/ρm)` above one `t_o` thick, whatever the gravity. A wet world's crust is thin below a crust line, the level of the continents' noise that leaves 41 % of its surface above it (Earth's continental crust, Cogley 1984; measured on a fixed 128 × 64 grid, whatever the resolution), and thickens above it across a slope, a shelf (0.3 km) and a platform rising 1.45 km inland (`1 − e^(−x/0.42)`), the high ground left to the mountains (from 0.28 of the noise above the crust line), the plate boundaries and the volcanoes; below it, the old floor deepens by up to Parsons & Sclater's 6.4 km less their 4 km, only trenches going deeper. Under Earth's ocean the old floor lies about 3.25 km deep and the abyss reaches about 5.65 km (`earth-like`, seeds 1–3): the water fills the relief from its volume, and the noise's basins have no ridges or margins to hold part of it above 4 km as Earth's do. Worlds with plates take Earth's crust, 41.1 km continental (Christensen & Mooney 1995) and 7.1 km oceanic (White et al. 1992) at 2.83 and 3.3 g/cm³: the continents stand 4.8 km above the old floor. Worlds without plates take Mars's dichotomy, 58 and 32 km (Neumann et al. 2004): 4.5 km. The crust stands on a fixed datum and the sea fills it from its water, so a world with less water exposes its shelves, then its ocean floors (the terran look keeps 0.40 of land with half of Earth's ocean, 0.64 with a tenth). The noise widths, the shelf, the platform's rise and the mountains' onset and weight (0.45 at Earth's gravity) are calibrations on Earth's hypsometry (ETOPO 2022, ice surface: land median 0.42 km, 56 % of land under 500 m, 10.8 % above 2 km, 1.8 % above 4 km, 1 % of the sea floor deeper than 6 km). On `earth-like` worlds (seeds 1–3, 256 × 128, Earth's ocean) land covers 29–32 % of the surface, 52–65 % of it under 500 m, 6–9 % above 2 km and 1.3–2.9 % above 4 km, and under 1 % of the sea floor is deeper than 6 km. The biomes' altitude lines: highland 2 km above the waterline (`highland` −0.07, its jitter `0.15 × noise`), snow line 4.5 km, volcanoes from 3 km, ash from 1.5 km, shores below 1.5 km, deep ocean below 5 km. On a wet world without plates with a planet model, the highland band ignores the Airy step: the thick crust's height above the datum is taken off (through `highland_offset`), so a highland plateau is not a mountain, only the relief built on it is (a choice, no source: Mars's southern highlands are cratered plains; `mars-like`, seeds 1–3, 11–16 % mountain against 41 % measured from the sea and about 9 % before the crust floated). Worlds with plates, dry worlds and moons keep it above the sea. A dry world keeps its rolling plains, laid from its datum.
- **Coastlines.** Near the waterline the FBM is sampled at a position displaced sideways by every octave finer than its own, down to about two map pixels, each by half its wavelength and 0.8× less at each finer octave. A low "plate" step is added there too: a Voronoi cell noise (±0.06, borders softened over about 1/20 of a cell) that gives coasts straight stretches and angular corners. Both fade out between 0.02 and 0.08 from the waterline, where they would only wrinkle the relief. Coasts are fractal instead of smooth contours. The finest outline octaves follow the map resolution, so a larger map adds detail to the same continents.
- **Mountains.** A ridged noise on a domain-warped position, with two finer ridged octaves concentrated on its ridges (35 % of its height), weighted from 0.28 of the noise above the crust line upwards (over 0.4 more; on a dry world, from 0.15 above its waterline). Worlds with plates keep half that weight: their other ranges follow the plate boundaries. Worlds without (stagnant, episodic or heat-pipe lids) keep it whole, but only in a few highland provinces: a low-frequency noise on the unit sphere (frequency 1.6, three octaves, a few provinces whatever the world's size) ramps the mountains in between 0.23 and 0.33, past its middle on about 9 % of the sphere (a seed draws more or fewer). Elsewhere they are plains with their craters, basins, maria, old sea floors and volcanoes, not a mountain fabric over the whole globe (a dry world stands all above the mountains' onset). The share is Venus's: tesserae cover about 7–8 % of it (Ivanov & Head 2011) and highlands with them about a tenth, against 70–80 % of plains. Measured on the `venus-like` archetype (seed 12345), with its rolling plains (below), 79 % of its area lies within ±1 km of its median, from 2.6 km below it to 13 km above (Venus: more than 80 % within 1 km of the mean radius, Ford & Pettengill 1992; about 3 km below to Maxwell Montes's 11 km above); before, 33 % and a mountain fabric everywhere. `mars-like` keeps its dichotomy and spans 30 km (Mars: about 30 km, Smith et al. 1999); `mercury-like` spans 18 km, about twice Mercury's 9.9 km (Becker et al. 2016), its craters and highlands under the impact cap calibrated on the Moon.
- **Dry worlds.** A world with no water at its surface (none kept, or all of it steam: a runaway greenhouse) is `dry` (`look.rules.dry` in `world.json`): no pixel of it is open water, whatever its elevation, so its lakes, rivers, shores, coast distance, cold currents and the viewer's flattened seas all see land, and nothing runs off or pools on it (lava still flows on a volcanic world). Its sea level then only sets the datum its relief and altitude lines are measured from: 0.1 below the middle of the range (`DRY_SEA_LEVEL`), so that its plains, on its own platform 0.5 km above the waterline (`DRY_PLATFORM_KM`: the continents' platform before the crust floated by its thickness, which a dry world, never shaped by a sea, keeps), stand in the linear part of the elevation range, with room below them for basins and craters (before, a dry world took the lowest sea level, −1: its plains sat in the compressed band near −1, its crater floors were cut flat at the floor, up to a fifth of an airless world, and every pixel stood above the highland line, all `Mountain`). No sea levelled its plains: they roll with the continents' noise, 3 km per unit at Earth's gravity (`DRY_PLAINS_KM`, scaled as `1/g`), calibrated on Venus's hypsometry with the `venus-like` figures above.
- **Plates.** Worlds with plate tectonics (the model's regime `plates`, in the `[geology] regime` of the world's look, when large and wet enough, see [Archetypes and looks](#archetypes-and-looks)) have 10 to 15 plates made of jagged Voronoi subplates, each turning about an Euler pole at 2 to 10 cm/yr, the faster the longer the edge along which it sinks under its neighbours (slab pull, Forsyth & Uyeda 1975: oceanic crust under a continent or under the oceanic plate riding over it; full speed from 30 % of its boundary, a calibration), found on the present boundaries; the drift's other 50 Ma stages (out to 300 Ma) get theirs the same way from the present boundaries carried rigidly by the plates' rotations, an approximation with no source: gaps, overlaps and crust changes are ignored (see `src/plates.rs`). On a fixed cube-sphere grid (six faces of 256 × 256 cells, about 40 km each on an Earth-sized planet, within 30 % of one another all over the sphere), whatever the map's size, each point gets the relief of its nearest boundary, from the boundary's convergence (full from 5 cm/yr) and the crust on each side (continental above the edge of the shelf, 0.05 under the waterline, from the continents' shape alone), interpolated between the grid's cells, after the cells' relief is blurred with their eight neighbours (1-2-1): where the nearest boundary changes (halfway between two boundaries, or along one that closes at different speeds or borders different crust), the relief used to step by up to 7 km from one cell to the next, a straight cliff a pixel or two wide, and now ramps over about three cells (the largest step of the mountains' weight between neighbouring cells falls from 1.0 to 0.49, of the uplift from 0.28 to 0.11 on a continent-and-ocean test world; ridges' swell is not blurred):

  | Boundary                                 | Relief                                                                      |
  | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Continent–continent, closing             | A range on both sides, 350 km wide: 0.12 of uplift and full mountain ridges |
  | Continent over ocean, closing            | A coastal range 150 km inland (0.08) and a volcanic arc 220 km inland       |
  | Ocean under a continent or another ocean | A trench along the boundary, 0.16 deep                                      |
  | Ocean over ocean, closing                | An island arc 120 km from the boundary (0.12)                               |
  | Ocean–ocean, parting                     | A mid-ocean ridge: the floor's age-depth swell, 1.5 km at the crest         |
  | Continent–continent, parting             | A rift valley and its shoulders                                             |

  Relief scales with gravity as the mountains do (`relief_scale`), at the poles as anywhere else, except the ridges: young ocean floor stands high because it is hot, and sinks with the square root of its age as it cools (Parsons & Sclater 1977, `d = 2.5 + 0.35 √t` km, `t` in Ma, up to about 70 Ma), thermal isostasy that does not depend on gravity. The floor's age is its distance to the ridge over half the spreading rate, and the ridge stands above the generator's old floor (Parsons & Sclater's 4 km) until the floor is 18 Ma old: 460 km out at 5 cm/yr, 180 km at 2 cm/yr, its crest 1.5 km above the old floor at any rate (2.5 km deep at 4 km; about 1.75 km under Earth's sea, whose old floor lies about 3.25 km deep) (it fades in only as plates start to part, under 0.5 cm/yr). Older floor keeps the noise's depth, which knows no age.
- **Drift.** `--drift <Ma>` (`drift_ma` in a request, stored in `world.json` when not 0) generates the world as its plates carried it, up to 300 Ma in the past (negative) or the future. Each epoch is a world of its own, under an id that names it (`terran-past250ma-42`, `terran-future12p5ma-42`, a decimal point written `p`), so it never replaces the present world (`terran-42`) and is listed by the server like any other. Each plate turns about an Euler pole that changes every 50 Ma stage (a pole and a speed drawn per plate and stage, the present one at stage 0, the rotation vector moving linearly between stages), integrated in 1 Ma steps. A point at the epoch takes the relief of its crust where it lies today: continents, mountains, volcanic provinces and their cones move with their plate, while the climate, the arcs' volcanoes and the boundaries stay at the point. Where two plates overlap, continental crust overrides oceanic crust, then the lower plate index wins (the hidden crust has dived under); where none covers a point, the plates have parted and new ocean floor fills the gap (at the old floor's 4 km, 0.16 below the waterline, smoother), with a mid-ocean ridge along the new boundary whose swell lifts it by its age. Boundaries and their relief are found again at the epoch, from the plates' motion then. The present is unchanged (`--drift 0` gives the same world to the bit). About a quarter of the surface is new crust at 150 Ma (Earth renews its ocean floor in about 180 Ma). The CLI writes the world to `<planet>-<seed>_<drift>Ma`. Continents still come from the present noise, so they tear along plate boundaries going back in time instead of reassembling. See `PlateMap::at_epoch` in `src/plates.rs`. Arcs need subduction more than its speed (full from 2 cm/yr) and carry volcanoes of their own, one per 200 km cell in 80 % of the cells, stratovolcanoes rising up to `0.4` above the terrain, growing with the arc's weight from nothing at 0.02 to their full height at 0.3, so that the arc's edge (which follows the coarse plate grid) slopes instead of dropping as a cliff; where an arc's volcanoes and a province's meet, the higher of the two stands. The noise's mountains keep half their weight: the other ranges follow the boundaries. About 0.13 s per world to build the plates.
- **Drift preview.** The viewer's **Drift** panel (worlds with plates) previews the whole pipeline at an epoch, at low resolution: `GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/drift/{ma}/surface.png` generates the planet again from the parameters stored in `system.json` (a planet's sea level and temperature calibrated on its model, as its generation does) with `drift_ma` set to `ma` (Ma from the present, negative in the past, within ±300), at 256 or 512 pixels wide (`width=`, 512 by default, never wider than the world) and the world's aspect ratio, and answers the render of its base map as a PNG: about 0.4 s at 512 on a 1024 × 512 world (release build, 8 cores), less than half that at 256. A preview is deterministic (the seed and the epoch give the same pixels) but is not the full world downscaled: the pipeline runs at its own resolution, so rivers and small coasts differ. The server keeps the last previews in memory (32 MiB, by body, revision, epoch and width) and the calibrated parameters of the last 16 bodies (calibrated once per revision, about a second, after the 503 check, whatever the epoch), runs one at a time, and refuses them with 503 and `Retry-After` while a generation runs. 404 for a body without plates (giants, stagnant, episodic and heat-pipe worlds, small or dry worlds that lose their plates), 400 out of range or for another width. The panel's slider moves by 10 Ma, draws a 256 pixel preview a moment after it stops and a 512 pixel one on release; **Generate at this epoch** opens the generation form (as *Vary*) with the same planet and `drift_ma`, whose result is a world of its own next to the present one. See `src/drift.rs`.
- **Volcanoes.** The volcanic noise, above the threshold set by the world's volcanism (from the planet model), outlines broad volcanic provinces; the threshold is the noise's quantile for the share of the surface asked for (the noise is roughly normal, σ ≈ 0.21). Volcanoes are cones within them: at most one per 900 km cell (half the cells), 50–120 km in radius whatever the planet's size, rising up to `0.25` above the terrain at Earth's gravity (volcanic islands at sea), as `1/g` like the mountains (`relief_scale`): on light worlds volcanoes are the tallest features, as Olympus Mons is. Where cones overlap, the highest profile is kept, not the nearest cone's, so a large cone and a small one meet without a step. Cones are young: they are added after the hydraulic erosion, which would wear them down to their base. The volcanic zone peaks on each cone and falls to 12 % of the province away from it, below the ash threshold, so a province is ordinary land dotted with volcanoes, each with its vent (about 1.2 radii), lava flows (1.8, lobed by a noise) and an ash halo (2.6). On highland, lava flows start at a zone of `lava_zone` (0.3); on other dry land (forest, plain, desert…) at `vent_zone` (0.45), with a volcano halfway from it to 1, so cones rise out of the lowland too. Presets that shift the volcanic zone (`volcanic` worlds, +0.5) still cover whole provinces with lava, and raise `vent_zone` to 0.9 so their lowlands keep their biomes away from the cones.

### Hydraulic erosion

Rain droplets are dropped at random on land. Each one rolls downhill with a little inertia, picks up sediment while it speeds up on steep ground, and drops it when it slows down or climbs (Beyer, 2015); what it still carries when it reaches the sea is carried offshore. Thousands of droplets carve valleys into the mountains and leave fans at their feet.

Erosion runs in two passes, on grids coarser than the map. The main pass always uses 1024 × 512 cells (or the map size if smaller), whatever the resolution: it carves the valleys and shapes the basins, so a world has the same lakes at 1024 and at 8192 pixels of width (with one grid per resolution, the lake area of a world varied from 0.3 % to 0.6 % of its land between sizes). Neither pass fills the basins of the terrain before erosion (cells more than `0.001` below their spill level): droplets and the blur of flat ground may carve them deeper but never raise them, so lakes survive erosion (it used to leave only 0.01–0.4 % of the land under lakes, against 0.6–1.8 % without erosion; now 0.4–1.4 %, Earth: about 1.5 %). Maps wider than 4096 pixels add a detail pass on a grid of one cell per 4 × 4 pixels (2048 × 1024 at 8192 × 4096) and keep only its change finer than a main-grid cell: gullies on the slopes, without new dams or basins. An erosion of 1 drops one droplet per grid cell. Droplets run in parallel: the grid is cut into tiles at least 104 cells wide (twice the farthest a droplet reaches), run in four checkerboard phases so that tiles running together never touch the same cells; each tile gets droplets in proportion to its land and its own random stream, so a world does not depend on the number of cores.

- **Real slopes.** Beyer's constants (the droplets' `gravity` of 4 among them, a constant of the method, not the planet's: a heavier world is not worn faster) assume heights of about one per cell; this terrain is about 100 times flatter. Droplets run on heights exaggerated 10× (on a 1024-cell grid, scaled with the cell size), so their capacity follows the slope instead of its minimum everywhere.
- **Plains stay smooth.** Droplets only carve where the slope (exaggerated, per cell) exceeds 0.04, fully from 0.1. On flatter cells the height change is blurred (three box passes of 2 cells): valleys continue as wide, shallow swales, so they still drain and leave no basin at the foot of the mountains, and fans no longer roughen plains and deserts. Fine-scale roughness on deserts is about 5× lower than with uniform erosion, and mountains are about 3.5× rougher than plains.
- **Gullies are at least three cells wide.** On steep ground the droplets of a flank follow one another into parallel channels one cell wide, on the grid's Nyquist frequency: the map samples them at two pixels per cell or fewer and they read as stripes. Half of the change is replaced by its blur (three box passes of 1 cell) before it is kept, which widens the channels and leaves the valleys and the hypsometry within their bands (a full blur lowered the peaks less and pushed the land above 4 km over its band). On a steep synthetic island the share of the change finer than three cells falls from 0.031 to 0.022 (pinned by a test).
- **Coasts keep their profile.** Erosion fades out within 0.05 of the waterline, and the change spread back onto the full-resolution map (cubic B-spline, smooth across cell borders) never moves a pixel across the waterline: land is never dug below the sea and no new land rises from it.
- **Poles.** Erosion fades out between 60° and 75° of latitude: droplets move in pixel steps and the projection stretches pixels east-west towards the poles, which would otherwise carve artificial north-south grooves.
- **Frozen ground.** Ice carves no river valleys: erosion fades out on rows whose sea-level temperature (latitude and the look's climate shift) stays below the look's polar threshold, rising to full strength 4.5 °C above it. Frozen worlds keep smooth ice sheets, and only their thawed belts are eroded.

### Impact craters

Craters follow the planet's surface age (from the planet model), gravity, atmosphere and wet era (`[geology] wet_until`); the research behind them is in [docs/analysis/08-geology.md](docs/analysis/08-geology.md). See `src/craters.rs`.

- **How many.** The lunar chronology of Neukum gives the density of craters of 1 km or more on a surface `T` Ga old: `N(1) = 5.44e-14 (exp(6.93 T) − 1) + 8.38e-4 T` per km², a steep decline until about 3.5 Ga, then a constant flux (0.063 per km² at 4 Ga). Sizes follow Hartmann's (2005) piecewise law, `N(>D)` falling as `D^-3.82` below 1.4 km (where secondary craters take over), `D^-1.8` from 1.4 to 64 km and `D^-2.2` above, so young surfaces seen up close are crowded with small craters. Each size octave is capped at saturation, `0.75 × 0.115 / D²` per km² for `[D, 2D)` (a tenth of geometric saturation, the app's calibration: real equilibrium densities vary), where each new crater erases as many as it adds: the oldest are the ones erased, so a saturated octave keeps its youngest craters, and the time slider brings the erased ones back at the epochs they still stood (up to twice as many as are kept; beyond, for craters of 1 km on surfaces older than about 4.2 Ga or sub-km ones of a 4 Ga surface, the oldest are never drawn, which keeps the detail tiles cheap). The flux below scales the density before that cap, so a saturated surface stays saturated whatever the flux. On a Moon-sized 4 Ga surface this gives about 5 500 craters of 20 km or more (more than 5 000 are counted on the Moon, Head et al. 2010). The chronology is the Moon's, scaled by the locking below and by the body's flux against the Moon's (`src/impacts.rs`), the crater counts being the Moon's at `D / crater_scale` times that flux. A world with a model takes both from its orbit: its system's impactors (the giants' or the system's impact factor), the asteroids crossing its orbit (more near the belt, at `2.7 √L` AU, than inside it, up to a calibrated ceiling of six times the Moon's), their encounter speed (Earth's 17.2 km/s from Le Feuvre & Wieczorek 2011, scaled as the orbital speed `√(M★/a)`) and its gravitational focusing `1 + v_esc²/v∞²`, the whole bounded smoothly by a calibrated ceiling of 20 times the Moon's (the speed grows without end close to a star); a lone world, with no system drawn, takes the Solar System's impactors. The crater of the same impactor scales as `g^(−μ/(2+μ)) v^(2μ/(2+μ))` with `μ = 0.55` for rock (Holsapple), the impact speed against the Moon's cited 19.7 km/s and bounded smoothly by Mercury's, 2.16 times the Moon's. Among the Solar System's impactors, `mars-like` gets 2.6 times the Moon's flux with craters 0.74 times as wide (Hartmann: 2.6 and 0.751), `mercury-like` 1.55 and 0.98, `venus-like` 1.42 and 0.74, `earth-like` 1.38 and 0.69 (Le Feuvre & Wieczorek: fluxes of 1.82, 1.75 and 1.58). The belt's eccentricity spread that sets Mars's flux and the ceilings are calibrations. A moon keeps the Moon's craters at its planet's (or its giants') rate; a moon of a gas or ice giant takes the comets its giant focuses on it instead (Zahnle et al. 2003): the heliocentric flux at the giant's orbit by the rule above, with the Solar System's impactors whatever belts the seed drew, times `1 + v_esc(a)²/v∞²`, `v_esc(a) = √(2 G M / a)` at the moon's distance `a` (Callisto 1.5 times the Moon's flux, Io 5.1), stored as its `impact_factor`. Ages are drawn from the same curve, so most craters of an old surface are very old.
- **Where.** Diameters are split into octaves (`[2^k, 2^(k+1)]` km), each with a grid on the faces of a cube projected on the sphere, every cell holding a random number of craters expected from its area. The grids depend on the seed and the planet, not on the map size. The base map draws the craters of 4 pixels or more (80 km at 1920 pixels on an Earth-sized planet, 32 km on a Mercury-sized one); smaller ones read as dots at that scale.
- **Shape.** Simple craters are bowls `D/5` deep with a rim of `0.036 D`. Above a transition diameter they get a flat floor and a central peak, and follow Pike's complex depth law, `d = 1.044 D^0.301` on the Moon (4.2 km at 100 km), written in transition diameters so that it scales with gravity; the depth passes from the simple to the complex law between 1 and 1.5 transitions, as the two fits do not meet. A peak ring grows beside the peak from 9.1 transitions (protobasins, 137 km on the Moon) and replaces it from 13.8 (peak-ring basins, 207 km; Baker et al. 2011). On a planet the transition is `9.0 Y / (ρ g)` (O'Keefe & Ahrens 1993) with the strength of its crust, read from its model: dry rock (12.5 MPa at 2.8 g/cm³, a calibration: `earth-like` 4.0 km, `mercury-like` 10.9, `venus-like` 4.4), rock rich in volatiles without plates (1.5 times weaker, after Barnouin et al. 2012: `mars-like` 7.2 km), or an ice world's shell. A moon, a round one with its model included, keeps the lunar law, `15 km × 1.62 / g`, 0.15 of it on icy moons (a calibration with no readable source: Ganymede near 2.5 km, where the lunar law gives 17). Icy craters keep rock's simple bowl, Schenk (2002) giving their depth only in a figure; multi-ring basins (Orientale) are not drawn. Ejecta thin out as `(r/R)⁻³` and stop at 3 radii; rims and ejecta are irregular. A rim a pixel or two wide is sampled rather than drawn (the pixel under the crest catches or misses it by chance), so the rim and ejecta fade in with the crater's size in pixels: nothing at the smallest crater drawn (4 pixels across), full from 12. Elevations are converted with `ELEVATION_KM` = 25 km per elevation unit, calibrated on Earth-like worlds (median sea floor about 4 km deep, highest peaks 8–10 km): a fixed unit on every world, the relief itself scaling with gravity.
- **Superposition.** Craters are drawn in age order, oldest first: inside a crater the previous relief is replaced (15 % of it kept), outside, ejecta bury it.
- **Water.** Craters older than the end of the wet era are drawn before the hydraulic erosion, worn by their exposure to water (a 20 km crater keeps `1/e` of its relief after 0.1 Ga at erosion 1, larger ones last longer in proportion: a calibration, no source fixes these numbers), then eroded; younger ones are drawn after it, fresh. A world with liquid water today (0.01 bar or more) is wet to the present, so its craters are worn: Earth-like worlds keep only a few large, faint ones. Craters on the floor of a sea that still fills the basins (the look's ocean is still water, ice-sealed sea, acid or magma) leave nothing, on every tectonic regime: the rule reads the sea, not the plates. Craters make closed basins, which the hydrology fills into lakes on wet worlds.
- **Diffusion.** Small impacts slowly smooth every crater (lunar topographic diffusivity, 5.5 m² per Ma): a crater of radius `R` keeps `1 / (1 + κt / (0.0715 R²))` of its relief (`κ` = 5.5 m²/Ma; a hollow spreading under diffusion keeps its volume, so its depth falls as the inverse of its widening area), so that after 3 Ga a 1 km crater keeps 52 % of its depth and a 300 m one 8.9 %, as Fassett & Thomson (2014) measure on the maria (52 % and about 7 %); a 200 m crater keeps 4 % (they find craters below 200–300 m beyond recognition), while the craters of the base map are untouched.
- **Detail tiles.** Craters too small for the base map appear in the [detail tiles](#detail-tiles--deep-zoom). The grids are searched only around each tile, giving exactly the craters of the whole sphere. These craters are added to the relief instead of replacing it, which keeps neighbouring tiles identical at their borders: at saturation small cavities cover only about 7 % of the ground per octave, so adding them digs no pits.
- **Rays.** Fresh craters are bright: their floor, their ejecta (thinning as `(r/R)⁻³`) and 6 to 14 rays of their own, 8 to 30 crater radii long (Tycho's reach about 35), narrowing with distance. Space weathering darkens them, `1/e` every 250 Ma, so that they are all but gone after 1 Ga, like the Moon's Copernican craters (a calibration on that time scale, not a fit). They are drawn on worlds without liquid water today (air, wind and rain erase them elsewhere), from craters down to one pixel wide, into `raw_data/albedo.png`, a brightness shift that takes the ground part of the way to white (`ALBEDO_GAIN`, 60 % at a shift of 1). The detail tiles read it at the base resolution; at another epoch, the rays are those of the craters fresh then. About 0.1 s at 1920 × 1080 on a 4 Ga `mercury-like` (74 000 craters to look at).
- **Maria.** On looks with a window of maria (`[geology] maria`: `barren` 3.9–3.1 Ga, `mars-like` 3.7–3.0, `mercury-like` 4.0–3.6), lava floods a share of the basins of 100 km or more (and six times the simple-to-complex transition) formed before the window ends: 30 % without volcanism up to 90 % at `--volcanic 1`, each at a date of the window after its basin formed. The lava fills the floor flat out to 0.9 basin radii, up to 40–75 % of the way from the lowest of the floor to the mean of the rim as they are then, and darkens it (`MARE_ALBEDO`, an albedo shift of −0.35). Floods are events among the impacts, in age order: older craters inside are buried, or show only their rims; younger ones and their rays lie over the lava. They are all drawn after the erosion, lava plains wearing little, so that the time slider can show them come and go.
- **Tidally locked planets.** A planet locked to its star keeps one hemisphere facing its orbital motion, the apex, on the equator 90° west of the point under its star (longitude 90° on the map, the substellar point being at 180°), as the Moon's apex lies 90° west of the point under the Earth. That hemisphere sweeps up more impactors: since the locking (`star age − locking time` ago), craters are drawn more densely towards the apex, `(1 + k cos γ) / (1 + k)` of a raised density with `k` for an apex-to-antapex ratio of 1.3 (about 1.14 from one hemisphere to the other; models of the Moon give 1.14 to 1.32, observations more). Older craters, formed while the planet still rotated, are spread evenly; the mean density does not change. Unlocked worlds keep their craters.
- **Air.** A thick atmosphere breaks up small impactors: no crater below `0.1 km × c^0.75`, `c` the air's column (surface pressure over gravity) against Earth's (3 km on Venus, about 0.6 km on a Titan, whose 1.5 bar under its low gravity weigh ten times Earth's air), fewer below ten times that.
- **Cost.** Craters are drawn in waves of craters that share no pixel, each crater after every older one it overlaps, so a wave runs in parallel and the result is the same as drawing them one at a time. On a 4 Ga Mercury-sized world, the most cratered of the examples, generations with and without craters take the same time within run-to-run variation (about 1 s at 1920 × 1080, 18 s at 8192 × 4096).

### Epochs — a surface through time

A world without liquid water today can be shown at another epoch: its young impact craters (those of the dry era, drawn after the erosion) are drawn again on `raw_data/elevation_base.png` as they were then, or will be. See `src/epoch.rs` and [docs/analysis/08-geology.md](docs/analysis/08-geology.md).

- **Range.** Epochs are in Ma before present (negative in the future): from 1 Ga ahead back to the surface age, or to the end of the wet era if more recent, since erosion reshaped the craters before (`mercury-like`: −1 000 to 4 000 Ma, `mars-like`: −1 000 to 3 500 Ma). Worlds with liquid water today offer none: their lakes, rivers and erosion would move with the relief, and are not recomputed. Neither do worlds made before format version 7, which have no `elevation_base`. The web app reads the range of a body from its `world.json`, and the server checks it for every epoch it serves.
- **Craters at an epoch.** A crater exists at epoch `t` when it is at least `t` old. It is worn for the time between its formation and `t` (diffusion), so going back in time freshens it. Craters still to come form at today's constant flux (8.38 × 10⁻⁴ craters of 1 km or more per km² and per Ga), from a lattice of their own: the present craters never move. Epoch 0 is the world as generated, within two 16-bit steps of `elevation.png`.
- **What follows.** The biomes, the relief normals and the temperature follow the relief through the lapse rate; moisture and the other layers keep their present values.
- **Serving.** The server computes a world at an epoch once and keeps it in the tile cache like a world of its own (same memory, same budget). It serves `GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/epoch/{ma}/colour.png`, `normal.png`, `elevation.png` and `biomes.png` at the base resolution, optionally reduced (`scale=2` or `4`), and the detail tiles at that epoch (`epoch=` on the tile URL). About 0.17 s to compute an epoch and 0.11 s to render its surface at 1920 × 1080; 2.9 s and 1.8 s at 8192 × 4096. Rendering the colour or the normals decodes the whole world and renders it at full size, reduced afterwards (the normals averaged as vectors), outside the tile cache's budget: about 48 bytes per pixel (1.6 GB at 8192 × 4096), so the server renders one of them at a time (the elevation and biomes, read from the cached world, four at a time like the tiles).

The two parameters derived from the circumference are computed as follows.

### Noise scale — continent & feature size

The world is projected onto a unit sphere. The Perlin noise samplers are called with coordinates on that sphere, so the spatial frequency of features is independent of image resolution.

For an Earth-sized planet (`C⊕ = 40 075 km`) those frequencies are the baseline. For any other circumference:

```text
noise_scale = C⊕ / circumference_km
```

This multiplies every noise coordinate, effectively zooming in or out on the sphere surface:

| Circumference | noise_scale | Visual effect                             |
| ------------- | ----------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| 20 000 km     | ≈ 2.0       | Many small continents and narrow seas     |
| 40 075 km     | 1.0         | Earth baseline                            |
| 80 000 km     | ≈ 0.5       | Few, sweeping continents with vast oceans |

### Gravity modifier — landscape relief

A rocky planet compresses its own rock: the larger it is, the denser. Its mass follows the mass–radius relation of rocky planets fitted to Earth's interior (Zeng et al. 2016, [doi:10.3847/0004-637X/819/2/127](https://doi.org/10.3847/0004-637X/819/2/127), Earth's composition, the coefficient taken as 1 so that Earth's size gives Earth's mass), and a body too small to compress itself keeps the density of uncompressed rock, the Moon's (3.344 g/cm³, NASA):

```text
r = C / C⊕     M = r^3.7 (but ρ ≥ 3.344 / 5.513 ρ⊕)     g = GM/r²  ∝  r^1.7
```

So `gravity_modifier = max(r^1.7, 0.607 r)`: about 0.34 g for a Mars-sized world (Mars: 0.38, where Earth's density gave 0.53), 0.92 for Venus's size (0.90), 0.165 for the Moon's (0.165) and 3.2 at twice Earth's size. Mercury, with its large iron core, has 0.38 g where this gives 0.23. Beyond 2.6 Earth radii (about 100 000 km round), far past the relation's fit (8 Earth masses), gravity stays at 5 g, the bound the app kept before. A moon generated as a world has its own gravity instead, from the density its system drew for it (the gravity on its card, `gravity` in its parameters), and a giant its mass over its radius squared. The planet card's mass and gravity follow the same rule, as does the mass the drawn giants keep their spacing from (`world_mass_earth` in `src/giants.rs`).

A mountain presses on the rock beneath it with its own weight, and rock bears only so much: the tallest mountain a planet holds is about `σ / ρg`, inversely proportional to its gravity (Weisskopf 1975). The relief built on the crust (the noise's mountains, the ranges, trenches, arcs and ridges of plate boundaries, and the volcanoes) is scaled by `relief_scale = 1 / gravity_modifier`, capped at 2: Olympus Mons, the tallest mountain known, has 2.5 times Everest's relief (Plescia 2004), and the lighter Moon, Io and Vesta stand far below their `1/g` ceiling. The continents and the sea floor are not scaled: they float on the mantle (Airy isostasy), and the height of floating crust does not depend on gravity. The mountains' weight in the elevation is the **mountain blend coefficient**:

```text
relief_scale   = min(1 / gravity_modifier, 2)
mountain_blend = 0.45 × relief_scale
```

| Circumference | gravity  | mountain_blend | Relief                  |
| ------------- | -------- | -------------- | ----------------------- |
| 20 000 km     | ≈ 0.31 g | 0.90 (capped)  | Dramatic, jagged peaks  |
| 40 075 km     | 1.00 g   | 0.45           | Earth baseline          |
| 80 000 km     | ≈ 3.24 g | ≈ 0.14         | Flat, rounded landscape |

A Mars-sized world (0.34 g, `1/g` = 2.9) and a Moon-sized moon (0.165 g, 6.1) both reach the cap: ranges and volcanoes twice Earth's (with the square root of gravity used before, 1.4 and 1.9 times). Elevations stay within `[-1, 1]`: beyond ±0.85 they are compressed smoothly instead of clipped, so the tallest summits of light worlds keep their shape (on Earth-sized worlds only the highest volcanoes reach that knee). Heights in kilometres follow, `ELEVATION_KM` (25 km per unit) being the same unit on every world.

Both values are written into `raw_data/world.json` as `circumference_km` and `gravity_modifier` for downstream use.
