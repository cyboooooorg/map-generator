# Known planet types

The French version is [`16-planet-types.fr.md`](16-planet-types.fr.md); the notes in [`16-planet-types/`](16-planet-types/) hold the per-topic sources and the verification tables.

*References checked against primary sources on 2026-11-15; [M] marks what remains unverified.*

The literature does not offer a single classification but two crossing axes: the **bulk composition** (rocky, water-rich, H/He envelope, giant) and the **regime** set by the environment (irradiation, orbit, host star, age). The reference mass split is the one of Chen and Kipping: **Terran → Neptunian around 2.04 M⊕, Neptunian → Jovian around 0.414 M_Jup (~131 M⊕), Jovian → stellar around 0.080 M☉** ([arXiv:1603.08614](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08614)). On the radius side, an occurrence valley around 1.5-2.0 R⊕ separates rocky super-Earths from sub-Neptunes ([Fulton 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10375)). Planets of 1-4 R⊕ on orbits shorter than 100 days are the most common type around Sun-like stars, hot Jupiters concern only ~1 % of them, and most of the "exotic" classes (carbon planets, coreless planets, eyeballs, confirmed ocean worlds) remain theoretical or candidates. For your generator, the decisive point is that mass and radius alone do not determine the composition: a second diagnostic is needed (density, stellar metallicity, atmosphere). This report separates the claims **verified** against primary sources from those **recalled from memory or not found, marked [M]**, whose figures must be rechecked; several arXiv identifiers recalled from memory also turned out to be wrong and were corrected during the verification.

## Official definitions and the mass and radius splits

The status of "planet" rests on dynamical criteria. Margot proposes a quantitative criterion using only the stellar mass, the planet mass and the orbital period to test the ability to clear the orbital zone: the eight Solar System planets pass it, Ceres, Pluto and Eris do not, and about 99 % of the known exoplanets can be classified and pass it (verified) ([Margot 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.06300v4)). The IAU 2006 definition (resolution B5: orbits the Sun, nearly round shape from self-gravity, cleared neighbourhood) is confirmed by secondary texts, the official IAU PDF having been unreadable. The IAU working definition of exoplanets, amended in August 2018 by Commission F2, combines two conditions: a true mass below the deuterium-burning limit (computed as 13 M_Jup for solar metallicity) and a mass ratio M/M_central < 2/(25+√621) ≈ 1/25 = 0.04 ([Lecavelier des Etangs and Lissauer 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09520)). The references of Soter 2006 ([arXiv:astro-ph/0608359](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0608359): planets dominate their orbital zone, with a gap of four to five orders of magnitude from asteroids) and of Stern and Levison 2002 (*Highlights of Astronomy* 12, 205) are confirmed, but the formula of Soter's discriminant μ and that of the Stern-Levison parameter Λ were not reread [M]. An alternative geophysical definition (Runyon et al. 2017), independent of the orbit, would include massive moons and give about a hundred "planets" in the Solar System, but only secondary summaries were read ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2017-03-scientists-case-pluto-planet-status.pdf)).

For the split by size, the probabilistic framework of Chen and Kipping (316 calibration objects) gives R ∝ M^S laws with verified indices: **Terran 0.279 ± 0.009; Neptunian 0.589 (+0.044/−0.031); Jovian −0.044 (+0.017/−0.019); stellar 0.881 ± 0.025** ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1603.08614)). The Jovian plateau, where the radius is almost independent of mass, is a result of this model; its exact width (~0.3 to ~80 M_Jup) was not verified in a source. Kopparapu et al. 2018 propose a size × flux grid: five size classes (rocky, super-Earth, sub-Neptune, sub-Jovian, Jovian) and three flux bands (hot, warm, cold) bounded by the condensation curves of ZnS, H2O, CO2 and CH4 (rocky 0.5-1.0 R⊕, super-Earth 1.0-1.75, sub-Neptune 1.75-3.5, sub-Jovian 3.5-6.0, Jovian 6-14.3; [arXiv:1802.09602](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09602)). The Kepler size classes (Earth < 1.25 R⊕, super-Earth 1.25-2, Neptune 2-6, Jupiter 6-15, very large 15-22) come from Borucki 2011 ([arXiv:1102.0541](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.0541)). The NASA catalogue settles for four labels (terrestrial, super-Earth, Neptune-like, gas giant) plus "unknown" ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/exoplanet-catalog)). The Earth Similarity Index (ESI) and the PHL classification are heuristic and insensitive to the atmosphere and the interior; their bounds were not verified [M] (founding paper: Schulze-Makuch et al. 2011, [DOI 10.1089/ast.2010.0592](https://doi.org/10.1089/ast.2010.0592)). The catalogue counted **6,470 confirmed planets** (4,799 by transit, 1,205 by radial velocity, 294 by microlensing, 98 by imaging, 29 by TTV), but the page consulted carries no date ([NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/docs/counts_detail.html)).

## Rocky planets: two axes, composition and regime

The notes separate the rocky classes along two axes. The **composition** axis, set at formation, includes the terrestrial type, the iron-rich type ("super-Mercury"), the coreless type, the carbon type and the water-rich type. The **regime** axis, set by irradiation, rotation, volatiles and tectonics, includes the ultra-short-period (USP) lava world, the desert planet, the Venus-like runaway greenhouse, the snowball and the eyeball. The reference mass-radius relations are Seager et al. 2007 ([arXiv:0707.2895](https://arxiv.org/abs/0707.2895)) and Zeng, Sasselov and Jacobsen 2016, based on the terrestrial PREM model ([arXiv:1512.08827](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.08827)). For Zeng 2016, the abstract gives R/R⊕ = (1.07 − 0.21 CMF)(M/M⊕)^(1/3.7) for 1-8 M⊕ and a core mass fraction CMF of 0 to 0.4, an exponent of 0.27 consistent with the terrestrial case; the coefficient for pure iron (~0.77 M^0.25) remains to be rechecked in table 3 [M]. Dorn et al. 2015 show that mass and radius leave a large degeneracy on the internal structure ([arXiv:1502.03605](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03605)).

The **confirmed or well-supported** types are the following. The iron-rich world is illustrated by K2-229 b (R = 1.165 ± 0.066 R⊕, M = 2.59 ± 0.43 M⊕, core fraction close to that of Mercury) ([Santerne 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08405)); Adibekyan et al. 2021 show that the iron fraction of a rocky planet is correlated with that of its star without being 1:1, and that the five known super-Mercuries orbit iron-rich stars ([arXiv:2102.12444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12444)). The example GJ 367 b, first presented as an extreme case (iron fraction ~0.91), was revised in 2026 to R = 0.736 ± 0.035 R⊕, M = 0.503 ± 0.078 M⊕, iron ~50-70 %; it is an accepted preprint, with unconfirmed peer-review status ([arXiv:2606.18355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18355)). USP planets (P < 1 day) form a population defined by Sanchis-Ojeda et al. 2014 ([arXiv:1403.2379](https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.2379)); their parameters are disputed, CoRoT-7 b for instance being quoted between ~4.8 and ~8 M⊕ for R = 1.58 ± 0.10 R⊕, so that no single density should be cited ([arXiv:1105.3372](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1105.3372)). JWST results blur the picture of the "bare lava world": 55 Cnc e carries a secondary atmosphere rich in CO2/CO rather than a mere rock vapour ([Hu 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04744)), TOI-561 b (R ~1.4 R⊕, M ~3.2 M⊕, P < 11 h) has a thick volatile atmosphere ([arXiv:2509.17231](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17231)), whereas LHS 3844 b ([arXiv:1908.06834](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.06834)), TRAPPIST-1 b ([arXiv:2303.14849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14849)) and c ([arXiv:2306.10150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10150)) have no thick atmosphere, and GJ 3929 b is probably bare according to a preprint ([arXiv:2508.12516](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12516)). The atmospheric retention boundary is therefore not simple.

The **theoretical or unconfirmed** types are the carbon planets (Kuchner and Seager 2005, [arXiv:astro-ph/0504214](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0504214)), whose only observational claim, 55 Cnc e ([Madhusudhan, Lee and Mousis 2012, arXiv:1210.2720](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.2720)), rests on a stellar C/O > 1 that Teske et al. 2013 revise from 1.12 ± 0.09 to 0.78 ± 0.08 ([arXiv:1309.6032](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6032)); the coreless planets, purely theoretical and indistinguishable from a terrestrial planet in mass-radius ([Elkins-Tanton and Seager 2008](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.1908)); the desert planets, whose habitable zone is wider than that of ocean planets (freezing at 77 % of the terrestrial insolation against 90 %; inner edge at 415 W/m² for the dry terrestrial planet against 330 W/m² for the ocean planet, that is 170 % against 135 % of the present insolation) ([Abe et al. 2011](https://vpl.uw.edu/publication/habitable-zone-limits-for-dry-planets-astrobiology-2011), [DOI 10.1089/ast.2010.0545](https://doi.org/10.1089/ast.2010.0545); the figure of 375 W/m² sometimes cited is not from Abe: a research summary attributes it to Leconte et al. 2013, [arXiv:1312.3337](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.3337), an attribution not reread in the paper [M]); and the eyeball and snowball climates of synchronously rotating planets ([Pierrehumbert 2011](https://doi.org/10.1088/2041-8205/726/1/L8), "A Palette of Climates for Gliese 581g"), no confirmed example existing. The tectonic regime of super-Earths is undecided: Valencia and O'Connell 2007 predict plate tectonics increasingly likely with mass ([arXiv:0710.0699](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.0699)), O'Neill and Lenardic 2007 the opposite ([synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.0031)).

## Sub-Neptunes and water worlds: the radius valley and its four readings

The **radius valley** is the structuring observational fact: Fulton et al. 2017 measure a deficit of a factor ≥ 2 between 1.5 and 2.0 R⊕ for P < 100 d, separating super-Earths < 1.5 R⊕ from sub-Neptunes of ~2-3 R⊕ ([arXiv:1703.10375](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10375)). Van Eylen et al. 2018, with asteroseismic stellar radii, place the valley near 2 R⊕ (super-Earths ~1.5, sub-Neptunes ~2.5 R⊕) and measure a slope R ∝ P^γ with γ = −0.09 (+0.02/−0.04), negative as predicted by photoevaporation and opposite to the late formation of gas-poor planets ([arXiv:1710.05398](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.05398)). Two mechanisms explain the valley: photoevaporation by the stellar XUV, mostly in the first ~100 Myr ([Owen and Wu 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.3899); [2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10810)), and core-powered mass loss ([Ginzburg et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01621); [Gupta and Schlichting 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03202)); the data do not clearly decide. The review by Bean, Raymond and Owen makes it the basis of two classes of small planets ([arXiv:2010.11867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11867v1)).

For compositions, the notes retain five classes, whose boundaries depend on models and whose densities overlap: rocky super-Earth, "gas dwarf" (a few percent of H/He by mass on a rocky core), water or steam world (tens of percent of water), Hycean (water ocean under an H2-rich atmosphere), and magma-ocean mini-Neptune. Zeng et al. 2019 argue that planets of 2-4 R⊕ are largely water worlds, with up to 50 % ices; a planet of ~10-15 M⊕ half ice, half rock would have R ≈ 2.5 R⊕ ([arXiv:1906.04253](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04253v1)). Luque and Pallé 2022 find, around M dwarfs, three density populations (rocky, water-rich ~50 % rock / 50 % water, gas-rich) and conclude that density, not radius, separates rocky from water-rich planets ([arXiv:2209.03871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03871)). This reading is contested: Rogers, Schlichting and Owen 2023 judge the evidence for a population of water worlds around M dwarfs still elusive ([ApJL 947, L19](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/acc86f)), and Rogers, Dorn, Raj, Schlichting and Young find that most super-Earths have less than ~3 % water (1σ upper limit at the population level) ([arXiv:2409.17394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17394), ApJ, accepted), and the outgassing of hydrogen in the interior produces only ~0.5-1.0 % mass of endogenous water ([arXiv:2402.14072](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2402.14072)). No peer-reviewed synthesis says which class dominates; the fraction of water worlds is therefore open. The Hycean worlds (radii up to ~2.6 R⊕ for 10 M⊕, inner habitable-zone edge near Teq ~500 K for late M dwarfs) come from Madhusudhan et al. 2021 ([arXiv:2108.10888](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10888)); the model of Schlichting and Young 2022 places more than 93 % of the hydrogen in the atmosphere and judges water-dominated atmospheres poorly compatible with the observations ([arXiv:2107.10405](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.10405)).

JWST shows that the mass-radius class does not predict the atmosphere: TOI-270 d (M = 4.78 ± 0.43 M⊕, R = 2.1 R⊕ according to [Van Eylen 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01593) or 2.2 R⊕ according to the refinement of Benneke 2024; CH4 at 9.4σ, CO2 at 4.8σ, mean molecular weight 5.47) is read either as a miscible metal-rich envelope or as a Hycean world, two interpretations still in conflict ([arXiv:2403.03325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03325); [Felix 2025](https://www.aanda.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202555194)); GJ 9827 d (R = 1.98 R⊕) is a steam world with more than 31 % water by volume, not pure water ([arXiv:2410.03527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03527)); GJ 1214 b has a Bond albedo of ~0.51 and a metal-rich atmosphere under clouds ([Kempton 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v620y2023i7972d10.1038_s41586-023-06159-5.html)); LHS 1140 b rejects an H2-rich atmosphere at more than 10σ ([arXiv:2406.15136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15136)). For K2-18 b, CH4 (5σ) and CO2 (3σ) are detected ([arXiv:2309.05566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05566)), but the claim of DMS/DMDS (3σ, [arXiv:2504.12267](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12267v1)) is not statistically secure: a joint reanalysis finds no sufficient evidence (Luque et al. 2025, [arXiv:2505.13407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13407), published in A&A 700, A284), a magma-ocean model reproduces the spectrum at ~3σ like a water ocean ([arXiv:2401.05864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05864)), and an unpublished preprint infers a water-rich interior with only a marginal DMS ([arXiv:2507.12622](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2507.12622)). The mass and radius parameters of these planets were rechecked: K2-18 b R ≈ 2.6 R⊕ ([Benneke 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.04642)) and M ≈ 8.6 M⊕ (Cloutier 2019, value read in a research summary, not in the paper); GJ 1214 b M = 8.17 ± 0.43 M⊕ and R = 2.742 R⊕ ([Cloutier 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14732)); LHS 1140 b M = 5.60 ± 0.19 M⊕ and R = 1.730 ± 0.025 R⊕ ([Cadieux 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15490)).

## Giants: from Neptune to ultra-hot Jupiters, and their boundaries

Giants are first split by composition: the **ice giants** (Uranus, Neptune) are dominated in mass by heavy elements with a thin H/He envelope, unlike the gas giants (Jupiter, Saturn); the term "ice" is historical and the interiors are probably hot fluids, whose structure (layered or gradual) is debated ([Helled, Nettelmann, Guillot 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04891); [Helled and Fortney 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10783)). The numerical values (Uranus 14.54 M⊕, Neptune 17.15 M⊕, densities 1.27 and 1.64 g/cm³) come from the [NASA NSSDC](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/uranusfact.html) fact sheets. They serve as a reference for a "Neptunian" class of about 10-30 M⊕, a deduction of the notes rather than a sourced result.

The second split is thermal and orbital. **Hot Jupiters** (P < ~10 d) cover 1.2 ± 0.38 % of Sun-like stars in radial velocities, more than twice the rates of Kepler and OGLE-III ([Wright 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2273)), and have no single origin: in situ formation, disk migration and high-eccentricity tidal migration all contribute, two main channels possibly explaining most of them ([Dawson and Johnson 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.06117)). The literature places warm Jupiters between 8 and 200 d ([Dong et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01970)); the bounds hot < 10 d and cold beyond vary by author and are a proposal of the notes. **Ultra-hot Jupiters** are better defined by a temperature than by the period, because the type of the star matters: Parmentier et al. 2018 define them as gas giants whose dayside temperature exceeds ~2200 K ([arXiv:1805.00096](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00096)), and not by Teq (some recent works reuse 2200 K for Teq). KELT-9 b has P ≈ 1.48 d around a star at ~10,170 K, Teq ≈ 4050 ± 180 K, a dayside of about 4,600 K and ~700 times the EUV flux of WASP-33 b ([Gaudi 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06723)). Their chemistry is distinct: H2, H2O, TiO and VO dissociate, the alkalis ionize and the H⁻ opacity dominates ([Parmentier 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00096); [Arcangeli 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.02489), who finds [M/H] = −0.01 ± 0.35 and C/O < 0.85 for WASP-18 b). The **hot Neptune desert** is documented by Mazeh et al. 2016 ([arXiv:1602.07843](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07843v2)); Castro-González et al. 2024 add an overdense "ridge" at ~3.2-5.7 d, 4.7σ above the desert, read as late tidal migration ([arXiv:2409.10517](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10517)). A caveat: ~87 % of the planets of the period-radius desert would not be in a desert defined in flux ([arXiv:2411.16960](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16960)). TOI-849 b (M = 39.1 +2.7/−2.6 M⊕, envelope ≤ 3.9 +0.8/−0.9 % of the mass) is the best candidate for a residual giant core, but the interpretation is contested ([Armstrong 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10314)). The term "chthonian" (residual core of a former hot Jupiter) comes from Hébrard et al. ([arXiv:astro-ph/0312384](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0312384)), not from the paper of Lecavelier des Etangs et al. 2004 on atmospheric escape; Mocquet, Grasset and Sotin 2014 exists (*Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A* 372, 20130164) but its link with TOI-849 b is not established. The helium atmospheres of Hu, Seager and Yung 2015 concern small warm planets, not massive giants ([arXiv:1505.02221](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.02221v1)).

**Inflation** must be treated apart from **super-puffs**. For hot Jupiters, the efficiency of flux deposition in the interior peaks at ~2.5 % around Teq ≈ 1500 K then falls to ~0.2 % around 2500 K, which is predicted by ohmic dissipation and not by thermal tides ([Thorngren and Fortney 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.04539)). Super-puffs are planets of a few to ~tens of M⊕ with a density of ~0.05 g/cm³ (Kepler-51 b, c, d: ~2.1, 4.0, 7.6 M⊕ by TTV, [Masuda 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.2885v2)), with flat spectra attributed to a high-altitude haze (P < 3 mbar, [Libby-Roberts 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12988)) or, as a hypothesis, to rings (JWST on Kepler-51 d, [arXiv:2505.21358](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.21358)). WASP-107 b, intermediate, requires internal heating: methane depleted by ~3 orders of magnitude, explained by tides or by ohmic heating ([Batygin 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01581v1)). The Sudarsky scheme (I ammonia, II water, III cloudless, IV alkalis, V silicates/iron) rests on Sudarsky, Burrows and Pinto 2000 ([arXiv:astro-ph/9910504](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9910504)) and Sudarsky, Burrows and Hubeny 2003 ([arXiv:astro-ph/0210216](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0210216)); its temperature bounds are I < ~150 K, II ~150-250 K, III ~350-800 K, IV ~900-1500 K, V > ~1500 K (with intervals not covered between classes; Teff in 2000, Teq in 2003 for irradiated objects, direction of the inequalities deduced from the order of the classes), and it ignores metallicity, C/O and internal heat. WASP-39 b illustrates this limit: photochemical SO2 and a metallicity ~10 times solar ([Tsai 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10490)). The three JWST analyses of WASP-107 b diverge on CH4: not detected in [Dyrek 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12515), 4.2σ in [Sing 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11027) (log abundance −6.03 ± 0.21), 5σ in [Welbanks 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11018).

The **upper boundary** has no consensual line. The limit of 13 M_Jup is a convention; deuterium burning plays no role in formation and the mass ranges overlap according to Chabrier et al. 2014 ([arXiv:1401.7559](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1401.7559)), while Schlaufman 2018 places the core-accretion ceiling below ~10 M_Jup, objects of ≲4 M_Jup favouring metal-rich hosts and those of >10 M_Jup not ([arXiv:1801.06185](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1801.06185)). The notes propose capping giants at ~13 M_Jup and labelling 4-13 M_Jup "super-Jupiter / limit".

## Environment-defined types: rogues, binaries, pulsars, white dwarfs, M dwarfs

**Rogue planets** exist but their abundance depends strongly on mass. Mróz et al. 2017 (2,617 OGLE-IV events) cap it at **0.25 Jupiter-mass rogue per star** (95 %) ([arXiv:1707.07634](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.07634)), contradicting the excess of ~1.8 per star of Sumi et al. 2011 ([arXiv:1105.3544](https://www.arxiv-vanity.com/papers/1105.3544/)). Sumi et al. 2023 (MOA-II, ~3,500 events, six of duration < 0.5 d) find a power-law index α = 0.96 (+0.47/−0.27) below 0.02 M☉ and rogues more numerous than wide-orbit bound planets by a factor of 19 (+23/−13); the two normalizations cited (dN/dlogM = 2.18 per dex and per star at 8 M⊕, against 0.49 at 38 M⊕ in a review) are the same fit at different pivot masses (2.18 × (8/38)^0.96 ≈ 0.49), with no conflict ([arXiv:2303.08280](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2303.08280); [review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07502)). In young clusters, Miret-Roig et al. 2022 find 70-170 rogues of ~1-13 M_Jup in Upper Scorpius, which no mass function tested explains, hence a probable ejection ([arXiv:2112.11999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.11999)). The Orion **JuMBOs** (~40 Jupiter-mass binaries among ~540 candidates, [Pearson and McCaughrean 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01231)) are contested: most would be absent from Luhman's substellar sample ([arXiv:2410.10406](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10406)), and ~90 % of ejected pairs would be destroyed within 1 Myr in the dense cluster ([White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/226514/1/2505.00762v1.pdf)). The final status of the JuMBOs is unknown; they must be treated as unconfirmed.

**Circumbinary planets** (P-type) are rare: the NASA archive counts, on 2026-11-15, 14 transiting planets in 12 systems (12 from Kepler in 10 systems, 2 from TESS), and they orbit just beyond the stability limit (Holman and Wiegert 1999, [arXiv:astro-ph/9809315](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9809315): about 1.6 separations for a circular binary of low mass ratio, up to ~4-5 separations for e ~ 0.5-0.7; "~2-4" is a summary, not a figure from the paper). The generation rule "P-type only beyond ~3 times the separation" is an inference of the notes. The examples are confirmed: Kepler-16 b (Saturn-like, P ≈ 229 d, [Doyle 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.3432)), Kepler-34 b and 35 b ([Welsh 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.3955)), Kepler-47 b/c ([Orosz 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.5489), a 3-planet system since 2019), Kepler-1647 b (1.52 ± 0.65 M_Jup, P ≈ 1100 d, [Kostov 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00189)) and TOI-1338 b (6.9 R⊕, P = 95.2 d, [Kostov 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07783)). Armstrong 2014 gives 10.0 +18/−6.5 % (95 % limits) of eclipsing binaries for R_p > 6 R⊕ and P < 300 d if the planets are coplanar with the binary, and a lower limit of 47 % if the inclinations are isotropic ([arXiv:1404.5617](https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.5617)); Kraus 2016 finds, below a_cut = 47 +59/−23 au, a planet frequency of 0.34 times that of wide binaries, that is ~3 times lower ([arXiv:1604.05744](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05744)). The NASA archive lists 7 **pulsar planets** (PSR B1257+12 b, c, d, of which c and d discovered by [Wolszczan and Frail 1992](https://doi.org/10.1038/355145a0); PSR B1620-26 b, [Sigurdsson 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0307339); PSR J1719-1438 b, whose companion could be a very low-mass carbon white dwarf, [Bailes 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.5201); J2322-2650 b; B0329+54 b, a candidate of 10,140 d). Around **white dwarfs**, WD 1856+534 b is the only known transiting giant, still called a candidate (R ≈ 10.4 R⊕, ≤ 14 M_Jup at 95 %, P = 1.4 d, [Vanderburg 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.07282)); the **disintegrating planets** (KIC 12557548 b, P = 15.685 h, fiducial mass 0.1 M⊕, evaporation time ~0.2 Gyr, [Rappaport 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.2662); K2-22 b, P = 9.1 h, [Sanchis-Ojeda 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04379); WD 1145+017, [Vanderburg 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.06387)) are a transient class whose lifetime of ~1 Myr to 1 Gyr was not found in a primary source [M]. **Planets around M dwarfs** form compact systems: Dressing and Charbonneau 2015 give 2.5 ± 0.2 planets of 1-4 R⊕ with P < 200 d per Kepler M dwarf ([arXiv:1501.01623](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01623)). Protoplanets (PDS 70 b, c), rings (J1407 b) and exomoons (Kepler-1625 b-i, not robust) are, according to the notes, age attributes or candidates, not types; no exomoon is confirmed.

## Occurrence rates usable for a procedural draw

Verified occurrences are few. For small planets, Hsu et al. 2019 give, for 0.75-1.5 R⊕ at 237-500 d, between 0.03 and 0.40 planet per FGK star, a very wide interval ([arXiv:1902.01417](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.01417)); Bryson et al. 2021 give an η⊕ (0.5-1.5 R⊕, hosts 4800-6300 K) of **0.37-0.60 per star (conservative) and 0.58-0.88 (optimistic)** ([arXiv:2010.14812](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14812); very wide 68 % intervals). For giants, the occurrence drops around 2-3 au, but two measurements conflict at 3.8-7.1 au: **1.44 ± 0.54 per 100 stars** (Fernandes 2019, [arXiv:1812.05569](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05569)) against **15.5 +3.2/−3.0 per 100** (California Legacy Survey, [arXiv:2105.11584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11584), which attributes the gap partly to the treatment of partial orbits in Fernandes), an unresolved factor of ~10; the same survey gives 14.1 +2.0/−1.8 per 100 stars at 2-8 au and 8.9 +3.0/−2.4 at 8-32 au. Suzuki et al. 2016 find, by microlensing, a broken power-law mass function around q ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ (~20 M⊕ for a 0.6 M☉ host), with slopes −0.93 ± 0.13 above and 0.6 (+0.5/−0.4) below, revised to ~5.5 × 10⁻⁵ by Jung 2019 ([arXiv:1612.03939](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.03939)). Weiss et al. 2018 show that, in 355 multiple systems (909 planets), neighbours have similar sizes and regular spacings, with no period ratio < 1.2 ([arXiv:1706.06204](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06204v3)), a result criticized by Murchikova and Tremaine ([arXiv:2003.02290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02290)). Reference reviews: [Zhu and Dong 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.02127), [Gaudi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.04703).

Other checked occurrences: Petigura 2013 finds 11 ± 4 % of Sun-like stars with an Earth-size planet at 1-4 times the terrestrial insolation and 22 ± 8 % with a planet of 1-2 R⊕ in the habitable zone (flux 0.25-4 F⊕) ([arXiv:1311.6806](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.6806)); the figure "~26 % for 1-2 R⊕ and P of 5-100 d" was not found in the paper and must not be used; Cumming 2008 gives 10.5 % of Sun-like stars with a giant of 0.3-10 M_Jup for P = 2-2000 d, that is < ~3 au ([arXiv:0803.3357](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3357)); Johnson 2010 gives 3.5 % at 0.5 M☉ and 14 % at 2 M☉ at solar metallicity, with an occurrence ∝ M_star and ∝ 10^(1.2[Fe/H]) ([arXiv:1005.3084](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.3084)); hot Jupiters 1.2 ± 0.38 % (Wright 2012, [arXiv:1205.2273](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2273)) against ~0.57 +0.14/−0.12 % for Petigura 2018 ([arXiv:1712.04042](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04042), value read in a secondary summary [M]), a gap of about 1.5σ. Still unverified: the exponent of the giant occurrence ∝ 10^(2[Fe/H]) attributed to Fischer and Valenti 2005 (the summary confirms a power-law fit, not the exponent) [M]. The notes contain no verified figure for the relative frequency of types, nor a per-type count of the NASA catalogue: any weighting by type must be built from these occurrences by size and by period, with the intervals above, and not from a catalogue count, biased by detection methods.

## What the generator does not produce yet

The generator's 13 classes are in `crates/mapgen/src/planet/classify.rs` (`PlanetClass`). Missing types were filed as backlog issues on 2026-11-15, and the user validated this order, to follow the science track (#423) after its step 5b (#487):

1. With the climate pass: #492 steam worlds, and labels on the existing model: #498 super-Mercury, #499 eyeball / snowball / desert climate states, #501 warm and ultra-hot Jupiters.
2. A distribution step that changes system draws (needs a `FORMAT_VERSION` bump): #494 ultra-short-period planets (periods are drawn from 1 d), #495 super-puffs (inflation only above 30 Earth masses), #496 dwarf planets (mass floor 0.05 Earth masses), #497 chthonian planets.
3. #500 Hycean worlds (needs H2 collision-induced greenhouse over an ocean).
4. Outside the track: #502 rogue planets, #503 mutual inclinations / retrograde orbits / stellar obliquity, #504 young systems with a disk, #505 theoretical compositions (carbon, coreless, helium).

Already covered by studies: #376 binaries and circumbinary planets, #375 compact and evolved hosts (white dwarfs, pulsars). Each new type would get a reference archetype after #487: Kepler-78 b (ultra-short period), Kepler-51 b (super-puff), TOI-849 b (chthonian), KELT-9 b (ultra-hot Jupiter), K2-229 b (super-Mercury), K2-18 b (Hycean candidate).

## Conclusion

The least expected result for a generator is that the "obvious" classes rest on uneven foundations. The mass boundaries (2 M⊕, ~131 M⊕), the radius valley (~1.5-2 R⊕) and the hot Neptune desert are sourced statistical facts; on the other hand, the nature of a planet of 2-4 R⊕ (gas dwarf, water world, Hycean, magma ocean) is not decided, and the JWST results of 2024-2026 have tightened the doubt rather than lifted it (TOI-270 d, K2-18 b, 55 Cnc e). It is therefore more honest to generate each planet with two independent attributes, a probabilistic composition and a regime deduced from the irradiation, the orbit and the star, rather than a single label, and to treat protoplanets, rings, JuMBOs and carbon planets as rare or optional cases.

The gaps to fill before implementation come down to what remains marked [M]: the formula of the discriminant μ of Soter 2006 and that of the Stern-Levison 2002 parameter Λ, the bounds of the ESI and of the PHL classification, the pure-iron coefficient in table 3 of Zeng 2016, the attribution of the 375 W/m² threshold to Leconte et al. 2013, the exponent of the giant occurrence of Fischer and Valenti 2005, the 0.57 % value of Petigura 2018 and the lifetime of disintegrating planets. To this is added the arbitration of the factor of ~10 gap on the occurrence of cold giants, which directly conditions the frequency of Jupiters in the generated systems, and the publication status of the preprint of Hu et al. on K2-18 b ([arXiv:2507.12622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12622)) to be rechecked.
