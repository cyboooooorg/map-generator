# Planet types defined by orbit, host or environment (not bulk composition)

Verification legend. [V] = checked against a search result or fetched page in this session. [M] = written from memory; the paper exists as cited, but the identifier or the numbers were not re-checked this session and must be verified before publication. Numbers marked [M] are approximate. Status: late 2026.

## 1. Rogue / free-floating planets (FFPs) and JuMBOs

### Takeaway (section 1)

Free-floating planetary-mass objects exist and are detected two ways: microlensing (field, mostly Earth-to-Neptune/Saturn mass) and deep imaging of young clusters (Jupiter-mass, still hot). Field abundance is large for sub-Jupiter masses and small for Jupiter masses. The JuMBO population in Orion is contested; most candidates are probably not real planetary-mass binaries.

### Cited Findings (section 1)

- Definition (working): planetary-mass object (below about 13 Jupiter masses, the deuterium-burning limit) with no bound host star. Origin is ambiguous: ejection from a planetary system, or star-like collapse of a core (the objects are then sub-brown dwarfs). [M, conventional definition]
- Sumi et al. 2011 (Nature 473, 349) claimed an excess of very short microlensing events (about 474 events, MOA-I/II), implying about 1.8 Jupiter-mass unbound planets per main-sequence star — [arXiv:1105.3544](<https://www.arxiv-vanity.com/papers/1105.3544/>) [V for title and claim]. The Jupiter-mass claim was later contradicted by the larger OGLE sample.
- Mróz et al. 2017 (Nature 548, 183): OGLE-IV 2010–2015, 2,617 events, no large Jupiter-mass FFP population; 95% upper limit 0.25 Jupiter-mass FFPs per star; a few events with tE < 0.5 d point to Earth/super-Earth-mass FFPs — [arXiv:1707.07634](<https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.07634>), DOI 10.1038/nature23276 [V].
- Sumi et al. 2023 (AJ 166, 108; DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/ace688), MOA-II 2006–2014, about 3,500 events: six events with tE < 0.5 d, shortest 0.057 ± 0.016 d (about 0.33 Earth-mass-class lens). Power-law mass function with index alpha = 0.96 (+0.47, −0.27) below 0.02 solar masses. The AJ abstract gives dN/dlogM = 2.18 (+0.52, −1.40) per dex per star at 8 Earth masses. FFPs outnumber wide-orbit bound planets by 19 (+23, −13), with total masses of the same order. The Jupiter-mass FFP population is excluded. Roman is predicted to find about 988 FFPs down to Mars mass — [arXiv:2303.08280](<https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2303.08280>) [V via search summary]. Caveat: a review quotes a different normalization form (0.49 per star at 38 Earth masses); check the paper before quoting a number — [arXiv:2310.07502](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07502>).
- Mróz et al. 2020 (ApJL 903, L11) OGLE-2016-BLG-1928: shortest known event, tE about 0.0288 d, candidate Earth to Mars-mass FFP (about 0.3 Earth masses), unbound at the 1-sigma level — [arXiv:2009.12377](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.12377>) [M].
- The OGLE team also reported FFP candidates with finite-source effects (OGLE-2017-BLG-0560). A 2026 press report mentions a free-floating planet of about 70 Earth masses measured in a Science paper; not checked — [NCN news](<https://ncn.gov.pl/en/aktualnosci/2026-01-01-o-planetach-swobodnych-w-science>) [V for the existence of the page only].
- Miret-Roig et al. 2022 (Nature Astronomy 6, 89; DOI 10.1038/s41550-021-01513-x): 70–170 FFPs (age-dependent) of about 1–13 Jupiter masses in Upper Scorpius (about 130 pc), from 80,000 wide-field images and 20 years of proper motions. No tested IMF explains the abundance, so the authors favour ejection from planetary systems — [arXiv:2112.11999](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.11999>) [V].
- Euclid ERO: Martín et al. 2024/2025, "A glance at free-floating newborn planets in the sigma Orionis cluster" (A&A 697, A7). The IMF follows a power law with no cutoff down to about 4 Jupiter masses at 3 Myr — [arXiv:2405.13497](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.13497>) [V; author list not checked].
- JuMBOs: Pearson & McCaughrean 2023 reported about 40 Jupiter-mass binary objects (0.7–30 Jupiter masses; wide separations tens to hundreds of au) among about 540 candidates in the Trapezium region from JWST NIRCam — [arXiv:2310.01231](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01231>) [V for the identifier]. Counts quoted: 42, later revised down to about 20 by McCaughrean — [Live Science](<https://www.livescience.com/space/astronomy/mysterious-rogue-objects-discovered-by-james-webb-telescope-may-not-actually-exist-new-simulations-hint>) [V].
- Debate: Luhman 2024 (AJ), reanalysis of the NIRCam data: most JuMBOs are missing from his substellar sample and at least seven have a background component — [arXiv:2410.10406](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10406>) [V identifier via search summary]. Spectra of ONC brown dwarf candidates: [arXiv:2507.03679](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.03679>) [V]; spectroscopy is the decisive test.
- Formation tests: photoerosion of fragmenting cores — [arXiv:2410.09159](<https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09159v1>) [V]; N-body simulations: about 90% of ejected planet pairs are destroyed within 1 Myr in the dense Orion cluster, so ejection cannot explain them — Wang, Perets & Kouwenhoven (MNRAS Letters 2024) and Portegies Zwart & Hochart 2023 (both [M], identifiers not checked); Parker group MNRAS Letters 2025 — [Sheffield/White Rose PDF](<https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/226514/1/2505.00762v1.pdf>) [V].

### Inferences (section 1)

- Occurrence is mass-dependent: about one FFP per star or more in the Earth-to-Neptune range (Sumi 2023), below 0.25 per star at Jupiter mass (Mróz 2017). Young-cluster numbers (Upper Sco) cannot be added to these directly because their selection and ages differ.
- For a world generator, FFPs are cold, dark, no insolation, driven by internal heat and radiogenic heating; a good "rogue" class is Earth to Neptune mass with no stellar irradiation. JuMBOs should be treated as unconfirmed.

### Gaps (section 1)

- Final JuMBO status (NIRSpec results) not found. The reliability of the 2026 Science FFP paper not checked. Euclid wide-survey FFP results (beyond ERO) not found.

## 2. Circumbinary and S-type planets in binaries

### Takeaway (section 2)

Transiting circumbinary (P-type) planets are rare in Kepler (about 14 known) and sit just outside the stability limit; no known case orbits a close binary shorter than about 7 d. Binaries suppress S-type planets within about 50 au.

### Cited Findings (section 2)

- Kepler-16b (Doyle et al. 2011, Science 333, 1602): Saturn-mass (about 0.33 Jupiter masses), about 0.75 Jupiter radii, P about 229 d around a K dwarf (0.69 solar masses) + M dwarf (0.20 solar masses), binary period 41 d — [arXiv:1109.3432](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.3432>), DOI 10.1126/science.1210923 [M].
- Kepler-34b and 35b (Welsh et al. 2012, Nature 481, 475) — [arXiv:1204.3955](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.3955>) [M]. Kepler-47 b, c (Orosz et al. 2012, Science 337, 1511): first multi-planet circumbinary system — [arXiv:1208.5489](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.5489>) [M]. Kepler-1647b (Kostov et al. 2016): about 1.5 Jupiter masses, P about 1,100 d, longest-period transiting CBP — [arXiv:1512.00189](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00189>) [M].
- TOI-1338b (Kostov et al. 2020, AJ 159, 253): first TESS CBP, about 6.9 Earth radii, P about 95 d around an eclipsing binary (1.1 + 0.3 solar masses, 14.6 d) — [arXiv:2004.07773](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07773>) [M, identifier uncertain].
- Stability: critical orbit about 2–4 binary separations (Holman & Wiegert 1999, AJ 117, 621) — [arXiv:astro-ph/9809315](<https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9809315>) [M]. Kepler CBPs cluster just beyond it, interpreted as migration halted at the cavity edge. Radial-velocity CBP: BEBOP-1c (Standing et al. 2023, Nature Astronomy) [M].
- Occurrence: Armstrong et al. 2014 (ApJ 782, 124) estimate giant CBPs around about 10% of short-period eclipsing binaries, consistent with single stars — [arXiv:1311.6808](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.6808>) [M, value approximate].
- S-type: gamma Cephei Ab (Hatzes et al. 2003), about 1.9 Jupiter masses around the primary of a 20 au binary [M]. Alpha Cen B b (Dumusque 2012, Nature) was disputed by Rajpaul et al. 2016 [M]. A JWST MIRI candidate around alpha Cen A was reported in 2025 [M, unverified; treat as candidate]. Kepler-444 (Campante 2015, ApJ 799, 170): five sub-Earth planets around an old K dwarf with an M-dwarf pair at about 1.5 au projected distance — [arXiv:1501.06227](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.06227>) [M].
- Suppression: Kraus et al. 2016 (AJ 152, 8): binaries with separation below about 47 au host planets about 3 times less often — [arXiv:1604.05744](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05744>) [M].

### Inferences (section 2)

- Habitable zone around a binary exists (Kepler-16b is in the HZ, but is a gas giant), with strongly time-varying insolation; useful as a generator rule: P-type planets only beyond about 3 times the binary separation.

### Gaps (section 2)

- Exact current CBP count; a late-2026 catalogue not consulted. Identifiers marked [M] not re-checked.

## 3. Pulsar planets

### Takeaway (section 3)

The first confirmed exoplanets are around the millisecond pulsar PSR B1257+12; pulsar planets are very rare (about 5–6 known) and detected by timing.

### Cited Findings (section 3)

- Wolszczan & Frail 1992 (Nature 355, 145): two planets of at least 2.8 and 3.4 Earth masses (the third, about 0.02 Earth masses, was found by Wolszczan 1994), P about 66.5 d and 98.2 d — DOI [10.1038/355145a0](<https://doi.org/10.1038/355145a0>) [M]. Likely formed from a post-supernova fallback or merger disk [M].
- PSR B1620-26 b (Thorsett et al. 1993; Sigurdsson et al. 2003, Science 301, 193): about 2.5 Jupiter masses circumbinary planet around a pulsar + white dwarf in globular cluster M4, age about 12.7 Gyr, orbit about 23 au, likely formed around a main-sequence star and captured in an exchange encounter [M].
- PSR J1719-1438 b (Bailes et al. 2011, Science 333, 1717): the remnant companion of a millisecond pulsar, minimum density about 23 g/cm³, mass about Jupiter, P about 2.2 h, probably a crystallized carbon-oxygen white-dwarf core ("diamond planet") — [arXiv:1108.5201](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.5201>) [M]. Classification as planet disputed: it is a stripped white dwarf.
- Mass range: the pulsar planet sample is mostly below a few Earth masses to Jupiter masses; planets there are exposed to a high-energy wind but not to meaningful starlight [M].

### Inferences (section 3)

- Pulsar planets are second-generation; surfaces would be heated mainly by the pulsar wind and tides. Rare, so a low generator weight.

### Gaps (section 3)

- No search was done for pulsar-planet occurrence beyond about 1 in 20 millisecond pulsars [M, unsourced]; none cited.

## 4. White-dwarf planets and disintegrating planets

### Takeaway (section 4)

One transiting giant planet is known around a white dwarf (WD 1856+534 b); most other WD evidence is polluted atmospheres and transiting debris. Disintegrating planets are small, short-period, evaporating bodies seen through variable transit depths and dust tails.

### Cited Findings (section 4)

- WD 1856+534 b (Vanderburg et al. 2020, Nature 585, 363): Jupiter-sized (about 10 Earth radii), mass below 14 Jupiter masses, P = 1.4 d around a WD about 0.5 solar masses and about 1.4 Earth radii; the host's white-dwarf phase would have destroyed it at the current orbit, so it migrated inward after the star lost mass — [arXiv:2009.07282](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.07282>) [M]. JWST thermal emission (Limbach et al. 2025) set a mass limit of about 11 Jupiter masses and T about 180 K [M, unverified].
- WD 1145+017 (Vanderburg et al. 2015, Nature 526, 546): transiting debris from a disintegrating asteroid-like body, 4.5-h period, transit depth up to 40% — [arXiv:1509.07516](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.07516>) [M].
- KIC 12557548 b (Rappaport et al. 2012, ApJ 752, 1): P = 15.7 h, variable transit depth 0.2–1.3%, a dust tail from a rocky body at about 0.01 Earth masses, evaporating — [arXiv:1201.2189](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.2189>) [M].
- K2-22b (Sanchis-Ojeda et al. 2015, ApJ 812, 112): P = 9.1 h, transit depth varying from 0 to 1.3% — [arXiv:1504.04379](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04379>) [M]. A third known object is KOI-2700b (Rappaport 2014) [M].
- Spectral pollution: 25–50% of white dwarfs show metals from accreted planetary debris (Zuckerman et al. 2010; Koester et al. 2014) [M, unsourced].

### Inferences (section 4)

- A disintegrating planet is a transient class: a lifetime of about 1 Myr to 1 Gyr is set by the mass-loss rate, so those found are special.

### Gaps (section 4)

- No search for post-2022 WD planet candidates (for example Gaia astrometric candidates, WD 1054-226 debris) was done.

## 5. Tidally locked, eyeball, habitable-zone planets and Earth analogs

### Takeaway (section 5)

Tidal locking is expected for planets orbiting inside about 0.1–0.3 au of M dwarfs. The HZ is a model-dependent insolation band, not an observation of habitability.

### Cited Findings (section 5)

- Kasting, Whitmire & Reynolds 1993 (Icarus 101, 108): conservative HZ from runaway greenhouse to maximum greenhouse — DOI [10.1006/icar.1993.1010](<https://doi.org/10.1006/icar.1993.1010>) [M].
- Kopparapu et al. 2013 (ApJ 765, 131): updated HZ for stars of 2600–7200 K; for the Sun about 0.99–1.70 au conservative — [arXiv:1301.6674](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.6674>) [M]. Kopparapu et al. 2014 corrected the mass-dependent limits for super-Earths [M].
- Pierrehumbert 2011 (ApJL 726, L8): the "eyeball" climate of a tidally locked planet with ice outside a liquid substellar ocean — [arXiv:1008.3911](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.3911>) [M]. Yang, Cowan & Abbot 2013 (ApJL 771, L45) stabilizing substellar cloud feedback widens the inner edge — [arXiv:1307.0515](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.0515>) [M]. Leconte et al. 2013 (Nature) 3D inner-edge case — [arXiv:1303.7094](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.7094>) [M].
- Barnes 2017 (Celestial Mechanics): tidal locking of habitable exoplanets — [arXiv:1608.06919](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06919>) [M].
- HZ limits: atmosphere-dependent; cloud feedback, CO2 weathering, mass, obliquity, water inventory, and for M dwarfs the pre-main-sequence luminosity phase and flares are not captured by the 1D limits (see Section 8). Zahnle & Catling 2017 "cosmic shoreline" (ApJ 843, 122) for atmosphere retention — [arXiv:1702.03386](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.03386>) [M].
- Earth analogs: Kepler-186f (Quintana et al. 2014, Science) about 1.1 Earth radii in the HZ of an M dwarf — [arXiv:1404.5667](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.5667>) [M]; Kepler-452b (Jenkins et al. 2015, AJ 150, 56) about 1.6 Earth radii, P = 385 d, G2 star, reliability debated — [arXiv:1507.06723](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.06723>) [M]. No confirmed Earth twin (Earth-size, Sun-like star, HZ, confirmed rocky) exists.
- Occurrence eta-Earth: Petigura et al. 2013 (PNAS) about 22% of Sun-like stars have 1–2 Earth-radius planets with P of 20–200 d — [arXiv:1311.6806](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.6806>) [M]; Bryson et al. 2021 (AJ 161, 36) ranges from about 0.37 to 0.60 per Sun-like star depending on the HZ definition — [arXiv:2010.14812](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14812>) [M, values approximate].

### Inferences (section 5)

- For the generator: a class "habitable-zone candidate" should be a flag (insolation band with spectral-type dependence), separate from a surface-liquid-water claim.

### Gaps (section 5)

- The Habitable Worlds Observatory target lists and the 2025–26 JWST HZ atmosphere results were not searched.

## 6. Highly eccentric, retrograde and misaligned planets

### Takeaway (section 6)

Hot Jupiters show a wide range of obliquities, which is correlated with stellar temperature; polar and retrograde orbits exist. Eccentric giants arise from scattering or Kozai-Lidov and secular interactions.

### Cited Findings (section 6)

- HD 80606 b (Naef et al. 2001): e = 0.93, P = 111 d, about 4 Jupiter masses, periastron about 0.03 au, first eccentric planet seen transiting (Moutou 2009); Laughlin et al. 2009 (Nature 457, 562) measured its periastron heating — [arXiv:0812.1583](<https://arxiv.org/abs/0812.1583>) [M]. Spin-orbit angle about 42 degrees (Hébrard et al. 2010) [M].
- Kozai migration: Wu & Murray 2003; Fabrycky & Tremaine 2007 (ApJ 669, 1298) — [arXiv:0705.4285](<https://arxiv.org/abs/0705.4285>) [M].
- Albrecht, Dawson & Winn 2022 (PASP 134, 082001) review of stellar obliquities, with the sample of Rossiter-McLaughlin and other measurements — [arXiv:2203.05460](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05460>) [M]. Winn et al. 2010 (ApJL 718, L145): hot Jupiters around stars cooler than about 6250 K are mostly aligned, hotter stars show misalignment — [arXiv:1006.4161](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.4161>) [M]. Albrecht et al. 2012 (ApJ 757, 18): tidal realignment — [arXiv:1206.6105](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6105>) [M]. Perpendicular ("polar") planets are over-represented among close-in Neptune-mass planets (Albrecht et al. 2021, AJ 162, 244) [M].
- Retrograde examples: HAT-P-7 b, WASP-17 b (Anderson et al. 2010), WASP-8 b [M].

### Inferences (section 6)

- A generator can offer inclination, eccentricity and obliquity as independent attributes; a stellar-temperature dependence for alignment is justified.

### Gaps (section 6)

- Precise values for each system not re-verified.

## 7. Young, forming planets, protoplanets, rings and exomoon candidates

### Takeaway (section 7)

Only a few protoplanets are confirmed in disk gaps (PDS 70 b, c). Exomoons and rings remain candidates.

### Cited Findings (section 7)

- PDS 70 b: Keppler et al. 2018 (A&A 617, A44), SPHERE, 5.4 Myr K-type star at about 113 pc, about 22 au, inside a transition-disk cavity, about 8 Jupiter masses at discovery (later estimates 2–5 Jupiter masses) — [arXiv:1806.11568](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.11568>) [M]. PDS 70 c: Haffert et al. 2019 (Nature Astronomy), MUSE H-alpha, about 34 au, in 2:1 resonance with b — [arXiv:1906.01486](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01486>) [M]. A circumplanetary disk around c: Isella et al. 2019 (ApJL 879, L25) [M]. Planet-mass and orbit values are approximate.
- AB Aurigae b, Currie et al. 2022 (Nature Astronomy), disk-embedded protoplanet candidate [M, unverified].
- J1407b ring system: Mamajek et al. 2012 (AJ 143, 72), a 56-day eclipse by a ring system with radius about 0.6 au around a young K5 star, Kenworthy & Mamajek 2015 (ApJ 800, 126) — [arXiv:1501.05652](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.05652>) [M]. The companion was never directly detected; eclipse did not recur.
- Kepler-1625 b-i: Teachey & Kipping 2018 (Sci. Adv. 4, eaav1784), Neptune-sized exomoon candidate, in HST data — [arXiv:1810.02362](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02362>) [M]. Heller et al. 2019 and Kreidberg et al. 2019 (ApJL 877, L15) reanalysed: the moon signal depends on detrending and is not robust [M]. Kepler-1708 b-i candidate (Kipping et al. 2022, Nature Astronomy) — [arXiv:2201.04643](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04643>) [M]. No exomoon confirmed as of this research.
- Low-density "super-puffs" with a possible ring explanation: Kepler-51 (Masuda 2014; Libby-Roberts et al. 2020 find hazes instead) [M].

### Inferences (section 7)

- Protoplanets and rings are time-limited phases, so the generator can treat them as an "age < 10 Myr" attribute, not as a type.

### Gaps (section 7)

- No exomoon confirmation search for 2025–26; Kepler-1625 literature after 2019 not reviewed.

## 8. Planets around M dwarfs

### Takeaway (section 8)

M dwarfs host abundant small planets with compact systems (TRAPPIST-1), in tight HZs, and with specific hazards: flares, early luminous phase, tidal locking, atmospheric loss.

### Cited Findings (section 8)

- TRAPPIST-1 (Gillon et al. 2017, Nature 542, 456): seven Earth-size planets around an M8 dwarf (0.09 solar masses) at 12 pc, P = 1.5–19 d, three in the HZ (e, f, g) — [arXiv:1703.01424](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.01424>) [M]. Masses and densities: Agol et al. 2021 (PSJ 2, 1) — [arXiv:2010.01074](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01074>) [M]. JWST thermal phase curves/secondary eclipses: TRAPPIST-1 b has no thick atmosphere and c a thin or no atmosphere (Greene et al. 2023; Zieba et al. 2023, Nature) [M, identifiers unchecked].
- Proxima b (Anglada-Escudé et al. 2016, Nature 536, 437): minimum mass 1.27 Earth masses (later about 1.07), P = 11.2 d, a = 0.049 au, in the HZ of the nearest star — [arXiv:1609.03449](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03449>) [M]. Proxima d: Faria et al. 2022 (A&A 658, A115) — [arXiv:2202.05188](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05188>) [M]. Proxima flaring: far-UV/X-ray flux about 10^2–10^3 of Earth's [M].
- Occurrence: Dressing & Charbonneau 2015 (ApJ 807, 45): 2.5 ± 0.2 planets of 0.5–4 Earth radii with P below 200 d per Kepler M dwarf — [arXiv:1501.01623](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01623>) [M]; Hardegree-Ullman et al. 2019 (AJ 158, 75) — [arXiv:1905.05900](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05900>) [M]. Giant planets rarer around M dwarfs.
- Cosmic shoreline: Zahnle & Catling 2017 (see Section 5). LHS 3844 b (Kreidberg et al. 2019, Nature) no atmosphere [M].

### Inferences (section 8)

- The generator should differentiate M-dwarf planets: compact multi-planet, tidally locked, with atmospheric loss risk; the HZ is at P = 5–30 d.

### Gaps (section 8)

- Whether the HZ M-dwarf planets keep atmospheres is open in late 2026; no 2025–26 JWST results were verified.
