# Planet viewer (web front-end)

Browser front-end of the map generator: a library of generated worlds, a 3D globe and a flat map that morph into each other, point inspection, measurements and side-by-side comparison, and a form that queues new generations on the server.

## Prerequisites

- Node 24 or later (`mise install` at the repository root installs the pinned version).
- The `mapgen-server` API, from `crates/server/`.

## Installation

```bash
mise run web:install
```

## Usage

Development, with hot reload: run the server and the Vite dev server together, then open <http://localhost:5173>. Vite forwards `/api`, `/worlds` and `/healthz` to the server on port 3000.

```bash
mise run dev
```

The interface is in English or French: the first visit follows the browser's languages (any `fr-*` gives French, anything else English), and the **EN / FR** selector in the header switches it at once and is remembered. Numbers, units and dates follow it (decimal comma and narrow no-break spaces in French). Biome names, generation stages, the request errors the server explains with a code, and the presets that carry a `[locales.fr]` table are translated too; the files a generation writes and the CLI stay in English. Keyboard shortcuts are the same in every language.

In the viewer:

- **Globe / Map** switches between the globe and the flat map, with an animated morph that keeps the place: the point at the centre of the screen stays there, at an equivalent zoom (the same scale near the ground, the whole map for the whole globe), and the camera follows it while the surface morphs. The map rolls round the globe like a sheet round a ball, the part at the centre of its view in front and its edges going back (`morphFrame` in `lib/viewer/viewTransfer.ts`, mirrored by the vertex shader); towards the map, the globe first turns to the meridian the map will be centred on, so that it does not open on the camera; under Sunlight the night side it may swing over is lit a little during the morph, so that the turn does not pass through black. The clipping planes follow the camera's distance to the morphing surface throughout, so that zoomed out the sheet is not clipped as the camera backs away while it unrolls. Zoomed out, the map is kept in view rather than half off screen.
- Drag to rotate the globe or pan the map, scroll to zoom towards the point under the cursor. On the globe the right button frees the camera, as in the star view: it pans on the screen's two axes, the globe then turning round the point reached and the wheel bringing the camera nearer that point or further from it, never into the ground; **Reset view** centres it on the planet again. Zooming goes from the whole planet down to about 300 m per pixel: on the biome layer, detail tiles rendered by the server stream in as you get closer (the coarser picture stays until they arrive, and a tile with nothing loaded above it also fetches its ancestor two levels up, so fast zooming shows something quickly). A tile that fails to load is retried after 2 s, then less and less often (up to once a minute); one the server refuses (a 4xx error) is not retried. On the globe, rotation slows down near the surface.
- Hovering shows the coordinates, biome and elevation under the pointer at the bottom of a wide viewer. On the globe the pointer's ray is marched against the relief as drawn (`rayRelief` in `lib/geo.ts`, with the shape and the displayed relief of `surfaceRadiusAt`), so a steep slope or a grazing view picks the point you see, not the one on the bare sphere; with no relief it is the sphere's hit. Detail tiles are not sampled: the base layer sets the surface picked.
- Click a point to see its coordinates, biome, elevation above the coastline, annual mean temperature in °C (worlds of format version 3 and older stored a `[0, 1]` scale, converted on load with the generator's Earth-fitted curve), moisture, drainage area and, on worlds with `raw_data` format version 2 or later, the distance to the sea, the volcanic zone, the floodplain weight and the shift of the mountain line. Near a river it also shows the river (flow there and at its mouth, length, where it ends, estuary or delta branch); with the hex grid on, the hex under the point. **Copy** copies the latitude and longitude in degrees. The panel opens centred under the toolbar, clear of the icon bar, the dock and the playback bar; drag it by its header to move it (kept inside the view and above the playback bar by `clampToView`, `infoPanelAt` in `lib/dock.ts`). Its place is remembered across points and sessions (setting `infoPanelAt`). On views narrower than 720 px it spans the bottom of the view instead and does not move.
- **Systems** (`docs/system-spec.md`): a planetary system is shown body by body, each under the id `systems/<system>/bodies/<body>` (its files under `/systems/…`, its tiles, epochs, thumbnail and hex grid under `/api/systems/<system>/bodies/<body>/…`; `systemBodyId`, `bodyFiles` and `bodyApi` in `lib/api/client.ts`). The world state loads the system (`GET /api/systems/{id}`, or its `system.json`) and shows the body asked, the one the system was drawn around, or its first planet (`loadSystem`, `showSystemBody` in `lib/world.svelte.ts`). **Body** then lists every body of the system, the star first, each planet followed by its moons, marking those not generated; `U` steps through the family of the planet shown. A body not generated yet is drawn from its preview, a lit sphere of its type's colours (a giant's bands, a rocky body's land and polar caps, lava glowing on a volcanic body's night side), and showing it queues its generation and its neighbourhood's (`POST /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/generate`): a bar under the toolbar names the body being made, its place in the job and its stage, and the maps replace the sphere when it ends. The sky, the light, the turn of the stars, the orbits and the labels come from the system (`lib/systemSky.ts`): every other body where its stored orbit puts it (Kepler's equation from its mean anomaly at the system's epoch, a moon in its planet's equatorial plane, which with its node, counted from the planet's equinox direction, is held fixed against the stars while the planet turns), turned by its stored spin (a locked body turning at a steady pace, facing what it orbits on average: that host swings in its sky by the libration, the true minus the mean anomaly, about ±2e radians), seen from the body shown; the other planets too, as small disks with their rings; the orbits of the planets round the star and of the moons of the shown body's planet. The clock is the season of the shown body's planet and the orbit time past it; the **Sun longitude** slider sets the hour of the body shown (the sun over that longitude at the season's start), after which it turns on itself at its own day. The star view shows the system (`lib/systemStar.ts`): every planet, the one shown included, as a sphere of its preview's colour on its orbit, listed under **Planets** with its type, mass, orbit and year (the one shown in bold); a generated planet's sphere is wrapped in its map (its thumbnail, at the system's revision), and one not generated yet in its preview, as the planet view draws it (a giant's belts and zones, a rocky body's land and polar caps; `lib/viewer/previewTexture.ts`). Hovering a planet (`StarView.onHoverPlanet`) rings it and opens its card beside it, linked to it by a line (`components/PlanetCard.svelte`, placed by `lib/planetCard.ts`: right of the planet, else left, kept on screen): its name and type, radius and mass, semi-major axis and eccentricity, current speed, year, day (or locked to its star), light (relative to Earth's), surface gravity, equilibrium temperature, place relative to the habitable zone (inside, or how far short of or beyond it), moons (each a link that opens it), and whether it is generated, with **Open** (or **Open and generate**). With two or more cards pinned, each line's highest value among them is marked ▲ and its lowest ▼ (`extremes` in `lib/planetCard.ts`), and **Unpin all** closes them. The card closes when the pointer leaves both the planet and the card, unless it is pinned. A click or a tap on a planet **selects** it and pins its card; a second click lets it go and unpins it; no planet is selected when a system opens. The card's pin button pins it too, and pinned cards stay open side by side, follow their planets as they move and keep their rings. Pins last while the system is shown (`pinnedPlanets` in `lib/world.svelte.ts`) and are kept in the address (`pins=c,e`), so a reload or a shared link restores them: opening a planet and coming back restores them, another system clears them. A double click on a planet, its card's button, or picking it in **Body**, flies to it and shows it; coming back from it selects it. The selected planet keeps the extras of a world's star view on its own sphere, and its facts fill the dock's *Selected planet* panel (a hint when none is): its orbit line, and with **Distances** its periapsis and apoapsis, the zone's edges and its live distance, speed and light. A row of the **Planets** list selects or lets go that planet as a click does, and the pin beside it pins or unpins its card alone. Every planet has its moons, on orbits not to scale round its sphere and scaled to it, at their real pace on the system's clock; a giant's rings are drawn round it to scale with their gaps when its system drew them, and its ring moons to scale among them, in their plane, each in its gap or at their edge (a gap is cut wide enough for the moon's marker, `ringBands` and `moonOrbits` in `lib/viewer/starSystem.ts`), the other moons beyond them, and every orbit is tilted by its inclination to the star's equator about its line of nodes (`orbitPoint` in `StarView.ts`): a degree or two for most planets, so the system stays nearly flat. Each planet is labelled with its name, on the side of the planet away from its velocity arrow so as not to hide it. **Lines to the star** (on by default) draws a dotted line from the star to each planet, its distance now written along it; off, the name label gives the mean distance instead (`MGS 48213 c · 1.5 AU`). **Velocities** (on by default) draws each planet's velocity: an arrow from the planet along its orbit, the way it goes, as long as its speed on one scale for the whole system (the fastest planet at its periapsis 0.7 view units long, `velocityScale`), labelled with its speed now in km/s. **Star's pull** (on by default) draws the star's pull on each planet, `G M / r²`: an arrow from the planet towards the star along its line, as long as the pull on one scale (the strongest 0.5 view units long, `gravityScale`, a few pixels at least, since the pull falls with the square of the distance), its value written along the line (the Sun's on Earth is 5.9 mm/s²). All follow the animation. With **Distances** and **Habitable zone**, a dashed line crosses the zone between its two edges, with its width and, in brackets, its area (`π (outer² − inner²)`, in AU²). A planetary system is laid out on one logarithmic scale of distance (`systemLogScale`, `lib/viewer/starSystem.ts`): from just outside the star to the farthest orbit or edge of the habitable zone, each doubling of the distance takes the same room (about 1.8 view units, less in a system spread over more than 5.5 e-folds, so that it fits one camera view), so a compact inner system (planets at 0.07, 0.09 and 0.15 AU) is as readable as giants tens of AU out, and the habitable zone keeps its place as a band; the one scale serves orbits, belts, trojans, Lagrange points and Hill spheres, and does not change with the planet selected. The markers are not to scale: each is drawn at most as large as its own size and at most 0.4 of the gap to the nearest orbit (never under 0.035 view units; picking has its own tolerance), so markers never touch (`markerSizes`); the gap is the distance between neighbouring orbit curves (periapsis to apoapsis, `orbitGaps`), and everything a planet draws round its body (rings, highlight halo, moon orbits) is held within half of it, so nothing reaches another planet's orbit line. To scale, the velocity and pull arrows are a share of the planet's drawn orbit radius (a velocity arrow at the planet's mean speed is a quarter of it), not the schematic's single length. The star card says so in the legend (`starView.systemScale`). The world's own star view (no planetary system) keeps its linear scale with a dashed circle where distances start being compressed, labelled with that distance. **Orbit** hides every planet's orbit line; the selected planet's is drawn thick, each pinned or hovered planet's thick and bright in its own colour until it is let go, as are the velocity and pull arrows (fat lines, `Line2`, a few pixels wide whatever the zoom). **Frost line** (on by default) draws a dashed blue circle where water ice condensed in the disk the planets formed from, `2.7 √L` AU (`frostLineAu`), labelled with its distance. **Hill spheres** (off by default) draws each planet's Hill sphere, how far its gravity holds moons against the star's, `a (1 − e) ∛(m / 3M)` at periapsis (`hillRadiusAu`), as a grid sphere (parallels and meridians, `sphereGrid`) on the view's scale; one smaller than the planet's marker or than its outermost moon's ring (both drawn larger than to scale) is drawn just outside them, dashed, as not to scale (`hillShownRadius`), so that the moons always sit inside it. **Belts** (on by default) draws the system's belts (`system.json`'s `belts`, see the main README) as clouds of points on their orbits, turning at Kepler's pace with the system's clock, their Kirkwood gaps left empty, rocky ones sandy and icy ones bluish, each labelled with its name and span; far denser than real belts, whose bodies lie about a million km apart (`lib/viewer/smallBodies.ts`, `smallBodiesLayer.ts`). **Trojans** (on by default) draws each planet's trojans as two swarms round its L4 and L5 points, 60° ahead of and behind it, on tadpoles from about 30° to 95° from the planet, at least 4 % of its orbit wide (most would be a line), moving with it; their number of points grows with the logarithm of the count, and the swarms of the selected and pinned planets are labelled with their counts. **Lagrange points** (off by default) marks the selected planet's five Lagrange points, L4 and L5 green, the unstable L1, L2 and L3 red, L1 and L2 kept clear of its marker (they lie within its Hill sphere). The *Planets* panel lists the belts (span, mass, composition, the planet whose resonances shape them, the gaps) and the planets' trojans under *Small bodies*. The labels of the small bodies come last in the layout and hide first. **To scale** (off by default) draws the system to scale and fits it again: distances linear from the star's centre, uncompressed, and the star, its glow and the planets their true size (mostly under a pixel), their moons hidden; a vertical pin rises from the star and from each planet, its name slanted at the tip, so that close bodies can still be told apart. **Date**, a time bar under the speed, moves the system to any day up to its longest year, **Day 1** back to the start, and *<planet> at periapsis* to the next day the selected planet is closest to the star; the animation goes on from there. Labels are kept apart (`lib/viewer/labelLayout.ts`): the names first (the selected planet's, then from the star out), then the distances, the speeds and the pulls; a level label moves up or down by a line or two to a free place, a label along a line stays on it, and either is hidden when no place is left, as on the crowded inner orbits of a fifteen-planet system. They also keep clear of the apsides and zone edges labels. The system has one scale and one clock, whichever planet is shown: distances are linear out to just beyond the habitable zone (`systemCompressedFrom`), then compressed, so showing another planet moves neither the orbits, the zone nor the camera. **Animate** runs every planet at its real pace; **Speed** sets how fast, in Earth days per second, from 1 (a day a second, the slowest) to 4,096 by powers of two (`SYSTEM_SPEED_MIN`, `SYSTEM_SPEED_MAX`); a system opens with its innermost planet going round in about 10 seconds (`defaultSystemSpeed`), and the clock line gives the days per second and the day since the animation started. The viewer generates a body only while it shows it: the star view leaves every planet a sphere.
- **Body** (the list at the top left) picks the body of the system the viewer shows, the star first, then each planet from the star out followed by its moons, nearest first, under its name and its type, those not generated yet marked (see **Systems** above). `U` steps through the family of the planet shown (the planet and its moons), `O` switches to the star and back. Every generated body is shown as a world is, with its detail tiles and crater epochs (`GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/tiles/…` and `…/epoch/…`); a giant as a giant world. The card on the left describes the body shown. A giant's card gives its rings as its system drew them (bright or faint, their edges in its radii, their gaps); a ring moon is listed as a *ring moon*, *shepherd moon*, *icy moon, resonant with the rings* or *small inner moon* (a small moon beyond a faint ring, the source of a dust ring), and its card says what it does in the rings: the gap it clears and its width in km, the edge its resonance cuts and the division it clears outward of it, the edge it keeps, or the dust it may feed (`lib/ringMoons.ts`; backlog item 18). A giant's dust rings (fed by its moons, see the main README) are drawn in its planet view as broad, smooth, faint annuli, densest along their source moon's orbit, their depth held at the camera's far plane so that they are not cut (`setDustRings`, `dustRingFragment` in `lib/viewer/ringShaders.ts`); the giant's card lists them and the source moon's card says what it feeds. A moonlet's own gap, a few tens of km wide, would be under a pixel from afar while the moonlet is drawn a few pixels across: gaps are drawn at least as wide as that marker on screen (`uGapPixels` in `ringFragment`, `uRingGapLeast` in `skyRingDensityGlsl`), so that each moonlet shows inside its gap. Showing a ring moon under **Sunlight**, in high quality, draws the boulders of the ring round it (`setRingRocks`, `lib/ringRocks.ts`, `ringRockShaders.ts`): low rocks of the ring's colour, lit by the sun and dark in the giant's shadow, as many as the ring's optical depth where they are, shaped by the same ringlets as the sheet (denser in a bright ringlet, up to about 1.5 times the ring's mean, and never above 0.95), none within a gap's width (sharp-edged, as Saturn's Encke and Keeler gaps are, where the sheet seen from afar fades into its gaps), their sizes on the power law of ring particles up to 12 m (3 m in a faint ring). Each goes round the giant at its own Keplerian pace, so the rocks inside the moon's orbit pass it and those outside fall behind, as the clock runs. They come from a lattice fixed in the ring, cells of 10 km round the giant's centre: the 61 × 61 cells round the moon are drawn, cells leaving that window on one side as others enter it on the other, each hashed from its row and place so that it comes back the same; the window is round, its rocks fading out from 210 to 300 km from the moon. Beyond them lies the ring's grain (`ringGrainVertex`): points a pixel wide from a coarser lattice of the same kind (cells of 30 km, out to 900 km), none in the gaps, fading in where the rocks fade out, fewer with the square of the distance and fading out from 540 to 900 km into the ring's sheet. Each point stands for the rocks' mean cross-section over its share of the ring, drawn as faint as that cross-section over its pixel and lit as a rock is on average at that phase angle, so that the ring's light per km² goes on from the boulders to the grain to the sheet with no edge, whatever the camera's distance. True boulders are under a pixel beyond a few km: **Ring rocks**, in the *Map* panel, draws them at true size, enlarged ×10, ×30 or ×100 (the default, which shows the ring as a belt; not to scale), or hides them. Under a pixel, a rock is drawn half a pixel wide and as faint as its true size makes it.
- A **moon**, on a world with moons, is shown in the planet view: each moon is a world of its own (generated with its planet, see the main README), so every layer and tool works on it as on the planet (globe and map, detail tiles, the crater time slider, measuring, screenshots), except the hex grid. An irregular moon is drawn with its shape (`raw_data/shape.png`), its long axis towards its planet, lit by the slopes of that shape. Its facts are in its body card (see **The cards** below): its orbit, its size, with its three diameters when it is irregular or stretched by its planet's tides, and its day. The world card counts the giant planets and gives their impact rate, counts the moons, gives the tides (relative to Earth's) and whether a large moon steadies the axial tilt, and keeps describing the planet while a moon is shown. In the star view, the moons circle the planet on orbits not to scale (one after the other, the largest drawn larger), at their real pace relative to the year (two seconds a turn at the fastest), retrograde ones backwards; a binary planet swings around its barycentre.
- **Giant planets** (the `gas-giant`, `ice-giant` and `hot-jupiter` types, see the main README) show their cloud tops: the globe flattened by the spin; the biome layer as the bands (bright zones of high clouds, dark belts of lower ones), storms and polar regions in the giant's own colours; **Cloud tops** (the elevation layer) as their heights, on a ramp of their own from the deep belts in dark rust through the tan cloud deck and the cream zones to the white storm tops, the legend reading "deep belts" to "high storms"; **Temperature** on a range of the giant's own (within 25 K of its effective temperature, or a hot giant's night to day side, the legend saying which); **Wind** (the moisture layer) on a ramp from westward blue through calm white to eastward red, the legend giving the strongest; the other layers are disabled. Rings are drawn in the equatorial plane, with their ringlets and gaps, shadowed by the planet under **Sunlight**, and casting their own shadow on the globe: a band of the winter hemisphere, darkened where the rings are dense, with their gaps letting the light through. **Band drift** (in place of the clouds) turns each band with its own wind, sampled every 2° so that narrow jets keep their direction: neighbouring bands turn east and west in turn, as on Jupiter, the fastest round in two minutes. Storms drift whole, each at the wind of its centre, with their halo, as a vortex keeps its shape; a great spot, the largest storm, drifts slowly westward instead (3.5 % of the fastest band, the Great Red Spot's 0.36° a day against Jupiter's fastest jets): they are found in the biome map (`lib/giantStorms.ts`, patches of the storm biome, the 16 largest), and where the bands would bring a sheared copy of one the band just beside it shows instead. The map repeats across longitudes and is read with the derivatives of the undrifted coordinates, so no line shows at the seam however far the bands have turned. A hot giant's night side glows red (above about 1 100 K, the more the hotter). Sea and altitude contours are off; there are no detail tiles. The world card gives its mass, radius and density, day and flattening, clouds and effective temperature, jets and strongest winds, internal heat, rings and storms, its poles (a cluster of cyclones, a hexagon or a haze, north and south), instead of the sea level, erosion, atmosphere, geology and land share; the star view draws the world as the giant it is, in its colours, with its rings.
- **Moons of giants** (see the main README): the giant hangs in the sky over the face the world keeps towards it (longitude 180°), with its bands (its own world in `host/`) at its true size (from a few degrees to over 15°), lit by the sun so it shows its phases, and its shadow falls on the world when it passes in front of the sun (eclipses), under **Sunlight**. A ringed giant keeps its rings there (as its system drew them, else as the world stores its giant): each pixel's ray meets their plane where it meets it (`skyRingFragment` in `lib/viewer/ringShaders.ts`), at that point's depth, so they pass in front of the giant and behind it, and round a ring moon in front of its globe and behind it, with their gaps and the same ringlets as the giant's own view (the radial profile `ringlets` and its seed `ringSeed` are shared by `ringFragment` and `skyRingFragment`; each pixel's span along the radius is worked out from its neighbours' rays, as `fwidth` is undefined in the sky shader's branches, and fine ringlets fade towards their mean once they are under two pixels, so distant rings do not shimmer; seen edge on, the line is dimmed where its ray skims a gap and by the ringlets' mean (about a quarter dimmer than a uniform sheet); opened up, from about 7 degrees over their plane, they are as opaque as in the giant's own view, so the ringlets show (fainter than the slanted sheet seen before, which made them a solid disk); the ring rocks and grain round a ring moon follow the same ringlets, `ringlets` in `ringRockShaders.ts`), lit on the face the sun shines on and faint through themselves on the other, darkened in the giant's shadow, and casting their own shadow on its disk as a band. A regular moon orbits in the giant's equatorial plane and sees them edge on: real rings, a few km thick, would vanish, so they are kept a line about two pixels wide across the giant, as dense as they are seen along their length; from an inclined orbit they open into an ellipse. From a ring moon, which stands over them, they lie along the horizon past the moon's own gap, and spread below it (or above) as the camera rises over their plane (or sinks under it). Seen nearly edge on they are held at most 55 % opaque (closer than about 9° to their plane, easing back to their full opacity by 27°), a choice rather than physics: dense rings would otherwise veil half the sky uniformly and hide the giant behind them. **Labels** (on by default, under **Sunlight**) names the bodies of the sky and gives their basic facts, each label tied to its body by a thin line, so that a moon a pixel wide can still be found: the moons from their planet (name, radius, orbit: *12 km radius · orbit 178,364 km*), from a moon its planet (name, radius, and its distance from the moon now, centre to centre, as the host line gives it) and the other moons, from a world orbiting a giant the giant (designation, mass, distance) and its other moons, and the star (designation and type, distance); the names are those given on the world card, else the designations; the labels are laid out together so that none covers another (`lib/labelLayout.ts`): each hangs up and to the right of its point, and moves down, to the left, or further up or down in a stack, when that place is taken or off the view (seen near a ring plane, the moons, the host line and the arrows line up along it), its leader still tying it to its point; a body hidden by the globe or behind the camera has none. **Per-body switches** (from a moon, or a world orbiting a giant, the planet hangs in the sky): each icon-bar switch that applies to a body gets two small squares beside its main button, one for the moon (the body shown) and one for its host planet. The main button sets both to the opposite of the moon's state; a square flips only its body and shows its own state (`aria-pressed`, named *Grid on the moon*, *Grid on the host*; logic in `lib/switches.ts`). They cover **Atmosphere**, **Clouds**, **Drift** (band drift on a giant), **Grid**, **Tropics**, **Spin axes** and **Spin direction**; the host's halo, clouds (with their own drift), grid, tropics, spin axis and spin arrow are drawn on it as a sky body (`SkyBodyShow`, `skyBodyFragment`). The host has no elevation data in the moon's view, so the depth and elevation contours have no square and its band layer has no drift: a giant host's **Drift** and **Clouds** squares are disabled (its bands are in its surface map, drawn static), and so are the atmosphere's when the host has no air. **Host rings** is a full-width switch (only the host has rings to switch). The system-wide switches (**Labels**, **Orbits**, **Small bodies**) get a *local* square (the host planet and its moons; a moon's orbit lines, the planet's own trojans) and a *stellar* one (the rest of the star system: the star, the other planets and their orbits, the belts), shown whenever the body has a local group, from a planet with moons too; their main button sets both. Switches with a single target stay full width: Sunlight, Star light, Stars, Hill sphere and **Link to the host**. **Compass** is the rotation dial (moon square: the body shown's) and the host compass (host square); off a moon it is the rotation dial alone. The **Drift** and **Band drift** rows also have a small *Reset the drift* button (`PlanetView.resetDrift`): bands, storms, clouds and the host's clouds go back to where they were generated. Hexes is not in the icon bar: its on/off is the checkbox at the top of the *Hexes* panel. With the bar collapsed to icons the squares shrink to two 14 px dots stacked beside the icon. The squares' states are remembered (`hostGrid`, `hostSpinAxes`, `orbitLinesLocal`, …); a value saved before the split (`spinAxes`, one switch) is copied to the new ones (`migrateDisplay`), and the old *Host planet* dock panel is gone. The **Compass** host square draws the rotation dial round the host planet: its ring and pointer, the star's half-circle and the moon alone on it, at its bearing and height over the host's equator, labelled (`hostCompass` in `lib/rotationDial.ts`, `PlanetView.setHostCompass`; the dial's own code is unchanged, held at the far plane like the host's spin arrow). A body locked to its planet turns once per orbit against the stars, its planet fixed in its sky on average, swinging by the libration (the gap between the true and the mean anomaly, nil on a circular orbit): on a moon, and on a world orbiting a giant, the star field turns with the orbit as the orbit slider or its animation moves it, so the other moons are seen to move against the stars rather than to circle the moon. A captured moon is not locked: it spins once per day of its own, 6 to 24 hours (drawn by its seed, as `world.json` does not store it; the irregular moons of the giants turn in 7 to 12 hours), so from it the planet rises and sets and the stars turn faster, and from its planet it shows each of its sides in turn. The giant turns on itself too, at its day against the world's orbit, its storms with it. The world card gives the giant (kind, mass, distance in its radii and km), the day (the orbit around it) and its size in the sky, and the tidal heating, flagged inside the habitable edge; the star view draws the giant on the world's orbit, in its colours and with its rings, and the world going round it.
- **The star** (in **Body**, or `O`, which switches back too) shows the world's star and its system. The star is a globe you can turn and zoom: its colour from its temperature (white-balanced on the Sun), its limb darker than its centre (more on cool stars), convective granules (none on hot stars, above about 7 500 K, whose outer layers do not convect), spots scattered over the whole surface with bright faculae around them (many on M dwarfs, fewer on K, a few on G and F, none on hotter stars), prominences (arcs of plasma held above the surface by its magnetic field, glowing in the red of hydrogen, each a bundle of twelve thin strands pinned near the surface, spreading and winding along the arch, with knots of gas draining down its legs; seen against the disk, a prominence is a dark filament, its cooler gas absorbing the light of the surface behind: seven on M dwarfs, five on K, four on G, two on F, none on hotter stars, placed by the world's seed) and a corona glow. Around it, in the orbital plane: the habitable zone as a green ring, the planet's orbit (an ellipse with the star at a focus, oriented by the world's seed) and the planet on it, the star not to scale but the distances in proportion. **Distances** draws a line from the star to the planet labelled with their distance, the planet's speed at that point (vis-viva: faster near the star), the light the planet gets there (relative to Earth's) and whether it is inside the habitable zone at that moment, marks the periapsis and apoapsis with their distances (a circular orbit has neither: the panel says so, with its distance) and labels the zone's edges; the labels follow the view. **Orbit** and **Habitable zone** hide or show the orbit line and the green ring (and their labels); **Prominences** hides or shows the arcs. Worlds made before eccentric orbits show a circle. The giant planets of the system (see the main README) are drawn on their orbits, each in its own colour (cream to ochre Jovians, blue ice giants, white water-cloud giants, dark hot Jupiters), larger than the world's marker as they are larger, ringed giants with a tilted ring, labelled by their designation (`MGS 46433 d`), and moving at Kepler's pace with their years in proportion to the world's (a Jupiter beside an Earth turns twelve times slower); beyond the world's orbit and the habitable zone, marked by a dashed circle, distances are compressed (logarithmically) so that giants tens of AU out fit in without shrinking the world's system, which keeps its scale. The left button turns the view and the wheel zooms; the right button frees the camera, panning it on the screen's two axes (the target moves with it; **Reset view** brings it back to the star). A gizmo in the top right corner shows the world's axes as the camera sees them, x red, y green, z blue (`components/AxesGizmo.svelte`, `lib/viewer/axes.ts`), in the planet view too, where on the globe they are the axes of the frame the camera stays in while the clock plays: the sky's, still while the body turns under them, or the body's with **Camera turns with the body** (`gizmoFrame`). Opening a planet from the star view flies the camera to it, about a second eased in and out, before its view opens; going back to the star view flies out from it to the whole system (`StarView.flyToPlanet`, `flyOutFrom`); a press, the wheel or a key ends a flight at once. The view opens framing the whole system, the farthest giant included, and frames it again when another world's system is shown, until you turn or zoom it; **Reset view** frames it again. **Giants** hides or shows them. The star view's data sits in a **dock** on the right (`components/dock/Dock.svelte`): a tab per panel, *The star* (its spectral type and what it means, a G2V being a yellow dwarf like the Sun, its surface temperature, luminosity, radius and mass relative to the Sun, its metallicity `[Fe/H]` and the share of the Sun's metals, its lifetime on the main sequence, the habitable zone in AU, and the notes on the scale), *The planet* (the orbit, its place relative to the zone, the eccentricity with the closest and farthest distances, the orbital speed, mean and at periapsis and apoapsis, Earth's being 29.8 km/s, the year and the equilibrium temperature without greenhouse effect) and *Planets* or *Giant planets* (each planet or giant, the eccentricity the giants force and the impact rate they set, or why there are none). One panel is open in the dock at a time; its tab again closes it, and the chevron folds the dock to its icons. The arrow in a panel's header takes it out into a **floating card** over the view, dragged by its header and kept inside the view, which the dock icon puts back. Which panel is open, whether the dock is folded and where the floating cards are is remembered per kind of viewer (`mapgen.dock.star`, `lib/dock.ts`); views narrower than 720 px have no floating cards and show icon-only tabs. The **display switches** are an icon bar on the left (`ToggleBar.svelte`), each named in its tooltip, in groups: lines (orbits, habitable zone, prominences, giants), measures (distances, lines to the star, velocities, star's pull) and structure (frost line, Hill spheres, to scale); the chevron under it shows each switch's short name beside its icon (remembered). **Time** is a bar at the bottom, like a video player's (`PlaybackBar.svelte`): play / pause, the time bar with the day, *Day 1* and *to periapsis*, and the speed in days per second; a lone world's bar has the play button and the clock line only. Each label explains itself in a tooltip. **Animate** (off by default) turns and boils the star, makes red dwarfs flare now and then, sways the prominences and, on M, K and G stars, launches an eruption every nine seconds (an arc that rises, stretches, tears into separate strands and fades), and moves the planet round its orbit, a turn a minute at Kepler's pace (faster near the star), its distances updated live, thirty frames a second at most on the display's clock (a stall, such as the next world loading, pauses the motion rather than making the planet jump). A line under the checkboxes tells how fast time runs (`1 s = 4.5 days (×386,200)` for a 268-day year) and the day of the year since the periapsis; the speeds shown stay real ones, so in a second of animation the planet covers its speed times that factor. Off, the view is drawn only when it moves. Switching worlds keeps the current system on screen until the next one's metadata is in, and restarts the planet from the same point of its orbit. The planet view pauses meanwhile. Worlds made before stars show the Sun and Earth.
- The tools in the top-left corner: **Reset view** (whole planet, north up), **Measure** (click points to measure a path of great-circle legs, in km on this planet), **Screenshot** (the view as a PNG) and **?** (the keyboard shortcuts, then a table of the less common units: Ga and Ma for ages, bar for pressure, g for gravity, AU for distances to the star, L☉ for its luminosity, each with reference values). The same units are explained in a tooltip wherever they appear, on the world card (gravity, atmosphere, geology) and in the generation form (pressure, surface age): values with such a tooltip are underlined with dots.
- **Time slider**, under the tools, on worlds that offer other epochs (no liquid water today, made with impact craters, `raw_data` format version 7): it moves the surface through time, from its oldest surviving surface (4 Ga on `mercury-like`; back to the end of the wet era on worlds that had water, 3.5 Ga on `mars-like`) to 1 Ga ahead, the present in between. Going back, the craters younger than the epoch disappear and the others look fresher; going ahead, craters still to come appear. The label reads "3.8 Ga ago", "present" or "in 250 Ma" (Ma and Ga explained in its tooltip); the stops are 1 Ma apart within 100 Ma of the present, 10 Ma within 1 Ga, 50 Ma beyond. While the slider moves, the base map shows the epoch at a quarter of its resolution (after a pause of 120 ms) and the detail tiles are hidden; once released, the full map, the detail tiles, the relief, the contours and the point information follow the epoch. **Present** goes back to it. The biome statistics of the world card, the hex grid and the comparison stay at the present. The epoch chosen is kept when another world is shown, each world showing its nearest stop (the present on one without epochs). See `GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/epoch/...` in the repository README.
- **The planet viewer's chrome** is the star view's (see below): a **dock** on the right with its panels, *Info* (the body's card and world sheet, when the app gives them), *Map* (globe or flat map, the layer, the **Relief**: an exaggeration of the true relief, from 0 (flat) to ×10, ×1 by default; ×1 gives the heights the world was made with, one elevation unit being 25 km as the generator calibrates it (`ELEVATION_KM` in `lib/relief.ts`, `craters::ELEVATION_KM` in the generator), in radii of the body shown, and is marked by a tick on the slider's track; **True relief** goes back to it. Displacement and the slope shading of the data layers follow the same scale, the shading over the span of a screen pixel at least so that it does not shimmer when the map is minified. A relief saved on the former, arbitrary scale (where 1 was about 15 times the true relief of an Earth-sized world) starts at ×1), *Light and season* (the season, the sun's longitude and what the sun is over, the orbit time and a moon's sun), *Epoch* (the time slider, when the world has epochs), *Drift* (on worlds with plate tectonics: a slider of the epoch of the plates, from 300 Ma ago to 300 Ma ahead in steps of 10 Ma, under which a low-resolution preview of the whole world at that epoch is drawn, 256 pixels wide while the slider moves and 512 once it rests; **Present** goes back, **Generate at this epoch** opens the generation form on the same planet with `drift_ma`, and the world is added next to the present one; the form shows the epoch among the fixed parameters and keeps it when a drifted world is varied), and *Hexes* (the grid's columns, river threshold and download), each able to float over the view; the **icon bar** on the left (light: sunlight, starlight, atmosphere, stars; surface: clouds and their drift, or a giant's band drift, host rings, grid, tropics, depth and elevation contours; sky: labels, orbit lines, Hill sphere, small bodies, spin axes, spin direction, link to the host, compass; see *Per-body switches*); and the **playback bar** at the bottom, whose single clock in days moves the seasons, the hour and the moons together (play / pause, the day of the planet's year, *Back to day 1*, the speed, `S` playing it too). The biome legend sits at the bottom left, right of the icon bar, its list scrolling past a dozen biomes or so. Drag it with the mouse from anywhere on it to move it (a press that moves 5 px is a drag, else a click on its title or rows; it stays inside the view and is remembered, `mapgen.legendAt`); a double click off its buttons puts it back. The colour ramp legends of the other layers move with it. With **Orbits** on, the body shown's own orbit is dashed, every other dash bright and the others dim rather than gaps (every 1.5 of its radii at rest, `SKY_ORBIT_DASH`; a fainter solid line where the dashes would be under a few pixels), so that the body is seen sliding along it. While the orbits play, the dashes lengthen with the pace (`skyOrbitDash`): at the default speed a moon goes round its orbit in 10 s, often hundreds or thousands of its radii a second, and dashes of a fixed length would stream past or blur into a solid line; instead at most six dashes a second go past any point of the line, whatever the speed (`SKY_ORBIT_DASHES_PER_SECOND`), and where a dash would be longer than three screens (a fast orbit seen near, `SKY_ORBIT_PLAIN_SCREENS`) the line is drawn plain. What each switch and setting does:
- **Small bodies** (in the icon bar, sky group; off by default, shown when the system has belts or trojans): the system's small bodies are far too small to see from a planet, so this marks where they are in the sky (on the globe, in sunlight; `smallBodiesSky` in `lib/systemSky.ts`). Each planet's trojan swarms get a dashed ring at their L4 and L5 points, 60° ahead of the planet and behind it round the star (an equilateral triangle with the star and the planet, eccentric orbit or not), labelled *L4 trojans of MGS 97897 e · about 66,000 bodies over 1 km*. Each belt (asteroid belt, Hildas, Kuiper belt, scattered disk; see the star view) is traced by its two edges, circles round the star in its equatorial plane, drawn faint and warm like the orbit lines (`setSkyBelts`), and labelled with its span in AU where it crosses the view, each belt's label at its own place along the band; when the body shown orbits within a belt, its label says so (*you are inside it*). Labels follow the bands and swarms as the camera turns (`setSkyMarks`, `marks` in `skyScreenPositions`).
- **Hill sphere** (in the icon bar, sky group; off by default, shown for a body of a system, kept with the other view switches): draws the Hill sphere, `a (1 − e) ∛(m / 3M)` at the periapsis (the bound of the orbits of the body's moons), of the body shown round the star, or from a moon its host planet's, round the planet as it stands in the moon's sky. A grid and a faint shell lit towards its limb; the camera is usually inside it, since a body's moons orbit well within. With the switch on, the globe camera may go out far enough to see the whole sphere from outside (`hillReach` in `lib/systemSky.ts`: the sphere's farthest point from the body over the sine of the view's 20° half field, plus a tenth, in steps of 10 %, so about 3.2 Hill radii; a planet's is tens to hundreds of its radii, a giant's hundreds), where the usual limit is 8 radii (`GLOBE_MAX_DISTANCE`) or three times the farthest moon's orbit (`systemReach`), whichever is greater; the greatest of these is the limit (`PlanetView.setGlobeReach`). The wheel scales the altitude by a fixed factor per step, so the surface-to-Hill-sphere trip takes a few tens of steps whatever its length. The sky is drawn from the camera's real position (the bodies with a position, the star, the orbits, the Hill sphere and the labels), so the parallax of the moons and the orbits stay right far out; the clipping planes follow the altitude (`cameraPlanes`: near a tenth of it, far a few radii past the centre), so the near and far planes keep a ratio near 10 and the globe, the rings and the lines do not z-fight at any distance. Switching to the map from there brings the camera back to the map's own height range through the morph. Its depth is held at the far plane, so the globe hides it and the star, the rings and the orbit lines are never cut by it; the sky bodies drawn at their size hide the parts of it behind them (`skyHiddenChunk`, as for the orbit lines), so the far half of the host planet's sphere is hidden by the planet's disc and its near half passes in front. The labels of the sky bodies, the star and the marks (`hiddenBySkyDisc` in `lib/viewer/occlusion.ts`) are hidden likewise while a nearer sky body's disc covers them. The switch's help gives the radius in km and in planet radii (the Earth's is about 1.5 million km, 235 radii).
- **Spin direction** (in the icon bar; **Spin axes** draws the axes alone) shows how the body shown turns (`lib/viewer/bodyGuides.ts`): a solid curved arrow round its north axis, the way it turns, labelled with its speed at the equator and its day; a body locked to what it orbits gets a closed ring instead, labelled as locked, with its turn per orbit. On a moon of a planetary system, **Link to the host** draws its host planet: a dotted line from its surface all the way to the planet, never cut by the camera's far plane (its depth is held at that plane, so the globe still hides it), labelled with their distance centre to centre, the same as the planet's own sky label gives: at a fixed point of the line, on the axis, wherever the camera goes: one diameter of the moon beyond its surface towards the planet (the surface plus 2 radii), or the tip of the pull arrow when that lies farther out, hidden where the moon stands in front of it (`hostLineRadii`, `hostLabelRadius`), the planet's pull on it (an orange arrow towards the planet, in m/s²) and its orbital velocity (a cyan arrow along its orbit, from vis-viva, in km/s). Both arrows are drawn as the spin arrow is, a tube and a cone.
- **Rotation dial** (the moon square of **Compass** in the icon bar, sky group; off by default, remembered) draws a ring round the body shown's equator, with a tick every 5° and a numbered tick every 30°, drawn on the globe of a body of a system only (not on lone worlds, nor in the map view) (`lib/rotationDial.ts`, `lib/viewer/rotationDial.ts`), with a thin pointer from a meridian of the body. Standing on the ring at the star's bearing, a vertical half-circle graduated every 10° from −90° to +90° carries the star's marker (a soft gold, not the star's glare) at its height above the equator; the bodies of the planet's own system (from a moon, its planet and the other moons; from a planet, its moons; other planets are left out) each get a fainter arc with their marker at their height, the host only by default (the host's is a half-circle from −90° to +90° like the star's, its marker sliding along it, so that it does not flip between the top and the bottom as the libration of a moon makes its height cross 0; a moon's is a quarter-circle from the ring up or down): the *Light and season* panel lists them under *On the dial* to tick or untick (per system, for the session). When the dial comes on with the camera within 10° of the equator, where the ring, its markers and the guides all fall on one line, the camera rises to 20° above it, travelling there along its arc (same distance and azimuth, about 0.7 s, eased; a press, the wheel or a key ends it at once), and *Reset view* travels to the whole planet seen from there too. A free body's ring is graduated against the stars, 0° at its equinox, the pointer starting from longitude 0 and reading how far the body has turned (`137°`); a body locked to what it orbits has its ring graduated against its host (the planet for a moon, the star for a planet), 0° at the host, the pointer starting from the face turned to it (longitude 180°) and reading the angle the libration swings it by, signed (`+0.3°`). Its numbers stand outside the ring and the half-circle (`DialLabels.svelte`); the pointer's reading, the star (by its name, with its height) and each body's marker are labelled with the other labels of the sky, laid out so that none covers another (`SkyLabels.svelte`). With the spin axes, the line to the host and the host's pull point the same way: their label is one, on the line (*MGS 82899 d*, *139 144 km · pull 4.25 m/s²*), the host's marker on the dial only giving its height. Every label near the body is hidden where the body stands in front of it, by its true shape and its relief as displayed rather than a sphere (`hiddenByShape` in `lib/viewer/occlusion.ts`), so that a small irregular moon hides the dial's far side. While it is drawn, the *Light and season* panel gives its figures: for a free body the rotation and the local time at the pointer's meridian, the sidereal and the solar day (`Rotation 137° · Local time 14:20 (meridian 0)`, `Sidereal day 10 h 53 min · Solar day 10 h 54 min`); for a locked body the libration now (the pointer's swing round 0°, the same signed reading as its label; the host swings the other way in the body's sky) and its amplitude (the greatest true minus mean anomaly, from Kepler's equation: slightly more than 2e, 28.9° rather than 28.6° at e = 0.25, `librationAmplitude` in `lib/systemSky.ts`), the local time on the face turned to the host and the solar day (`Synchronous rotation · Libration +0.3° (±0.4°)`, `Local time 18:05 (face to the host) · Solar day 2.49 d`), or, for a planet locked to its star, *Solar day: none, the star stands still*. A planet its star holds in the 3:2 spin–orbit resonance (Mercury's three turns every two orbits) has a free ring, its first line reading `3:2 spin–orbit resonance · Rotation …`, its solar day two of its years; the viewer turns it 1.5 times per orbit, its long axis (longitude 0 or 180°) towards the star at each periapsis. Local time is `HH:MM` of the local day; a day under 48 h is written in hours and minutes, a longer one in days with two decimals (`formatLocalTime`, `formatDayLength`). The `?` beside the figures opens a glossary: the sidereal day is one turn against the stars (Earth: 23 h 56 min); the solar day runs from one noon to the next (Earth: 24 h), longer or shorter as the body moves along its orbit while it turns; *sol* is the name kept for the solar day of Mars; local time cuts the solar day into 24 local hours, noon when the sun crosses the meridian (the convention of Mars missions); a synchronous body turns once per orbit and shows the same face to what it orbits; a planet in the 3:2 resonance turns three times every two orbits; libration is the host's swing in its sky on a slightly elliptical orbit, about ±2e radians; and in this app a synchronous body's face to its host is longitude 180°, where the scientific convention puts longitude 0.
- **Large maps seen whole** stay smooth: the data layers carry mipmaps (when the GPU can render to half floats), so contours and colour layers average the texels a pixel covers instead of sampling one every few, and the relief samples the elevation averaged over each vertex's span (`uMeshSegments` in the surface shader: 720 across the base globe, 16 across a detail patch), so ridges no longer alias into spikes between vertices; zooming in brings back the full detail.
  - **Layer**: biomes (the generated map), or elevation, temperature (−30 °C to 40 °C on Earth-like worlds; a hot or frozen world, whose range mostly lies outside it, is coloured over its own coldest-to-hottest range rounded to 10 °C, `lib/temperatureRange.ts`, and the legend, the globe and the detail tiles state and use the same range), moisture, river flow, volcanic zone, distance to the sea and floodplains coloured on the GPU, with a legend. The last three need a world of format version 2 (3 for floodplains). On the biome layer, the legend lists the biomes of the planet with their share of its surface, in their surface colours (classification colours while the hex grid is shown): hover one to highlight it on the planet, click it to keep it highlighted.
  - **Relief**: raises land above the coastline and shades its slopes; seas stay flat, except on worlds whose seas have dried up (barren), where basins sink below the old coastline and are shaded like land. The sun glints only on liquid seas, as a broad, faint sheen, and barely on magma. The biome layer and the detail tiles are hillshaded already, so on them it only raises the land.
  - **Sunlight**: lights the planet from a sun whose latitude follows the season and the world's axial tilt, with day, night and a terminator on both the globe and the map. In a system the season slider is the planet's orbit angle from its node: spring equinox at 0, summer solstice at a quarter, autumn equinox at a half, winter solstice at three quarters (`lib/seasonClock.ts`, from Kepler's equation), so the seasons of an eccentric orbit are unequal in days (a season saved in a system link before this clock now lands on a different date of the same orbit); the playback bar and the day of the year count days since the northern spring equinox (a lone world keeps a circular year, `sunDeclinationDeg`). The **Light and season** panel names the date "solstice" or "equinox" only when the sun is truly over the tilt or the equator (`namedSeason`), and never on a moon, whose sun follows its planet's clock but its own axis; else the day of the year; its orbit line reads "Day D of a P-day orbit" with P the year of a planet or the orbit of a moon (`orbitReadout`), never the span of the orbit slider. Lava and magma glow on the night side (on the biome layer and the detail tiles, not on the hex grid); they are told by their surface colour, red with almost no green. The world's star is drawn where the light comes from; in a system, at its place (its distance and radius, `star` of `systemSky`), so that the camera drawn back still sees it at the centre of the planets' orbits, else at infinity like the star field; it moves with the other stars when the planet is turned: a disk of its apparent diameter seen from the planet (the Sun from Earth: 0.53°; a red dwarf seen from its habitable planet: about 1°), at least a few pixels wide, in the colour of its light and a glow growing with the light the planet gets; the planet hides it, the atmosphere dims it at the limb. Worlds made before stars show the Sun. **Star light** (off by default) lights the planet, its clouds, its sea glint and its sky with the colour of its star's light, as an eye used to the Sun would see them (the black body's colour divided by the Sun's, stored in `world.json`): a Sun-like star changes nothing, a red dwarf turns the day side orange and the sky less blue, an A star turns them bluish; off, the light is white and the planet shows its usual colours. It applies with the headlight too. On the biome map and the detail tiles it also recolours the vegetation for the star, following the idea that photosynthesis adapts to the light (Kiang et al. 2007, speculative): leaves darken towards purple-black, a third as bright, under stars cooler than 4 900 K (fully at 3 400 K; early K dwarfs leave them green, Kiang et al. finding plants there "very similar to the Earth"), turn golden under hotter ones (fully at 7 300 K), by the share of plants of each biome (forest and jungle 1, taiga 0.9, wetland 0.8, plain, savanna and shrubland 0.6, tundra 0.35; `lib/vegetation.ts`); the exported maps keep Earth's colours. The biome legend follows: under Star light its swatches take the same vegetation colours and the star's light (`starLitColour`), so that each still matches the globe. On a tidally locked world the star stays above the centre of the map (the season and sun longitude controls give way to a note). The other bodies of the system are drawn in the sky too, at infinity like the star, at their true apparent size (at least a few pixels, with a faint ring around a body drawn larger than it is, so that a moon a few km across is not taken for a star) and lit by the sun, so they show their phase (a body with air darkens towards its limb and has a twilight band at its terminator; an airless one is lit like dusty regolith, which hardly darkens at the limb; the grid and tropics on the host planet are shaded with it), in front of the star field and of the star (a giant passing in front of the sun hides it): from a moon, its planet, mapped with the planet's surface, under its clouds (with **Clouds**: the planet's own cloud map in its cloud colour, drifting with the clouds of the body shown, read from the planet's `world.json` with its air, see `planetLook` in `lib/skyBodies.ts`; a giant has no separate cloud layer, its surface being its clouds) and in its air (with **Atmosphere**: a halo at the limb in the colour of its sky, brightest when the sun is behind the planet), towards the moon's long axis (longitude 180°, the face it keeps towards its planet: from a Phobos, the planet spans about 40°), turning on itself as **Moon orbits** plays (at its day against its year, from the hour of the planet view; a binary's planet turns with its moon and keeps its face), its spin axis tilted by the moon's inclination, and drawn in perspective, so that from a close moon its parallels bend round it; and the planet's other moons where they are on their orbits, each showing the face it keeps towards the planet or the side it turns to the moon; from the planet, its moons, each mapped with its own surface (its preview until it is generated) and showing the face it keeps towards the planet (a captured moon, not locked, spins instead, see below), full at the start of the season and turning round at their period relative to the year as the seasons play (`lib/systemSky.ts`). The moons go round on their ellipses (Kepler's equation, with the eccentricity of `world.json` and a periapsis drawn by each moon's seed, which `world.json` does not store): nearer, larger and faster at periapsis, and their orbit lines are ellipses too. Where they start is a display choice: the first moon full at the start of the season (but for its eccentricity), each next one 2.4 radians further round its orbit. **Spin axes** (off by default, remembered) draws the axis each body turns on, out of its poles, the north end light and the south end blue: the body shown's through its globe (out of its real surface: an irregular moon's shape and its highest relief as displayed, so that the axis, its arrow and the arrows of the host's pull and of the body's velocity start outside a moonlet a few km across rather than inside it; `BodyGuides.setSurface`), each body of the sky's out of its disk, so that its tilt shows against its orbit. **Spin direction** draws the arrow bent round the north end (a ring when locked) that shows which way it turns. From a moon, its host planet's spin is drawn in 3D as the body shown's is: a tube bent round its axis above its north pole with a cone at its end, or a ring for a planet locked to its star, scaled to the planet and hidden where the planet stands in front of it (`setHostSpin`; held at the camera's far plane, so the globe still hides it), with its own label: *Spin of MGS 82899 d*, its equatorial speed and day, or the same face to its star. **Link to the host** (from a moon, its own switch) draws the dotted line to the host and the pull and velocity arrows, with their labels. **Orbits** (in the display panel, when there are moons, or a giant's other moons, in the sky) draws their orbits in the sky as faint lines, where they are round the body shown (its near side in front of the globe, its far side behind it), and places the bodies of the sky from the camera too, so that each stays on its line however the globe is turned: great circles from the planet, loops round the sky or swinging beside the planet from one of its moons, and from a moon its own orbit, through the moon and round its planet (a binary's moon, which hangs still in its planet's sky, has none from the planet). The bodies of the sky drawn at their size (the four largest in view, `skyOccluders` in `lib/skyBodies.ts`) hide the parts of the lines behind them and are crossed by those in front of them (`skyOrbitFragment` in `lib/viewer/shaders.ts`), wherever the camera's far plane falls: seen from a giant's moon, the giant stands in the middle of its moons' orbits, their near halves over it, their far halves hidden by it. Each line runs through its body's centre and is sampled ever closer to it (the giant's own orbit round its star, seen from its moon, would otherwise pass beside it with corners), and evenly round the rest of it: 720 points in the high quality profile, 160 in the light one (`orbitSamples` in `lib/viewer/quality.ts`), whose corners can show on a line passing near the camera. From a giant's moon, the giant's other regular moons hang beside it too (`siblings` under `host` in `world.json`: two to four, sharing about 10⁻⁴ of the giant's mass as the Galilean moons do, on a resonant chain around the world's orbit, each period twice the last), at their size from the world and turning at their period relative to the world's as the seasons play, each mapped with its own surface (its preview until it is generated) and showing the face it keeps towards the giant. Their shadows fall on the ground: a moon passing in front of the sun darkens a spot of its planet (umbra, penumbra, an annular eclipse when its umbra falls short), and the planet darkens its moon in an eclipse. The surface takes the four bodies whose shadow reaches it now, the largest in its sky first (`pickShadowCasters` in `lib/skyBodies.ts`), so a small moon close by is never left out for a far giant. A body that lies between the camera and the body shown (`inFrontOfGlobe`) is drawn in front of it, with its label, instead of behind it. The surface shader takes the four largest shadows: in a system of five or six moons, the smallest moons cast none. **Play** runs through a year in 12 seconds; the latitude under the sun is shown below the slider. With moons in the sky, **Moon orbits** moves them along their orbits, as many days past the season as the slider says (over one orbit of the moon shown, or of the largest moon; in the system of a giant whose moon the world is, of the sibling shown, else of the world round the giant, whichever body is shown), changing their phases and their eclipses; its **Play** runs through that orbit in 10 seconds at ×1, and **Speed** (×1/256 to ×4,096, by powers of two; slower for a short orbit, down to real time, `orbitSpeedMin`) slows or speeds it, the line below telling how much time a second of playback covers (`1 s of playback = 6.1 h`). Every body turns on itself meanwhile, at its own pace: the planet at its day (its hour, the sun's longitude over it, goes on from the **Sun longitude** slider, so its night sweeps round and its moons rise and set), a locked moon once per orbit, a captured one at its own day, the giant at its day, its other moons once per orbit; while it plays, the camera turns with the stars, so it stays still against the sky, the star and the orbits, round the globe's centre or the point it looks at once moved with the right button, and the globe is seen to turn on itself (the arrow of its spin axis stays still too). The corner gizmo then shows the sky's axes on the globe (the body's turned with the stars), so it stays still while the globe turns. **Camera turns with the body** (in the *Light and season* panel) keeps the camera fixed on the body instead, always seeing the same face while its day and night, the stars, the star, the orbits and the bodies of the sky turn round it. It is off by default, and kept apart for a body locked to what it orbits (most moons), where it is on by default: against the stars, its planet, filling much of its sky with its rings, would sweep round it once per orbit (10 s at the default speed), and the camera would seem to go round the moon. With a system of moons, the camera can draw back far enough to see it whole: three times the furthest a moon of the planet gets from the body shown (from a moon, its own distance to the planet plus the furthest moon's; `systemReach` in `lib/systemSky.ts`). It starts over at 0, paused, when another world opens, and a copied link keeps it. On a moon the sun follows its orbit: it goes round the moon once a month, the moon keeping its face to its planet (once a day of its own on a captured moon), and the latitude under it comes from the planet's season and axial tilt and from the moon's inclined orbit, whose node stays fixed among the stars (drawn by the moon's seed, like its periapsis), so an inclined moon sees the sun climb and fall over its equator through the planet's year, so the planet in its sky waxes and wanes (the **Sun longitude** slider still turns the sun round the planet). Off, a headlight follows the camera.
  - **Night side** (under Sunlight): lit by the bodies of the system that reflect their star's light onto it, not by a fixed ambient (`lib/nightLight.ts`). Each body of the sky, from a moon its host planet and its siblings, from a planet its moons, gives a Lambert sphere's light, `p Φ(α) (R/D)² × insolation` of the star's illuminance on a surface facing it (`p` geometric albedo, here the luminance of the body's colour kept within 0.05 to 0.8, a proxy since the system stores none; `Φ(α) = (sin α + (π − α) cos α) / π` the phase function, from the angle at the body between the star and the body shown, Russell 1916; `R/D` from its angular size; `insolation` the star's flux at it over that at the body shown); a giant's rings add their lit area, `0.4 (outer² − inner²) × opacity × |cos elevation|` against the disk's `R²` (a ring albedo of about 0.4, Saturn's B ring being near 0.5). Checks: the full Moon from Earth gives 2.4e-6 of the Sun's 1.3e5 lux, 0.3 lux, the top of the 0.1 to 0.3 lux usually quoted; the full Earth from the Moon 1.2e-4; starlight with airglow, 1e-3 to 2e-3 lux, about 1.5e-8. The four brightest sources go to the surface shader, each adding its ratio by how high it stands over the horizon at the point (a disk, so one just below the horizon still gives a little). That span of seven orders of magnitude is mapped to the screen on a logarithmic curve (the log-luminance compression of tone mapping, Ward 1994, Reinhard et al. 2002, as the eye adapts to dim light, Ferwerda et al. 1996) that sets the brightest the night gets, from the sum of the sources' ratios: **7 %** of the day side at 1e-7 of the star's light, **18 %** at 2e-2 (a giant of Jupiter's size as seen from Io is about 1e-2), the log of the ratio in between. The ceiling stays well under the day side so the terminator is always obvious. Across the ground that peak is scaled linearly by the horizon law (the log is not applied point by point: a log of the cosine law would drop from half brightness to nothing within a degree of a source's horizon, a hard straight line on the globe). The two ends of the curve and the 7 % floor, which keeps the shape of a body readable when nothing lights it (a lone planet), are display choices, not physics: they set how bright a night looks, not how bright it is. Lava and a hot giant's glow come on top, unchanged, and the morph's night lift (`uNightLift`) still raises the floor towards 45 % during the turn. With Sunlight off the lighting is the flat headlight, as before. Approximations: sources are points at the body's centre for direction, the surface is not shadowed by relief or the host's eclipse, a body's colour stands for its albedo, and the display curve is shared by all worlds, whatever the star.
  - **Atmosphere**: the world's sky, from its pressure and its preset's `[atmosphere]` table: single scattering of sunlight (Rayleigh for the gas, Mie for the haze) through a shell around the globe, which draws the halo, the twilight band at the terminator and the veil over distant and grazing ground (an opaque atmosphere such as Venus's hides the surface). The shell is far thicker than a real atmosphere so the sky reads at planet scale, and rises with the **Relief** exaggeration (keeping the same optical depth) so displaced mountains stay under the sky and hide the halo behind them; airless worlds have none, and worlds made before atmospheres get Earth's. The light reaching each point of a view ray is attenuated by the optical depth towards the sun, read from a small table (128 × 32 half floats per world, `sunDepth.ts`) of the Chapman function of an exponential atmosphere over a sphere, instead of being marched: the sky costs about half as much GPU time as with the former march (measured on an Apple M1 Pro at 2520 × 2000 pixels, 6.0 to 2.4 ms per frame for the whole globe, 8.4 to 3.7 ms for a close-up of the limb) and looks the same. **Clouds**: the world's cloud layer (`raw_data/clouds.png`, worlds of format version 5 with clouds), on the globe and the flat map: thin clouds are translucent veils, thick ones hide the ground, tinted by kind (white water, pale yellow sulfuric acid, ochre dust), lit by the sun and casting shadows on the globe; they fade out when zooming close to the ground. The layer is downloaded the first time clouds are shown, so a world viewed with Clouds off never loads it. **Drift** (under Clouds, off by default) turns the cloud layer slowly eastwards, once around the planet in five minutes; while it drifts the viewer redraws ten times a second instead of only when something moves, and stops when the clouds are hidden or faded out. **Stars** (on by default): about 8 500 stars around the globe, fixed in space, so they turn with the planet when the camera moves; their brightness and colour vary (many faint white stars, a few bright ones from blue to orange), and fainter ones crowd along a band tilted 60° to the equator, like the Milky Way. They are points at infinity drawn behind everything (a few kilobytes, one draw call), dimmed by the atmosphere at the limb, and hidden on the flat map. **Grid**: 30° graticule. **Tropics**: the equator, the tropics and the polar circles of the world's axial tilt, dashed in the colours of `world.png`, 3 screen pixels wide with a dark edge at every zoom, drawn over the clouds and the detail tiles. **Depth contours**: the depth contours (isobaths) under water at the levels of `world.png`, in pale blue. **Elevation contours**: contour lines of elevation on land, in topographic brown, every 0.05 elevation units above the coastline, every fifth one stronger (index contours). Contours are drawn about one screen pixel wide at every zoom, detail tiles included, from the elevation data, so the world's data must be loaded. All three are on by default. Worlds of format version 6 and later show `raw_data/surface.png`, which has no lines, so they can be hidden; older worlds show `world.png`, whose tropics and depth contours are baked in.
  - **Hexes**: the hex grid of the world (majority biome per hex, outlines, a blue dot on river hexes), computed by the server for the chosen number of columns and river threshold. Moving a slider or turning the hexes off aborts the request under way. While the server generates a world it answers that it is busy: the panel shows "server busy, retrying…" and asks again every 5 seconds. A request that fails shows a notification, and the globe stays usable. **Download hex JSON** saves the grid shown.
- The display settings (except the hex grid itself) are remembered by the browser. Only the main viewer's are: a compared world opens with them, and changing its display does not change them.

A system that exists already (the same star and seed) is refused by the server, and the form then offers to replace it. The form remembers its mode. In *Around a planet*: (sea level or land share, volcanism, circumference, axial tilt, erosion, surface pressure, surface age, the star and the orbit, the moons). **Moons** is drawn by the seed (the default), none, or 1 to 6 moons: with a number, a random star and orbit are drawn again until the planet has room for them (an error explains why when the star and orbit are fixed). **Star metallicity** (`[Fe/H]`, random by default: drawn like the stars nearby) sets how likely giant planets are; **Giant planets** is drawn by the seed or none (not modelled, the Solar System's impact rate kept); **A giant's moon** is as the seed draws (1 in 50), never or always. Choosing a giant planet type (`gas-giant`, `ice-giant`, `hot-jupiter`) leaves only the axial tilt, the star, the orbit, the metallicity and the **Mass** (Earth masses, on a logarithmic slider from 5 to 4 131, random by default: the preset's range). The star is **Auto** (the planet type's, or one drawn like the stars nearby) or a spectral class and subtype, each class explained below the selectors; the orbit is a position in the star's habitable zone (0 inner edge, 1 outer edge) or a distance in AU, with its eccentricity (random by default). The pressure slider is logarithmic, from 0.001 to 1000 bar, with airless (0 bar) at its far left, so Mars's 0.006 bar is as easy to reach as Venus's 92; the number beside it takes any value. Under the size, the form shows how long the generation should take (from the stage timings learnt from earlier generations in this browser, once there are some), the server's memory peak (interpolated from the figures measured in Docker, see "Web app" in the repository README: 0.16 GiB at 1920 × 1080, 0.55 GiB at 4096 × 2048, 2.0 GiB at 8192 × 4096) and the page's memory for the decoded world (19 bytes per pixel, see below). Each parameter is either random (left to the planet preset, then to the generator defaults) or fixed with its slider; **Roll** draws a seed. The browser remembers the last form you submitted: **New world** starts from it (planet, size and every parameter, the star, the moons, the metallicity, the giant neighbours, whether the world is a giant's moon and a giant's mass, but not the seed, which would name the same world), and **Defaults** goes back to the preset defaults (the moons, metallicity, giants and mass drawn again) and forgets it. A number field left empty is drawn, as if left to random. **Vary…** always starts from the varied world. A world is named after its planet and seed, and the server refuses to overwrite one: when the planet and seed match an existing world, the form says so and offers to replace it. A system replaced (`POST /api/systems?replace=true`) is swapped for the new one as soon as it is drawn; its bodies are generated again as they are opened. **Generate** sends the job and closes the form: generations run in the background, one at a time, the others waiting in a queue. The **Generations** list shows each one with its stage, elapsed time, an estimate of the time left (learnt from earlier generations in this browser) and **Cancel**. A reloaded page picks up the generations still running. When one finishes, the new world opens if you have not picked another one meanwhile; otherwise a notification offers to open it. A world replaced in place reloads in the panes that show it: its map URLs carry the world revision (`?rev=`), and its raw data is revalidated with the server, so the browser never shows files of the replaced world.

**Compare** shows two worlds side by side (stacked on narrow screens). **Link cameras** moves both views together. **Link display** (off by default) makes the compare pane mirror the main one's layer, relief, sunlight (season and sun longitude), atmosphere, clouds and grid as they change, so both worlds are seen the same way; the rest (contours, drift, hexes) stays per pane. **Swap** exchanges them. Both choices are remembered.

On narrow screens (760 px wide or less), the library and the viewer take the whole screen one at a time: **Worlds** and **Viewer** in the header switch between them. Picking a world opens the viewer, and **New world** opens the form, whose buttons stay pinned to the bottom of the screen while the parameters scroll.

While a world loads, a slim bar under **Loading…** (the base map) and **Loading the raw data…** (the layers, fetched and decoded in a Web Worker) names the file being downloaded and shows the megabytes received out of the total of all the files (from their `Content-Length`; the bar slides back and forth while a size is unknown), then each decoding step and the biome statistics. Picking another world meanwhile aborts the downloads and the decoding.

The viewer draws a frame only when something changes (the camera and its damping, the morph between globe and map, a loaded tile, a display setting, a resize), so an idle page costs no GPU time. Frames are drawn at the screen's pixel ratio capped at 2 (antialiased); while the view moves (dragging, damping, zooming, the globe and map morph) they drop to 1.25, and the view is drawn again at full resolution 150 ms after it stops. A hidden viewer (on narrow screens, while the world list shows) draws nothing: the season animation and the cloud drift pause, and resume where they were when it shows again. A decoded world takes about 11 bytes per pixel in the page (19 with the layers of format version 2 and later), about 0.6 GB for 8192 × 4096. Worlds wider than the GPU's texture limit (8192 × 4096 on a GPU limited to 4096 pixels) are shown at a lower resolution; the point readout still reads every pixel.

On the GPU, a pane holds the data layers as RGBA half floats (8 bytes per pixel), the biome ids (1 byte) and the base map (4 bytes, with mipmaps). The layers of format version 2 and later (volcanic zone, distance to the sea, floodplains; another 8 bytes per pixel) are uploaded only the first time one of those layers is shown. The cloud layer takes one byte per pixel (a single-channel texture, with mipmaps), decoded in a Web Worker, once clouds are shown. Decoded images (the base map, the clouds, the detail tiles) are freed as soon as the GPU has them, and so are the copies of layers shrunk to fit the GPU: the page keeps only the decoded world it reads point values from. A pane keeps up to 300 detail tiles on the GPU (about 340 kB each with their mipmaps), the least recently shown going first; while two worlds are compared, each pane keeps 150, so comparing does not double their memory.

For everyday use, run the whole app in Docker instead: see "Web app" in the repository README.

## Rendering quality

Without a graphics card (a virtual machine, a remote desktop, a server),
the browser runs WebGL on the processor (SwiftShader in Chrome, llvmpipe
in Firefox on Linux, the Microsoft Basic Render Driver on Windows), and
the full 3D views crawl. The header's quality picker (`QualitySelect.svelte`,
hidden on screens narrower than 480 px) chooses what the views draw:

- **Auto** (the default): light quality when the browser draws on the
  processor, high otherwise. The renderer is probed once
  (`detectRenderer` in `lib/viewer/quality.ts`): its name
  (`WEBGL_debug_renderer_info`), and whether the browser refuses a
  context without a major performance caveat. The first time auto
  picks light quality, a notice says so. The picker's tooltip gives the
  renderer detected.
- **High**: the views as described above.
- **Light**: the same worlds, drawn for less:
  - fewer pixels (0.75 per CSS pixel when still, 0.5 while moving, scaled
    up by the browser, against up to 2);
  - no antialiasing;
  - 20 frames per second at most;
  - no camera inertia, which keeps drawing frames after the pointer
    stops.
  - a coarser globe: 240 vertices round the planet instead of 720. On a
    CPU renderer its vertices cost more than its pixels, so this saves
    more than any other item; the relief keeps its heights, with less
    detail;
  - a plain halo for the atmosphere: the same colours and veil as the
    scattering shell, from optical depths in closed form instead of a
    16-step march (`atmosphereHaloFragment` in `lib/viewer/shaders.ts`),
    with a coarser twilight;
  - no cloud shadows on the ground (the clouds themselves stay);
  - a quarter of the detail tile textures kept in memory;
  - in the star view, a coarser star (48 segments instead of 128) and 4
    strands in each prominence instead of 12;
  - no ring rocks round a ring moon (about 1.3 ms a frame on an Apple M1
    Pro at 2560 × 1516).
  - coarser orbit lines in the planet view's sky: 160 points round each
    instead of 720, recomputed every frame while the orbits play; their
    corners can show where a line passes near the camera.

On SwiftShader, at 1280 × 758 CSS pixels and a device pixel ratio of 1, a
frame of the planet view takes about 40 ms in light quality against 490
ms in high quality (60 ms against 470 ms with the globe filling the
view). A giant takes 48 ms against 560 ms, and the star view 6 ms against
45 ms.

The choice is remembered (`mapgen.quality`). A view takes its quality
when it is made (`QualityProfile`, in `lib/viewer/quality.ts`), as
antialiasing is fixed with the WebGL context, so changing it reloads the
page; the address keeps the system and body shown.

## Themes

The header's theme picker restyles the interface: **Default** (the original look), **Holo HUD** (cyan, cut corners, Orbitron and Rajdhani), **Orbital station** (amber, bracket corners, Exo 2), **Cyberpunk** (neon magenta and yellow, diagonal cuts, Audiowide and Chakra Petch, a short glitch on hovered buttons, off with reduced motion) and **Military** (phosphor green, scan lines, Share Tech Mono). The choice is remembered in the browser (`mapgen.theme`, Default when unknown or without storage).

A theme is data: `src/lib/themes.ts` maps each theme to CSS custom properties (colours, `--control-bg`, `--font-ui`, `--font-display`, `--radius`, the panel frame `--frame-clip`, `--frame-deco` and `--glow`, the labels' `--label-bg` and `--label-text`, the overlays' `--overlay-orbit`, `--overlay-selected`, `--overlay-line` and `--overlay-muted`), which `applyTheme` writes on the root before the first paint; `app.css` holds Default's values as fallbacks. Panels take `background: var(--frame-deco), var(--panel)`, `clip-path: var(--frame-clip)` and the glow in their shadow; the star view's neutral overlays (orbits, the selected orbit, dotted lines, moon rings, the compression circle) take the overlay colours (`StarView.setOverlayColours`, live on a switch). Colours that carry meaning are not tokens and keep their hue in every theme: the habitable zone, the frost line, the velocity and pull arrows, the map layers. A test (`themes.test.ts`) checks that every theme keeps text, muted text and links at 4.5:1 or more on its panels and controls (WCAG AA). The fonts load from Google Fonts (`index.html`); without them, the system's fonts stand in.

## Translations

Every string the interface shows comes from `src/lib/i18n/en.ts`; components read it through `t` (`t.viewer.layers.elevation`), and messages with values are functions of named parameters (`t.jobs.left({ time })`), so each language orders its words. To add a message, add it to `en.ts` and to every other catalog (the type check lists the ones missing it). To add a language, copy `fr.ts`, translate it, and add its code to `LOCALES` and `LOCALE_NAMES` in `locale.ts` and its catalog to `index.svelte.ts`; built-in presets take a `[locales.<code>]` table. Format numbers with the helpers of `lib/format.ts` (`formatNumber`, `formatUpTo`, `withUnit`…), which follow the locale. Tests run in English whatever the machine's language (`src/test-setup.ts`). A test (`src/lib/i18n/templates.test.ts`) fails on any word written in a component's markup outside an expression, text or `title`, `aria-label`, `placeholder`, `alt` and `label` attributes: write it in the catalogs instead (units and symbols that read the same in every language go in its `ALLOWED` list).
