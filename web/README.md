# Planet viewer (web front-end)

Browser front-end of the map generator: a library of generated worlds, a 3D globe and a flat map that morph into each other, point inspection, measurements and side-by-side comparison, and a form that queues new generations on the server.

## Prerequisites

- Node 24 or later (`mise install` at the repository root installs the pinned version).
- The `mapgen-server` API, from `crates/server/`.

## Installation

```bash
mise run web:install
```

## Usage

Development, with hot reload: run the server and the Vite dev server together, then open <http://localhost:5173>. Vite forwards `/api`, `/worlds` and `/healthz` to the server on port 3000.

```bash
mise run dev
```

The interface is in English or French: the first visit follows the browser's languages (any `fr-*` gives French, anything else English), and the **EN / FR** selector in the header switches it at once and is remembered. Numbers, units and dates follow it (decimal comma and narrow no-break spaces in French). Biome names, generation stages, the request errors the server explains with a code, and the presets that carry a `[locales.fr]` table are translated too; the files a generation writes and the CLI stay in English. Keyboard shortcuts are the same in every language.

In the viewer:

- **Globe / Map** switches between the globe and the flat map, with an animated morph that keeps the place: the point at the centre of the screen stays there, at an equivalent zoom (the same scale near the ground, the whole map for the whole globe), and the camera follows it while the surface morphs. Zoomed out, the map is kept in view rather than half off screen.
- Drag to rotate the globe or pan the map, scroll to zoom towards the point under the cursor. Zooming goes from the whole planet down to about 300 m per pixel: on the biome layer, detail tiles rendered by the server stream in as you get closer (the coarser picture stays until they arrive, and a tile with nothing loaded above it also fetches its ancestor two levels up, so fast zooming shows something quickly). A tile that fails to load is retried after 2 s, then less and less often (up to once a minute); one the server refuses (a 4xx error) is not retried. On the globe, rotation slows down near the surface.
- Hovering shows the coordinates, biome and elevation under the pointer at the bottom of a wide viewer.
- Click a point to see its coordinates, biome, elevation above the coastline, annual mean temperature in °C (worlds of format version 3 and older stored a `[0, 1]` scale, converted on load with the generator's Earth-fitted curve), moisture, drainage area and, on worlds with `raw_data` format version 2 or later, the distance to the sea, the volcanic zone, the floodplain weight and the shift of the mountain line. Near a river it also shows the river (flow there and at its mouth, length, where it ends, estuary or delta branch); with the hex grid on, the hex under the point. **Copy** copies the latitude and longitude in degrees.
- The tools in the top-left corner: **Reset view** (whole planet, north up), **Measure** (click points to measure a path of great-circle legs, in km on this planet), **Screenshot** (the view as a PNG) and **?** (the keyboard shortcuts, then a table of the less common units: Ga and Ma for ages, bar for pressure, g for gravity, AU for distances to the star, L☉ for its luminosity, each with reference values). The same units are explained in a tooltip wherever they appear, on the world card (gravity, atmosphere, geology) and in the generation form (pressure, surface age): values with such a tooltip are underlined with dots.
- **Time slider**, under the tools, on worlds that offer other epochs (no liquid water today, made with impact craters, `raw_data` format version 7): it moves the surface through time, from its oldest surviving surface (4 Ga on `mercury-like`; back to the end of the wet era on worlds that had water, 3.5 Ga on `mars-like`) to 1 Ga ahead, the present in between. Going back, the craters younger than the epoch disappear and the others look fresher; going ahead, craters still to come appear. The label reads "3.8 Ga ago", "present" or "in 250 Ma" (Ma and Ga explained in its tooltip); the stops are 1 Ma apart within 100 Ma of the present, 10 Ma within 1 Ga, 50 Ma beyond. While the slider moves, the base map shows the epoch at a quarter of its resolution (after a pause of 120 ms) and the detail tiles are hidden; once released, the full map, the detail tiles, the relief, the contours and the point information follow the epoch. **Present** goes back to it. The biome statistics of the world card, the hex grid and the comparison stay at the present. The epoch chosen is kept when another world is shown, each world showing its nearest stop (the present on one without epochs). See `GET /api/worlds/{id}/epoch/...` in the repository README.
- **Display**: open or folded as you last left it on a wide screen; narrow screens (under 900 px) fold it and the biome legend, which would hide the planet, and reopen them when the window grows back:
  - **Layer**: biomes (the generated map), or elevation, temperature (−30 °C to 40 °C), moisture, river flow, volcanic zone, distance to the sea and floodplains coloured on the GPU, with a legend. The last three need a world of format version 2 (3 for floodplains). On the biome layer, the legend lists the biomes of the planet with their share of its surface, in their surface colours (classification colours while the hex grid is shown): hover one to highlight it on the planet, click it to keep it highlighted.
  - **Relief**: raises land above the coastline and shades its slopes; seas stay flat, except on worlds whose seas have dried up (barren), where basins sink below the old coastline and are shaded like land. The sun glints only on liquid seas, as a broad, faint sheen, and barely on magma. The biome layer and the detail tiles are hillshaded already, so on them it only raises the land.
  - **Sunlight**: lights the planet from a sun whose latitude follows the season and the world's axial tilt, with day, night and a terminator on both the globe and the map. Lava and magma glow on the night side (on the biome layer and the detail tiles, not on the hex grid); they are told by their surface colour, red with almost no green. The world's star is drawn where the light comes from, at infinity like the star field, so it moves with the other stars when the planet is turned: a disk of its apparent diameter seen from the planet (the Sun from Earth: 0.53°; a red dwarf seen from its habitable planet: about 1°), at least a few pixels wide, in the colour of its light and a glow growing with the light the planet gets; the planet hides it, the atmosphere dims it at the limb. Worlds made before stars show the Sun. **Star light** (off by default) lights the planet, its clouds, its sea glint and its sky with the colour of its star's light, as an eye used to the Sun would see them (the black body's colour divided by the Sun's, stored in `world.json`): a Sun-like star changes nothing, a red dwarf turns the day side orange and the sky less blue, an A star turns them bluish; off, the light is white and the planet shows its usual colours. It applies with the headlight too. On the biome map and the detail tiles it also recolours the vegetation for the star, following the idea that photosynthesis adapts to the light (Kiang et al. 2007, speculative): leaves darken towards purple-black under stars cooler than the Sun (fully at 3 300 K), turn golden under hotter ones (fully at 7 300 K), by the share of plants of each biome (forest and jungle 1, taiga 0.9, wetland 0.8, plain, savanna and shrubland 0.6, tundra 0.35; `lib/vegetation.ts`); the exported maps keep Earth's colours. On a tidally locked world the star stays above the centre of the map (the season and sun longitude controls give way to a note). **Play** runs through a year in 12 seconds; the latitude under the sun is shown below the slider. Off, a headlight follows the camera.
  - **Atmosphere**: the world's sky, from its pressure and its preset's `[atmosphere]` table: single scattering of sunlight (Rayleigh for the gas, Mie for the haze) through a shell around the globe, which draws the halo, the twilight band at the terminator and the veil over distant and grazing ground (an opaque atmosphere such as Venus's hides the surface). The shell is far thicker than a real atmosphere so the sky reads at planet scale, and rises with the **Relief** exaggeration (keeping the same optical depth) so displaced mountains stay under the sky and hide the halo behind them; airless worlds have none, and worlds made before atmospheres get Earth's. The light reaching each point of a view ray is attenuated by the optical depth towards the sun, read from a small table (128 × 32 half floats per world, `sunDepth.ts`) of the Chapman function of an exponential atmosphere over a sphere, instead of being marched: the sky costs about half as much GPU time as with the former march (measured on an Apple M1 Pro at 2520 × 2000 pixels, 6.0 to 2.4 ms per frame for the whole globe, 8.4 to 3.7 ms for a close-up of the limb) and looks the same. **Clouds**: the world's cloud layer (`raw_data/clouds.png`, worlds of format version 5 with clouds), on the globe and the flat map: thin clouds are translucent veils, thick ones hide the ground, tinted by kind (white water, pale yellow sulfuric acid, ochre dust), lit by the sun and casting shadows on the globe; they fade out when zooming close to the ground. The layer is downloaded the first time clouds are shown, so a world viewed with Clouds off never loads it. **Drift** (under Clouds, off by default) turns the cloud layer slowly eastwards, once around the planet in five minutes; while it drifts the viewer redraws ten times a second instead of only when something moves, and stops when the clouds are hidden or faded out. **Stars** (on by default): about 8 500 stars around the globe, fixed in space, so they turn with the planet when the camera moves; their brightness and colour vary (many faint white stars, a few bright ones from blue to orange), and fainter ones crowd along a band tilted 60° to the equator, like the Milky Way. They are points at infinity drawn behind everything (a few kilobytes, one draw call), dimmed by the atmosphere at the limb, and hidden on the flat map. **Grid**: 30° graticule. **Tropics**: the equator, the tropics and the polar circles of the world's axial tilt, dashed in the colours of `world.png`, 3 screen pixels wide with a dark edge at every zoom, drawn over the clouds and the detail tiles. **Depth contours**: the depth contours (isobaths) under water at the levels of `world.png`, in pale blue. **Elevation contours**: contour lines of elevation on land, in topographic brown, every 0.05 elevation units above the coastline, every fifth one stronger (index contours). Contours are drawn about one screen pixel wide at every zoom, detail tiles included, from the elevation data, so the world's data must be loaded. All three are on by default. Worlds of format version 6 and later show `raw_data/surface.png`, which has no lines, so they can be hidden; older worlds show `world.png`, whose tropics and depth contours are baked in.
  - **Hexes**: the hex grid of the world (majority biome per hex, outlines, a blue dot on river hexes), computed by the server for the chosen number of columns and river threshold. Moving a slider or turning the hexes off aborts the request under way. While the server generates a world it answers that it is busy: the panel shows "server busy, retrying…" and asks again every 5 seconds. A request that fails shows a notification, and the globe stays usable. **Download hex JSON** saves the grid shown.
- The display settings (except the hex grid itself) are remembered by the browser. Only the main viewer's are: a compared world opens with them, and changing its display does not change them.

The help takes the keyboard focus when it opens and gives it back when it closes (`Esc` or `×`). The brackets and `+` / `-` also work when the layout types them with Alt (Option), as French and German Mac keyboards do.

In the sidebar:

- The selected world's card shows every parameter, its gravity, its atmosphere (surface pressure and cloud kind; worlds made before atmospheres show Earth's 1 bar), its geology (tectonic regime and surface age; worlds made before it show Earth's regime and no age), its star (type, temperature, luminosity), orbit (distance, and place relative to the habitable zone, whose edges show in a tooltip), the starlight it gets (relative to Earth's, with the equilibrium temperature), its year, the apparent size of its star and whether it is tidally locked (worlds made before stars orbit the Sun at 1 AU), surface, land and water shares, river count and age, and how much of the planet each biome covers. Its actions: **Reroll** queues the same world again with a new random seed (every other parameter kept, the new world opens when ready), **Vary…** opens the form filled with the world's parameters, **Copy command** copies the `mise run gen` command that regenerates it, **Copy link** copies a link that opens it as shown (see the address below), **Compare with** shows a second world beside it, and **Download** offers the map PNG, the SVG, the legend and a ZIP of `raw_data/` built in the browser.
- The world list is asked for again when the page comes back to the front (a window focus or the tab shown again, at most once every 5 seconds), so worlds made meanwhile with `mise run gen` or in another tab show up. When the server cannot be reached at startup, **Retry** tries again.
- The world list can be searched by id or seed, filtered by planet and sorted; the sort and filter are remembered. Each row, and the compare bar, shows the world's thumbnail (`thumb.png`, 512 pixels wide, from `GET /api/worlds/{id}/thumb?rev=`) rather than its full-size map, which would decode to about 128 MB at 8192 × 4096; thumbnails load as they scroll into view, at a fixed size, with a striped placeholder when one cannot load. **Select** picks several worlds, to delete them or to compare two of them.
- Deleting a world hides it at once and offers **Undo** for six seconds; the files are deleted afterwards (or when the page closes).
- The address names the worlds shown (`#world=<id>&compare=<id>`): reloading keeps them, and the link can be shared. The link that **Copy link** copies also carries the view of the main pane, so it opens the same view: `view=g,<lon>,<lat>,<altitude>` on the globe (the point under the camera in degrees, and its altitude in planet radii) or `view=m,<lon>,<lat>,<height>` on the flat map (the point at the centre of the screen, and the camera's height in map units, the map being 2π × π), `layer=<id>` (`biomes`, `elevation`, `temperature`, `moisture`, `flow`, `volcanic`, `coast`, `floodplain`), `relief=<0–1>`, `epoch=<Ma>` (the time slider, when not at the present) and `sun=<season>,<longitude>` (fraction of the year and longitude under the sun in degrees) or `sun=off`. A compared world follows the camera when **Link cameras** is on. Each part is optional, and one that is malformed or out of range is ignored. The view is applied once when the link opens; the address is not updated as the camera moves, and names only the worlds afterwards. For example: `#world=terran-11&view=g,-40,25,0.3&layer=elevation&relief=0.6&sun=0.25,120`.

**New world** opens the generation form (sea level or land share, volcanism, circumference, axial tilt, erosion, surface pressure, surface age, the star and the orbit). The star is **Auto** (the planet type's, or one drawn like the stars nearby) or a spectral class and subtype, each class explained below the selectors; the orbit is a position in the star's habitable zone (0 inner edge, 1 outer edge) or a distance in AU. The pressure slider is logarithmic, from 0.001 to 1000 bar, with airless (0 bar) at its far left, so Mars's 0.006 bar is as easy to reach as Venus's 92; the number beside it takes any value. Under the size, the form shows how long the generation should take (from the stage timings learnt from earlier generations in this browser, once there are some), the server's memory peak (interpolated from the figures measured in Docker, see "Web app" in the repository README: 0.16 GiB at 1920 × 1080, 0.55 GiB at 4096 × 2048, 2.0 GiB at 8192 × 4096) and the page's memory for the decoded world (19 bytes per pixel, see below). Each parameter is either random (left to the planet preset, then to the generator defaults) or fixed with its slider; **Roll** draws a seed. The browser remembers the last form you submitted: **New world** starts from it (planet, size and every parameter, but not the seed, which would name the same world), and **Defaults** goes back to the preset defaults and forgets it. **Vary…** always starts from the varied world. A world is named after its planet and seed, and the server refuses to overwrite one: when the planet and seed match an existing world, the form says so and offers to replace it. The server swaps the new world in place of the old one only once it is complete (`POST /api/worlds?replace=true`), so the old world stays visible and usable meanwhile, and a request that is refused, a generation that fails or one you cancel leaves it in place. **Generate** sends the job and closes the form: generations run in the background, one at a time, the others waiting in a queue. The **Generations** list shows each one with its stage, elapsed time, an estimate of the time left (learnt from earlier generations in this browser) and **Cancel**. A reloaded page picks up the generations still running. When one finishes, the new world opens if you have not picked another one meanwhile; otherwise a notification offers to open it. A world replaced in place reloads in the panes that show it: its map URLs carry the world revision (`?rev=`), and its raw data is revalidated with the server, so the browser never shows files of the replaced world.

**Compare** shows two worlds side by side (stacked on narrow screens). **Link cameras** moves both views together. **Link display** (off by default) makes the compare pane mirror the main one's layer, relief, sunlight (season and sun longitude), atmosphere, clouds and grid as they change, so both worlds are seen the same way; the rest (contours, drift, hexes) stays per pane. **Swap** exchanges them. Both choices are remembered.

On narrow screens (760 px wide or less), the world list and the viewer take the whole screen one at a time: **Worlds** and **Viewer** in the header switch between them. Picking a world opens the viewer, and **New world** opens the form, whose buttons stay pinned to the bottom of the screen while the parameters scroll.

While a world loads, a slim bar under **Loading…** (the base map) and **Loading the raw data…** (the layers, fetched and decoded in a Web Worker) names the file being downloaded and shows the megabytes received out of the total of all the files (from their `Content-Length`; the bar slides back and forth while a size is unknown), then each decoding step and the biome statistics. Picking another world meanwhile aborts the downloads and the decoding.

The viewer draws a frame only when something changes (the camera and its damping, the morph between globe and map, a loaded tile, a display setting, a resize), so an idle page costs no GPU time. Frames are drawn at the screen's pixel ratio capped at 2 (antialiased); while the view moves (dragging, damping, zooming, the globe and map morph) they drop to 1.25, and the view is drawn again at full resolution 150 ms after it stops. A hidden viewer (on narrow screens, while the world list shows) draws nothing: the season animation and the cloud drift pause, and resume where they were when it shows again. A decoded world takes about 11 bytes per pixel in the page (19 with the layers of format version 2 and later), about 0.6 GB for 8192 × 4096. Worlds wider than the GPU's texture limit (8192 × 4096 on a GPU limited to 4096 pixels) are shown at a lower resolution; the point readout still reads every pixel.

On the GPU, a pane holds the data layers as RGBA half floats (8 bytes per pixel), the biome ids (1 byte) and the base map (4 bytes, with mipmaps). The layers of format version 2 and later (volcanic zone, distance to the sea, floodplains; another 8 bytes per pixel) are uploaded only the first time one of those layers is shown. The cloud layer takes one byte per pixel (a single-channel texture, with mipmaps), decoded in a Web Worker, once clouds are shown. Decoded images (the base map, the clouds, the detail tiles) are freed as soon as the GPU has them, and so are the copies of layers shrunk to fit the GPU: the page keeps only the decoded world it reads point values from. A pane keeps up to 300 detail tiles on the GPU (about 340 kB each with their mipmaps), the least recently shown going first; while two worlds are compared, each pane keeps 150, so comparing does not double their memory.

For everyday use, run the whole app in Docker instead: see "Web app" in the repository README.

## Translations

Every string the interface shows comes from `src/lib/i18n/en.ts`; components read it through `t` (`t.viewer.layers.elevation`), and messages with values are functions of named parameters (`t.jobs.left({ time })`), so each language orders its words. To add a message, add it to `en.ts` and to every other catalog (the type check lists the ones missing it). To add a language, copy `fr.ts`, translate it, and add its code to `LOCALES` and `LOCALE_NAMES` in `locale.ts` and its catalog to `index.svelte.ts`; built-in presets take a `[locales.<code>]` table. Format numbers with the helpers of `lib/format.ts` (`formatNumber`, `formatUpTo`, `withUnit`…), which follow the locale. Tests run in English whatever the machine's language (`src/test-setup.ts`). A test (`src/lib/i18n/templates.test.ts`) fails on any word written in a component's markup outside an expression, text or `title`, `aria-label`, `placeholder`, `alt` and `label` attributes: write it in the catalogs instead (units and symbols that read the same in every language go in its `ALLOWED` list).

There is nothing to configure here: the server address is fixed in `vite.config.ts` (development) and the page is served by `mapgen-server` in production.
