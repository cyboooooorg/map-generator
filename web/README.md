# Planet viewer (web front-end)

Browser front-end of the map generator: a library of generated worlds, a 3D globe and a flat map that morph into each other, point inspection, measurements and side-by-side comparison, and a form that queues new generations on the server.

## Prerequisites

- Node 24 or later (`mise install` at the repository root installs the pinned version).
- The `mapgen-server` API, from `crates/server/`.

## Installation

```bash
mise run web:install
```

## Usage

Development, with hot reload: run the server and the Vite dev server together, then open <http://localhost:5173>. Vite forwards `/api`, `/worlds` and `/healthz` to the server on port 3000.

```bash
mise run dev
```

The interface is in English or French: the first visit follows the browser's languages (any `fr-*` gives French, anything else English), and the **EN / FR** selector in the header switches it at once and is remembered. Numbers, units and dates follow it (decimal comma and narrow no-break spaces in French). Biome names, generation stages, the request errors the server explains with a code, and the presets that carry a `[locales.fr]` table are translated too; the files a generation writes and the CLI stay in English. Keyboard shortcuts are the same in every language.

In the viewer:

- **Globe / Map** switches between the globe and the flat map, with an animated morph that keeps the place: the point at the centre of the screen stays there, at an equivalent zoom (the same scale near the ground, the whole map for the whole globe), and the camera follows it while the surface morphs. Zoomed out, the map is kept in view rather than half off screen.
- Drag to rotate the globe or pan the map, scroll to zoom towards the point under the cursor. Zooming goes from the whole planet down to about 300 m per pixel: on the biome layer, detail tiles rendered by the server stream in as you get closer (the coarser picture stays until they arrive, and a tile with nothing loaded above it also fetches its ancestor two levels up, so fast zooming shows something quickly). A tile that fails to load is retried after 2 s, then less and less often (up to once a minute); one the server refuses (a 4xx error) is not retried. On the globe, rotation slows down near the surface.
- Hovering shows the coordinates, biome and elevation under the pointer at the bottom of a wide viewer.
- Click a point to see its coordinates, biome, elevation above the coastline, annual mean temperature in °C (worlds of format version 3 and older stored a `[0, 1]` scale, converted on load with the generator's Earth-fitted curve), moisture, drainage area and, on worlds with `raw_data` format version 2 or later, the distance to the sea, the volcanic zone, the floodplain weight and the shift of the mountain line. Near a river it also shows the river (flow there and at its mouth, length, where it ends, estuary or delta branch); with the hex grid on, the hex under the point. **Copy** copies the latitude and longitude in degrees.
- **Systems** (`docs/system-spec.md`): a planetary system is shown body by body, each under the id `systems/<system>/bodies/<body>` (its files under `/systems/…`, its tiles, epochs, thumbnail and hex grid under `/api/systems/<system>/bodies/<body>/…`; `systemBodyId`, `bodyFiles` and `bodyApi` in `lib/api/client.ts`). The world state loads the system (`GET /api/systems/{id}`, or its `system.json`) and shows the body asked, the one the system was drawn around, or its first planet (`loadSystem`, `showSystemBody` in `lib/world.svelte.ts`). **Body** then lists every body of the system, the star first, each planet followed by its moons, marking those not generated; `U` steps through the family of the planet shown. A body not generated yet is drawn from its preview, a lit sphere of its type's colours (a giant's bands, a rocky body's land and polar caps, lava glowing on a volcanic body's night side), and showing it queues its generation and its neighbourhood's (`POST /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/generate`): a bar under the toolbar names the body being made, its place in the job and its stage, and the maps replace the sphere when it ends. The sky, the light, the turn of the stars, the orbits and the labels come from the system (`lib/systemSky.ts`): every other body where its stored orbit puts it (Kepler's equation from its mean anomaly at the system's epoch, a moon in its planet's equatorial plane), turned by its stored spin (a locked body keeping its longitude 180° towards what it orbits), seen from the body shown; the other planets too, as small disks with their rings; the orbits of the planets round the star and of the moons of the shown body's planet. The clock is the season of the shown body's planet and the orbit time past it; the **Sun longitude** slider sets the hour of the body shown (the sun over that longitude at the season's start), after which it turns on itself at its own day. The star view shows the system (`lib/systemStar.ts`): every planet, the one shown included, as a sphere of its preview's colour on its orbit, listed under **Planets** with its type, mass, orbit and year (the one shown in bold); a generated planet's sphere is wrapped in its map (its thumbnail, at the system's revision), and one not generated yet in its preview, as the planet view draws it (a giant's belts and zones, a rocky body's land and polar caps; `lib/viewer/previewTexture.ts`). Hovering a planet (`StarView.onHoverPlanet`) rings it and opens its card beside it, linked to it by a line (`components/PlanetCard.svelte`, placed by `lib/planetCard.ts`: right of the planet, else left, kept on screen): its name and type, radius and mass, semi-major axis and eccentricity, current speed and year, equilibrium temperature, place relative to the habitable zone (inside, or how far short of or beyond it), moons, and whether it is generated, with **Open** (or **Open and generate**). The card closes when the pointer leaves both the planet and the card, unless it is pinned: a click or a tap on a planet pins its card, as does the card's pin button, and pinned cards stay open side by side, follow their planets as they move and keep their rings. Pins last for the session while the system is shown (`pinnedPlanets` in `lib/world.svelte.ts`): opening a planet and coming back restores them, another system clears them. A double click on a planet, or its card's button, shows it. The planet shown (or the moon's planet) keeps the extras of a world's star view on its own sphere: its orbit line, its moons, and with **Distances** its periapsis and apoapsis, the zone's edges and its live distance, speed and light. Each planet is labelled with its name, on the side of the planet away from its velocity arrow so as not to hide it. **Lines to the star** (on by default) draws a dotted line from the star to each planet, its distance now written along it; off, the name label gives the mean distance instead (`MGS 48213 c · 1.5 AU`). **Velocities** (on by default) draws each planet's velocity: an arrow from the planet along its orbit, the way it goes, as long as its speed on one scale for the whole system (the fastest planet at its periapsis 0.7 view units long, `velocityScale`), labelled with its speed now in km/s. **Star's pull** (on by default) draws the star's pull on each planet, `G M / r²`: an arrow from the planet towards the star along its line, as long as the pull on one scale (the strongest 0.5 view units long, `gravityScale`, a few pixels at least, since the pull falls with the square of the distance), its value written along the line (the Sun's on Earth is 5.9 mm/s²). All follow the animation. With **Distances** and **Habitable zone**, a dashed line crosses the zone between its two edges, with its width and, in brackets, its area (`π (outer² − inner²)`, in AU²). The dashed circle where distances start being compressed is labelled with that distance. **Orbit** hides every planet's orbit line. Labels are kept apart (`lib/viewer/labelLayout.ts`): the names first (the selected planet's, then from the star out), then the distances, the speeds and the pulls; a level label moves up or down by a line or two to a free place, a label along a line stays on it, and either is hidden when no place is left, as on the crowded inner orbits of a fifteen-planet system. They also keep clear of the apsides and zone edges labels. The system has one scale and one clock, whichever planet is shown: distances are linear out to just beyond the habitable zone (`systemCompressedFrom`), then compressed, so showing another planet moves neither the orbits, the zone nor the camera. **Animate** runs every planet at its real pace; **Speed** sets how fast, in Earth days per second, from 1 (a day a second, the slowest) to 4,096 by powers of two (`SYSTEM_SPEED_MIN`, `SYSTEM_SPEED_MAX`); a system opens with its innermost planet going round in about 10 seconds (`defaultSystemSpeed`), and the clock line gives the days per second and the day since the animation started. The viewer generates a body only while it shows it: the star view leaves every planet a sphere.
- **Body** (the list at the top left) picks the body of the system the viewer shows, the star first, then each planet from the star out followed by its moons, nearest first, under its name and its type, those not generated yet marked (see **Systems** above). `U` steps through the family of the planet shown (the planet and its moons), `O` switches to the star and back. Every generated body is shown as a world is, with its detail tiles and crater epochs (`GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/tiles/…` and `…/epoch/…`); a giant as a giant world. The card on the left describes the body shown.
- A **moon**, on a world with moons, is shown in the planet view: each moon is a world of its own (generated with its planet, see the main README), so every layer and tool works on it as on the planet (globe and map, detail tiles, the crater time slider, measuring, screenshots), except the hex grid. An irregular moon is drawn with its shape (`raw_data/shape.png`), its long axis towards its planet, lit by the slopes of that shape. A panel, which folds, gives its origin (giant impact, impact debris, captured), what it is made of, its size (and three diameters when irregular), its mass relative to the planet, density, surface gravity and rotation, then its orbit: distance in km and planet radii, eccentricity, inclination (prograde or retrograde), period, migration (receding, falling in, locked face to face with a binary planet, too far for the tides), apparent size in the planet's sky, the barycentre for a heavy moon, and the planet's day, synchronous orbit and Hill radius; each label explains itself in a tooltip. The world card counts the giant planets and gives their impact rate, counts the moons, gives the tides (relative to Earth's) and whether a large moon steadies the axial tilt, and keeps describing the planet while a moon is shown. In the star view, the moons circle the planet on orbits not to scale (one after the other, the largest drawn larger), at their real pace relative to the year (two seconds a turn at the fastest), retrograde ones backwards; a binary planet swings around its barycentre.
- **Giant planets** (the `gas-giant`, `ice-giant` and `hot-jupiter` types, see the main README) show their cloud tops: the globe flattened by the spin; the biome layer as the bands, storms and polar regions in the giant's own colours; **Cloud tops** (the elevation layer) as their heights, on a ramp of their own from the deep belts in dark rust through the tan cloud deck and the cream zones to the white storm tops, the legend reading "deep belts" to "high storms"; **Temperature** on a range of the giant's own (within 25 K of its effective temperature, or a hot giant's night to day side, the legend saying which); **Wind** (the moisture layer) on a ramp from westward blue through calm white to eastward red, the legend giving the strongest; the other layers are disabled. Rings are drawn in the equatorial plane, with their ringlets and gaps, shadowed by the planet under **Sunlight**, and casting their own shadow on the globe: a band of the winter hemisphere, darkened where the rings are dense, with their gaps letting the light through. **Band drift** (in place of the clouds) turns each band with its own wind, sampled every 2° so that narrow jets keep their direction: neighbouring bands turn east and west in turn, as on Jupiter, the fastest round in two minutes. A hot giant's night side glows red (above about 1 100 K, the more the hotter). Sea and altitude contours are off; there are no detail tiles. The world card gives its mass, radius and density, day and flattening, clouds and effective temperature, jets and strongest winds, internal heat, rings and storms, its poles (a cluster of cyclones, a hexagon or a haze, north and south), instead of the sea level, erosion, atmosphere, geology and land share; the star view draws the world as the giant it is, in its colours, with its rings.
- **Moons of giants** (see the main README): the giant hangs in the sky over the face the world keeps towards it (longitude 180°), with its bands (its own world in `host/`) at its true size (from a few degrees to over 15°), lit by the sun so it shows its phases, and its shadow falls on the world when it passes in front of the sun (eclipses), under **Sunlight**. **Labels** (on by default, under **Sunlight**) names the bodies of the sky and gives their basic facts, each label tied to its body by a thin line, so that a moon a pixel wide can still be found: the moons from their planet (name, radius, orbit), from a moon its planet (name, radius, distance) and the other moons, from a world orbiting a giant the giant (designation, mass, distance) and its other moons, and the star (designation and type, distance); the names are those given on the world card, else the designations; a label flips to the left near the right edge, and a body hidden by the globe or behind the camera has none. **Host planet**, in the display panel when a planet hangs in the sky (viewing a moon, or a world orbiting a giant), has switches of its own for that planet: its **Atmosphere** (halo), **Clouds**, **Grid** and **Tropics**, kept apart from the switches above, which apply to the body you are looking at (atmosphere and clouds on, grid and tropics off by default; remembered like the other display settings). A body locked to its planet turns once per orbit against the stars, its planet fixed in its sky: on a moon, and on a world orbiting a giant, the star field turns with the orbit as the orbit slider or its animation moves it, so the other moons are seen to move against the stars rather than to circle the moon. A captured moon is not locked: it spins once per day of its own, 6 to 24 hours (drawn by its seed, as `world.json` does not store it; the irregular moons of the giants turn in 7 to 12 hours), so from it the planet rises and sets and the stars turn faster, and from its planet it shows each of its sides in turn. The giant turns on itself too, at its day against the world's orbit, its storms with it. The world card gives the giant (kind, mass, distance in its radii and km), the day (the orbit around it) and its size in the sky, and the tidal heating, flagged inside the habitable edge; the star view draws the giant on the world's orbit, in its colours and with its rings, and the world going round it.
- **The star** (in **Body**, or `O`, which switches back too) shows the world's star and its system. The star is a globe you can turn and zoom: its colour from its temperature (white-balanced on the Sun), its limb darker than its centre (more on cool stars), convective granules (none on hot stars, above about 7 500 K, whose outer layers do not convect), spots scattered over the whole surface with bright faculae around them (many on M dwarfs, fewer on K, a few on G and F, none on hotter stars), prominences (arcs of plasma held above the surface by its magnetic field, glowing in the red of hydrogen, each a bundle of twelve thin strands pinned near the surface, spreading and winding along the arch, with knots of gas draining down its legs; seen against the disk, a prominence is a dark filament, its cooler gas absorbing the light of the surface behind: seven on M dwarfs, five on K, four on G, two on F, none on hotter stars, placed by the world's seed) and a corona glow. Around it, in the orbital plane: the habitable zone as a green ring, the planet's orbit (an ellipse with the star at a focus, oriented by the world's seed) and the planet on it, the star not to scale but the distances in proportion. **Distances** draws a line from the star to the planet labelled with their distance, the planet's speed at that point (vis-viva: faster near the star), the light the planet gets there (relative to Earth's) and whether it is inside the habitable zone at that moment, marks the periapsis and apoapsis with their distances (a circular orbit has neither: the panel says so, with its distance) and labels the zone's edges; the labels follow the view. **Orbit** and **Habitable zone** hide or show the orbit line and the green ring (and their labels); **Prominences** hides or shows the arcs. Worlds made before eccentric orbits show a circle. The giant planets of the system (see the main README) are drawn on their orbits, each in its own colour (cream to ochre Jovians, blue ice giants, white water-cloud giants, dark hot Jupiters), larger than the world's marker as they are larger, ringed giants with a tilted ring, labelled by their designation (`MGS 46433 d`), and moving at Kepler's pace with their years in proportion to the world's (a Jupiter beside an Earth turns twelve times slower); beyond the world's orbit and the habitable zone, marked by a dashed circle, distances are compressed (logarithmically) so that giants tens of AU out fit in without shrinking the world's system, which keeps its scale. The view opens framing the whole system, the farthest giant included, and frames it again when another world's system is shown, until you turn or zoom it; **Reset view** frames it again. **Giants** hides or shows them. A panel, which folds, has four sections. *The star*: its spectral type and what it means (a G2V is a yellow dwarf like the Sun), its surface temperature, luminosity, radius and mass (relative to the Sun), its metallicity (`[Fe/H]`, and the share of the Sun's metals), its lifetime on the main sequence and the habitable zone in AU. *Giant planets*: each giant (designation, kind, mass, orbit, year; its clouds, radius, temperature, eccentricity and rings in a tooltip), the eccentricity they force on the world and the impact rate they set, or why there are none. *The planet*: the orbit (semi-major axis, and place relative to the zone), the eccentricity with the closest and farthest distances, the orbital speed (mean, and at periapsis and apoapsis on an eccentric orbit; Earth's is 29.8 km/s), the year and the equilibrium temperature (without greenhouse effect). *Display*: the checkboxes. Each label explains itself in a tooltip. **Animate** (off by default) turns and boils the star, makes red dwarfs flare now and then, sways the prominences and, on M, K and G stars, launches an eruption every nine seconds (an arc that rises, stretches, tears into separate strands and fades), and moves the planet round its orbit, a turn a minute at Kepler's pace (faster near the star), its distances updated live, thirty frames a second at most on the display's clock (a stall, such as the next world loading, pauses the motion rather than making the planet jump). A line under the checkboxes tells how fast time runs (`1 s = 4.5 days (×386,200)` for a 268-day year) and the day of the year since the periapsis; the speeds shown stay real ones, so in a second of animation the planet covers its speed times that factor. Off, the view is drawn only when it moves. Switching worlds keeps the current system on screen until the next one's metadata is in, and restarts the planet from the same point of its orbit. The planet view pauses meanwhile. Worlds made before stars show the Sun and Earth.
- The tools in the top-left corner: **Reset view** (whole planet, north up), **Measure** (click points to measure a path of great-circle legs, in km on this planet), **Screenshot** (the view as a PNG) and **?** (the keyboard shortcuts, then a table of the less common units: Ga and Ma for ages, bar for pressure, g for gravity, AU for distances to the star, L☉ for its luminosity, each with reference values). The same units are explained in a tooltip wherever they appear, on the world card (gravity, atmosphere, geology) and in the generation form (pressure, surface age): values with such a tooltip are underlined with dots.
- **Time slider**, under the tools, on worlds that offer other epochs (no liquid water today, made with impact craters, `raw_data` format version 7): it moves the surface through time, from its oldest surviving surface (4 Ga on `mercury-like`; back to the end of the wet era on worlds that had water, 3.5 Ga on `mars-like`) to 1 Ga ahead, the present in between. Going back, the craters younger than the epoch disappear and the others look fresher; going ahead, craters still to come appear. The label reads "3.8 Ga ago", "present" or "in 250 Ma" (Ma and Ga explained in its tooltip); the stops are 1 Ma apart within 100 Ma of the present, 10 Ma within 1 Ga, 50 Ma beyond. While the slider moves, the base map shows the epoch at a quarter of its resolution (after a pause of 120 ms) and the detail tiles are hidden; once released, the full map, the detail tiles, the relief, the contours and the point information follow the epoch. **Present** goes back to it. The biome statistics of the world card, the hex grid and the comparison stay at the present. The epoch chosen is kept when another world is shown, each world showing its nearest stop (the present on one without epochs). See `GET /api/systems/{id}/bodies/{body}/epoch/...` in the repository README.
- **Display**: open or folded as you last left it on a wide screen; narrow screens (under 900 px) fold it and the biome legend, which would hide the planet, and reopen them when the window grows back:
  - **Layer**: biomes (the generated map), or elevation, temperature (−30 °C to 40 °C), moisture, river flow, volcanic zone, distance to the sea and floodplains coloured on the GPU, with a legend. The last three need a world of format version 2 (3 for floodplains). On the biome layer, the legend lists the biomes of the planet with their share of its surface, in their surface colours (classification colours while the hex grid is shown): hover one to highlight it on the planet, click it to keep it highlighted.
  - **Relief**: raises land above the coastline and shades its slopes; seas stay flat, except on worlds whose seas have dried up (barren), where basins sink below the old coastline and are shaded like land. The sun glints only on liquid seas, as a broad, faint sheen, and barely on magma. The biome layer and the detail tiles are hillshaded already, so on them it only raises the land.
  - **Sunlight**: lights the planet from a sun whose latitude follows the season and the world's axial tilt, with day, night and a terminator on both the globe and the map. Lava and magma glow on the night side (on the biome layer and the detail tiles, not on the hex grid); they are told by their surface colour, red with almost no green. The world's star is drawn where the light comes from; in a system, at its place (its distance and radius, `star` of `systemSky`), so that the camera drawn back still sees it at the centre of the planets' orbits, else at infinity like the star field; it moves with the other stars when the planet is turned: a disk of its apparent diameter seen from the planet (the Sun from Earth: 0.53°; a red dwarf seen from its habitable planet: about 1°), at least a few pixels wide, in the colour of its light and a glow growing with the light the planet gets; the planet hides it, the atmosphere dims it at the limb. Worlds made before stars show the Sun. **Star light** (off by default) lights the planet, its clouds, its sea glint and its sky with the colour of its star's light, as an eye used to the Sun would see them (the black body's colour divided by the Sun's, stored in `world.json`): a Sun-like star changes nothing, a red dwarf turns the day side orange and the sky less blue, an A star turns them bluish; off, the light is white and the planet shows its usual colours. It applies with the headlight too. On the biome map and the detail tiles it also recolours the vegetation for the star, following the idea that photosynthesis adapts to the light (Kiang et al. 2007, speculative): leaves darken towards purple-black under stars cooler than the Sun (fully at 3 300 K), turn golden under hotter ones (fully at 7 300 K), by the share of plants of each biome (forest and jungle 1, taiga 0.9, wetland 0.8, plain, savanna and shrubland 0.6, tundra 0.35; `lib/vegetation.ts`); the exported maps keep Earth's colours. On a tidally locked world the star stays above the centre of the map (the season and sun longitude controls give way to a note). The other bodies of the system are drawn in the sky too, at infinity like the star, at their true apparent size (at least a few pixels, with a faint ring around a body drawn larger than it is, so that a moon a few km across is not taken for a star) and lit by the sun, so they show their phase (a body with air darkens towards its limb and has a twilight band at its terminator; an airless one is lit like dusty regolith, which hardly darkens at the limb; the grid and tropics on the host planet are shaded with it), in front of the star field and of the star (a giant passing in front of the sun hides it): from a moon, its planet, mapped with the planet's surface, under its clouds (with **Clouds**) and in its air (with **Atmosphere**: a halo at the limb in the colour of its sky, brightest when the sun is behind the planet), towards the moon's long axis (longitude 180°, the face it keeps towards its planet: from a Phobos, the planet spans about 40°), turning on itself as **Moon orbits** plays (at its day against its year, from the hour of the planet view; a binary's planet turns with its moon and keeps its face), its spin axis tilted by the moon's inclination, and drawn in perspective, so that from a close moon its parallels bend round it; and the planet's other moons where they are on their orbits, each showing the face it keeps towards the planet or the side it turns to the moon; from the planet, its moons, each mapped with its own surface (its preview until it is generated) and showing the face it keeps towards the planet (a captured moon, not locked, spins instead, see below), full at the start of the season and turning round at their period relative to the year as the seasons play (`lib/systemSky.ts`). The moons go round on their ellipses (Kepler's equation, with the eccentricity of `world.json` and a periapsis drawn by each moon's seed, which `world.json` does not store): nearer, larger and faster at periapsis, and their orbit lines are ellipses too. Where they start is a display choice: the first moon full at the start of the season (but for its eccentricity), each next one 2.4 radians further round its orbit. **Spin axes** (in the display panel, off by default, remembered) draws the axis each body turns on, out of its poles, the north end light and the south end blue: the body shown's through its globe, each body of the sky's out of its disk, so that its tilt shows against its orbit; an arrow bent round its north end shows which way it turns. **Orbits** (in the display panel, when there are moons, or a giant's other moons, in the sky) draws their orbits in the sky as faint lines, where they are round the body shown (its near side in front of the globe, its far side behind it), and places the bodies of the sky from the camera too, so that each stays on its line however the globe is turned: great circles from the planet, loops round the sky or swinging beside the planet from one of its moons, and from a moon its own orbit, through the moon and round its planet (a binary's moon, which hangs still in its planet's sky, has none from the planet). From a giant's moon, the giant's other regular moons hang beside it too (`siblings` under `host` in `world.json`: two to four, sharing about 10⁻⁴ of the giant's mass as the Galilean moons do, on a resonant chain around the world's orbit, each period twice the last), at their size from the world and turning at their period relative to the world's as the seasons play, each mapped with its own surface (its preview until it is generated) and showing the face it keeps towards the giant. Their shadows fall on the ground: a moon passing in front of the sun darkens a spot of its planet (umbra, penumbra, an annular eclipse when its umbra falls short), and the planet darkens its moon in an eclipse. The surface shader takes the four largest shadows: in a system of five or six moons, the smallest moons cast none. **Play** runs through a year in 12 seconds; the latitude under the sun is shown below the slider. With moons in the sky, **Moon orbits** moves them along their orbits, as many days past the season as the slider says (over one orbit of the moon shown, or of the largest moon; in the system of a giant whose moon the world is, of the sibling shown, else of the world round the giant, whichever body is shown), changing their phases and their eclipses; its **Play** runs through that orbit in 10 seconds at ×1, and **Speed** (×1/256 to ×16, by powers of two) slows or speeds it, the line below telling how much time a second of playback covers (`1 s of playback = 6.1 h`). Every body turns on itself meanwhile, at its own pace: the planet at its day (its hour, the sun's longitude over it, goes on from the **Sun longitude** slider, so its night sweeps round and its moons rise and set), a locked moon once per orbit, a captured one at its own day, the giant at its day, its other moons once per orbit; while it plays, the camera turns with the stars, so it stays still against the sky, the star and the orbits, and the globe is seen to turn on itself (the arrow of its spin axis stays still too). With a system of moons, the camera can draw back far enough to see it whole: three times the furthest a moon of the planet gets from the body shown (from a moon, its own distance to the planet plus the furthest moon's; `systemReach` in `lib/systemSky.ts`). It starts over at 0, paused, when another world opens, and a copied link keeps it. On a moon the sun follows its orbit: it goes round the moon once a month, the moon keeping its face to its planet (once a day of its own on a captured moon), and the latitude under it comes from the planet's season and axial tilt and from the moon's inclined orbit, whose node stays fixed among the stars (drawn by the moon's seed, like its periapsis), so an inclined moon sees the sun climb and fall over its equator through the planet's year, so the planet in its sky waxes and wanes (the **Sun longitude** slider still turns the sun round the planet). Off, a headlight follows the camera.
  - **Atmosphere**: the world's sky, from its pressure and its preset's `[atmosphere]` table: single scattering of sunlight (Rayleigh for the gas, Mie for the haze) through a shell around the globe, which draws the halo, the twilight band at the terminator and the veil over distant and grazing ground (an opaque atmosphere such as Venus's hides the surface). The shell is far thicker than a real atmosphere so the sky reads at planet scale, and rises with the **Relief** exaggeration (keeping the same optical depth) so displaced mountains stay under the sky and hide the halo behind them; airless worlds have none, and worlds made before atmospheres get Earth's. The light reaching each point of a view ray is attenuated by the optical depth towards the sun, read from a small table (128 × 32 half floats per world, `sunDepth.ts`) of the Chapman function of an exponential atmosphere over a sphere, instead of being marched: the sky costs about half as much GPU time as with the former march (measured on an Apple M1 Pro at 2520 × 2000 pixels, 6.0 to 2.4 ms per frame for the whole globe, 8.4 to 3.7 ms for a close-up of the limb) and looks the same. **Clouds**: the world's cloud layer (`raw_data/clouds.png`, worlds of format version 5 with clouds), on the globe and the flat map: thin clouds are translucent veils, thick ones hide the ground, tinted by kind (white water, pale yellow sulfuric acid, ochre dust), lit by the sun and casting shadows on the globe; they fade out when zooming close to the ground. The layer is downloaded the first time clouds are shown, so a world viewed with Clouds off never loads it. **Drift** (under Clouds, off by default) turns the cloud layer slowly eastwards, once around the planet in five minutes; while it drifts the viewer redraws ten times a second instead of only when something moves, and stops when the clouds are hidden or faded out. **Stars** (on by default): about 8 500 stars around the globe, fixed in space, so they turn with the planet when the camera moves; their brightness and colour vary (many faint white stars, a few bright ones from blue to orange), and fainter ones crowd along a band tilted 60° to the equator, like the Milky Way. They are points at infinity drawn behind everything (a few kilobytes, one draw call), dimmed by the atmosphere at the limb, and hidden on the flat map. **Grid**: 30° graticule. **Tropics**: the equator, the tropics and the polar circles of the world's axial tilt, dashed in the colours of `world.png`, 3 screen pixels wide with a dark edge at every zoom, drawn over the clouds and the detail tiles. **Depth contours**: the depth contours (isobaths) under water at the levels of `world.png`, in pale blue. **Elevation contours**: contour lines of elevation on land, in topographic brown, every 0.05 elevation units above the coastline, every fifth one stronger (index contours). Contours are drawn about one screen pixel wide at every zoom, detail tiles included, from the elevation data, so the world's data must be loaded. All three are on by default. Worlds of format version 6 and later show `raw_data/surface.png`, which has no lines, so they can be hidden; older worlds show `world.png`, whose tropics and depth contours are baked in.
  - **Hexes**: the hex grid of the world (majority biome per hex, outlines, a blue dot on river hexes), computed by the server for the chosen number of columns and river threshold. Moving a slider or turning the hexes off aborts the request under way. While the server generates a world it answers that it is busy: the panel shows "server busy, retrying…" and asks again every 5 seconds. A request that fails shows a notification, and the globe stays usable. **Download hex JSON** saves the grid shown.
- The display settings (except the hex grid itself) are remembered by the browser. Only the main viewer's are: a compared world opens with them, and changing its display does not change them.

In the sidebar:

- The selected world's card shows every parameter, its gravity, its atmosphere (surface pressure and cloud kind; worlds made before atmospheres show Earth's 1 bar), its geology (tectonic regime and surface age; worlds made before it show Earth's regime and no age), its star (type, temperature, luminosity), orbit (distance, and place relative to the habitable zone, whose edges show in a tooltip), its eccentricity with the closest and farthest distances, the starlight it gets (relative to Earth's, with the equilibrium temperature), its year, the apparent size of its star and whether it is tidally locked (worlds made before stars orbit the Sun at 1 AU), surface, land and water shares, river count and age, and how much of the planet each biome covers. Its actions: **Reroll** queues the same world again with a new random seed (every other parameter kept, the new world opens when ready), **Vary…** opens the form filled with the world's parameters, **Copy command** copies the `mise run gen` command that regenerates it, **Copy link** copies a link that opens it as shown (see the address below), **Compare with** shows a second world beside it, and **Download** offers the map PNG, the SVG, the legend and a ZIP of `raw_data/` built in the browser.
- The library is asked for again when the page comes back to the front (a window focus or the tab shown again, at most once every 5 seconds), so systems made meanwhile with `mise run gen` or in another tab show up. When the server cannot be reached at startup, **Retry** tries again.
- **The library** lists the drawn systems (`GET /api/systems`, see the main README), most recent first: each row gives the star's name (the one given to it, else its designation such as `MGS 48213`), a strip of its planets from the star outwards, one dot each in the colour of its preview (giants larger, those not generated yet faded and outlined), the count per type, the star, seed, moons and age, and a small orrery as its thumbnail (`components/SystemOrrery.svelte`, laid out by `lib/orrery.ts`): the star in its light's colour, the habitable zone as a faint green ring, and each planet as a dot on its orbit, seen a little from above so that the orbits fill the box, on a square-root scale of the semi-major axis, in its preview's colour, giants larger, those not generated yet outlined. It can be searched by any name, designation or seed and sorted (newest, oldest, most planets, seed; remembered). Each row, and the compare bar, shows the system's thumbnail. **Select** picks several systems, to delete them or to compare two of them. Opening one, or creating one, shows its star view, round its body asked for (a system drawn around a planet), else its first generated planet, else its first planet; nothing is generated until a body is picked (see **Body**). The address then names no body (`#system=…`); a link naming one opens that body, and the compare pane opens on a body too.
- **The cards.** At the top of the sidebar, the system's card gives its star (name, designation, type, temperature and luminosity), a strip of its planets from the star outwards, one dot each in its preview's colour (those not generated faded, the planet shown ringed; a click shows it), and its planet and moon counts, ice line and metallicity. Below it, the shown body's card gives its name, designation and type, what it orbits, and what the draw fixed: its orbit (distance, eccentricity, period), size (radius, mass, density, gravity), day (or locked to what it orbits) and tilt, light (relative to Earth's) and equilibrium temperature, and a planet's moons, each a link to it. A body not generated yet says so and offers **Generate its maps** (the viewer also generates it as it opens; the card says when its job runs). A generated body's world card follows, with its maps' details. **Rename**, on either card, names the star or the body shown; an empty name goes back to the designation. Names are kept on the server, by body id (`names.json` of the system), so every browser sees them, and show in the library, the body list, the labels and the cards.
- Deleting a world hides it at once and offers **Undo** for six seconds; the files are deleted afterwards (or when the page closes).
- The address names the system shown, the body shown in it and the system compared (`#system=<id>&body=<id>&compare=<id>`): reloading keeps them, and the link can be shared. The link that **Copy link** copies also carries the view of the main pane, so it opens the same view: `view=g,<lon>,<lat>,<altitude>` on the globe (the point under the camera in degrees, and its altitude in planet radii) or `view=m,<lon>,<lat>,<height>` on the flat map (the point at the centre of the screen, and the camera's height in map units, the map being 2π × π), `layer=<id>` (`biomes`, `elevation`, `temperature`, `moisture`, `flow`, `volcanic`, `coast`, `floodplain`), `relief=<0–1>`, `epoch=<Ma>` (the time slider, when not at the present), `orbit=<days>` (the **Moon orbits** slider, days past the season, when not at 0) and `sun=<season>,<longitude>` (fraction of the year and longitude under the sun in degrees) or `sun=off`. A compared world follows the camera when **Link cameras** is on. Each part is optional, and one that is malformed or out of range is ignored. The view is applied once when the link opens; the address is not updated as the camera moves, and names only the worlds afterwards. For example: `#world=terran-11&view=g,-40,25,0.3&layer=elevation&relief=0.6&sun=0.25,120`.

**New system** opens the form, which asks first what to draw. *Around a planet* is the form below: a planet set as you like it, and a system drawn around it (its other planets and their moons), the planet's maps generated at once. *A whole system* asks for its star (a class and subtype, or drawn), its planets by type (a number per type, 1 to 15 in all, or all drawn), the star's metallicity, the seed and the map size of every body; the system opens at once on its star view, its planets as spheres, each body generated when it is opened. A system that exists already (the same star and seed) is refused by the server, and the form then offers to replace it. The form remembers its mode. In *Around a planet*: (sea level or land share, volcanism, circumference, axial tilt, erosion, surface pressure, surface age, the star and the orbit, the moons). **Moons** is drawn by the seed (the default), none, or 1 to 6 moons: with a number, a random star and orbit are drawn again until the planet has room for them (an error explains why when the star and orbit are fixed). **Star metallicity** (`[Fe/H]`, random by default: drawn like the stars nearby) sets how likely giant planets are; **Giant planets** is drawn by the seed or none (not modelled, the Solar System's impact rate kept); **A giant's moon** is as the seed draws (1 in 50), never or always. Choosing a giant planet type (`gas-giant`, `ice-giant`, `hot-jupiter`) leaves only the axial tilt, the star, the orbit, the metallicity and the **Mass** (Earth masses, on a logarithmic slider from 5 to 4 131, random by default: the preset's range). The star is **Auto** (the planet type's, or one drawn like the stars nearby) or a spectral class and subtype, each class explained below the selectors; the orbit is a position in the star's habitable zone (0 inner edge, 1 outer edge) or a distance in AU, with its eccentricity (random by default). The pressure slider is logarithmic, from 0.001 to 1000 bar, with airless (0 bar) at its far left, so Mars's 0.006 bar is as easy to reach as Venus's 92; the number beside it takes any value. Under the size, the form shows how long the generation should take (from the stage timings learnt from earlier generations in this browser, once there are some), the server's memory peak (interpolated from the figures measured in Docker, see "Web app" in the repository README: 0.16 GiB at 1920 × 1080, 0.55 GiB at 4096 × 2048, 2.0 GiB at 8192 × 4096) and the page's memory for the decoded world (19 bytes per pixel, see below). Each parameter is either random (left to the planet preset, then to the generator defaults) or fixed with its slider; **Roll** draws a seed. The browser remembers the last form you submitted: **New world** starts from it (planet, size and every parameter, the star, the moons, the metallicity, the giant neighbours, whether the world is a giant's moon and a giant's mass, but not the seed, which would name the same world), and **Defaults** goes back to the preset defaults (the moons, metallicity, giants and mass drawn again) and forgets it. A number field left empty is drawn, as if left to random. **Vary…** always starts from the varied world. A world is named after its planet and seed, and the server refuses to overwrite one: when the planet and seed match an existing world, the form says so and offers to replace it. A system replaced (`POST /api/systems?replace=true`) is swapped for the new one as soon as it is drawn; its bodies are generated again as they are opened. **Generate** sends the job and closes the form: generations run in the background, one at a time, the others waiting in a queue. The **Generations** list shows each one with its stage, elapsed time, an estimate of the time left (learnt from earlier generations in this browser) and **Cancel**. A reloaded page picks up the generations still running. When one finishes, the new world opens if you have not picked another one meanwhile; otherwise a notification offers to open it. A world replaced in place reloads in the panes that show it: its map URLs carry the world revision (`?rev=`), and its raw data is revalidated with the server, so the browser never shows files of the replaced world.

**Compare** shows two worlds side by side (stacked on narrow screens). **Link cameras** moves both views together. **Link display** (off by default) makes the compare pane mirror the main one's layer, relief, sunlight (season and sun longitude), atmosphere, clouds and grid as they change, so both worlds are seen the same way; the rest (contours, drift, hexes) stays per pane. **Swap** exchanges them. Both choices are remembered.

On narrow screens (760 px wide or less), the library and the viewer take the whole screen one at a time: **Worlds** and **Viewer** in the header switch between them. Picking a world opens the viewer, and **New world** opens the form, whose buttons stay pinned to the bottom of the screen while the parameters scroll.

While a world loads, a slim bar under **Loading…** (the base map) and **Loading the raw data…** (the layers, fetched and decoded in a Web Worker) names the file being downloaded and shows the megabytes received out of the total of all the files (from their `Content-Length`; the bar slides back and forth while a size is unknown), then each decoding step and the biome statistics. Picking another world meanwhile aborts the downloads and the decoding.

The viewer draws a frame only when something changes (the camera and its damping, the morph between globe and map, a loaded tile, a display setting, a resize), so an idle page costs no GPU time. Frames are drawn at the screen's pixel ratio capped at 2 (antialiased); while the view moves (dragging, damping, zooming, the globe and map morph) they drop to 1.25, and the view is drawn again at full resolution 150 ms after it stops. A hidden viewer (on narrow screens, while the world list shows) draws nothing: the season animation and the cloud drift pause, and resume where they were when it shows again. A decoded world takes about 11 bytes per pixel in the page (19 with the layers of format version 2 and later), about 0.6 GB for 8192 × 4096. Worlds wider than the GPU's texture limit (8192 × 4096 on a GPU limited to 4096 pixels) are shown at a lower resolution; the point readout still reads every pixel.

On the GPU, a pane holds the data layers as RGBA half floats (8 bytes per pixel), the biome ids (1 byte) and the base map (4 bytes, with mipmaps). The layers of format version 2 and later (volcanic zone, distance to the sea, floodplains; another 8 bytes per pixel) are uploaded only the first time one of those layers is shown. The cloud layer takes one byte per pixel (a single-channel texture, with mipmaps), decoded in a Web Worker, once clouds are shown. Decoded images (the base map, the clouds, the detail tiles) are freed as soon as the GPU has them, and so are the copies of layers shrunk to fit the GPU: the page keeps only the decoded world it reads point values from. A pane keeps up to 300 detail tiles on the GPU (about 340 kB each with their mipmaps), the least recently shown going first; while two worlds are compared, each pane keeps 150, so comparing does not double their memory.

For everyday use, run the whole app in Docker instead: see "Web app" in the repository README.

## Translations

Every string the interface shows comes from `src/lib/i18n/en.ts`; components read it through `t` (`t.viewer.layers.elevation`), and messages with values are functions of named parameters (`t.jobs.left({ time })`), so each language orders its words. To add a message, add it to `en.ts` and to every other catalog (the type check lists the ones missing it). To add a language, copy `fr.ts`, translate it, and add its code to `LOCALES` and `LOCALE_NAMES` in `locale.ts` and its catalog to `index.svelte.ts`; built-in presets take a `[locales.<code>]` table. Format numbers with the helpers of `lib/format.ts` (`formatNumber`, `formatUpTo`, `withUnit`…), which follow the locale. Tests run in English whatever the machine's language (`src/test-setup.ts`). A test (`src/lib/i18n/templates.test.ts`) fails on any word written in a component's markup outside an expression, text or `title`, `aria-label`, `placeholder`, `alt` and `label` attributes: write it in the catalogs instead (units and symbols that read the same in every language go in its `ALLOWED` list).
